Teaching audit

Audit of every written lesson (Units 1–2, ten days, 70 lessons), made before the explicit-instruction rewrite. A lesson teaches if it explains the idea, states the fact, rule or method, and gives a fully worked example. Partial means it explains or states a rule but has no fully worked example, or (for languages) presents words without an explicit word list, pronunciation and worked use. Assignment only means it mainly sets problems or a project. A second pass flags mathematics items that follow American standardised-test habits (word form, “expanded form” as a named drill, “value of the digit”, make-the-largest-number, bare rounding and ordering drills) rather than the Singapore method (concrete → pictorial → abstract, number bonds, bar models, place-value discs, mental strategies, heuristic word problems, variation practice).

American standardised style in the mathematics (before the rewrite)

These items were found and are to be replaced with Singapore-style work (place-value discs and charts, number bonds, bar models, mental strategies, variation, word problems). Writing a number “in words” or as a named “expanded form,” and “what is the value of the digit…,” are treated as test habits, not as the way the method teaches place value.

  • Day 1 core practice: write 12,756 in expanded form; value of the digit 9 in 1,391,400; write a number from an English word-form; write 384,400 in words; make the largest and smallest numbers from a set of digits.
  • Day 1 teaching text named “expanded form” and gave a word-form reading of 384,400 as something to learn.
  • Day 1 mental item 8 (“How many hundreds are in 3,800?”) is a useful renaming skill but was set as a bare test question with no teaching of renaming with discs.
  • Day 2 practice asked for rounding the same number to the nearest ten, hundred and thousand, and a bare smallest-to-largest list of diameters, in the style of a skills test rather than a decision in context.
  • Day 5 unit check item 4: write 1,432,000,000 in words.
  • Day 10 unit check Part A: write 2,867,000,000 in words; value of the 6 in 1,632,450; a bare rounding and ordering list.
  • Placement Level 4 item 13: write 3,050,400 in words.

The rest of the mathematics already used concrete → pictorial → abstract, part-whole and comparison bars, and the area model. Those stay. Challenge and circle problems are kept as problem-solving, but each now needs a taught method and a worked smaller case first. Journal writing in the notebook stays only where it helps understanding (labels, stems, a reason), not as “write the number in words.”

After the rewrite (intended standard)

Every lesson follows Learn → Remember → Watch me → Together (hints) → Check (only skills just taught) → independent practice or the day’s problem. Review sheets are one short page per lesson, for retention, matching the taught skill. Combined unit packets are retired. Listen-aloud is in English; only Spanish or Latin vocabulary clips use those languages.

Unit 4 / Level 7 (this chunk)

Level 7 (Days 16–20) follows the same explicit-instruction order and one-page-per-lesson reviews. Measurement is taught with official metric relations (place-value shifts; 1 L = 1,000 cm³) and Singapore-style pictorial models (walk-around perimeter, unit-square area, layers of cubes for volume). Habitat box and tank sizes are invented and labeled. NASA diameter and pressure figures, and BIPM liter relations, are sourced. Circumference and life-support rates are not invented. The Day 20 check is a mastery decision (four out of five or better on each part), not a score, badge, or reward. Notebook pages use because / but / so stems, a word bank, and draw-then-label-then-write. Spanish and Latin stay short present-tense picture lessons; SI units are not put into the Latin sentences.

Unit 5 / Level 8 (this chunk)

Level 8 (Days 21–25) follows the same explicit-instruction order and one-page-per-lesson reviews. Angles are taught as turns (not lengths); polygon angle sums use a pictorial triangle count; circumference uses C = πd with 3.14 and 22/7 labeled as approximations. NASA diameters may now be given a circumference, with π and rounding named. Tycho’s about 85 km diameter is sourced; any circumference or “lines of symmetry” of the crater is called a model. The Day 25 check is a mastery decision (four out of five or better on each part), not a score, badge, or reward. Notebook pages use because / but / so stems. Spanish and Latin stay short present-tense picture lessons; SI units and π are not put into those languages. latus (side) is kept apart from lātus (wide).

Unit 6 / Level 9 (this chunk)

Level 9 (Days 26–30) follows the same explicit-instruction order and one-page-per-lesson reviews. Decimals are taught with Singapore-style pictorial models (tenths strips, a hundred-square, a place-value chart, a number line in tenths). 0.4 and 0.40 are the same amount; a written trailing zero may still mark a finer measurement, which is taught as a caution, not as a course in significant figures. Addition and subtraction line up the decimal point (place value), not the last digits. Negative numbers use a number line through zero. Planet temperatures are sourced: NASA fact-sheet means, and NASA Science ranges, kept labeled; the fact sheet’s own note that a Mercury or Moon mean is not representative of a given region is taught. The Day 30 check is a mastery decision (four out of five or better on each part), not a score, badge, or reward. Notebook pages use because / but / so stems. Spanish and Latin stay short present-tense picture lessons; decimal arithmetic, Celsius figures, and SI units are not put into those languages. punctum is kept neuter (parvum); aqua is kept feminine (frīgida, calida).

Unit 7 / Level 10 (this chunk)

Level 10 (Days 31–35) follows the same explicit-instruction order and one-page-per-lesson reviews. Ratio is taught Singapore-style with equal-part bars, then a : b, then simplifying and equivalent ratios (both sides scaled together). A scale is named as a ratio; Day 5’s 1 cm : 70,000 km is kept as one honest job, and a new 1 cm : 1,000 km Earth–Moon size model is another. The 3.84 m gap is told, not squeezed at a second scale. A star chart is taught as usually angular, not a distance scale. NASA’s “about / roughly 400” wording sits beside about-400 (diameters) and about-390 (already-taught distances); they are not forced to match. Speed is distance per time, with matching units. The ISS figure is about 28,000 km/h from NASA’s Spot the Station FAQ; “five miles per second” is not averaged in. Percent is per hundred, tied to Level 9 hundredths, with a named whole. The Day 35 check is a mastery decision (four out of five or better on each part), not a score, badge, or reward. Notebook pages use because / but / so stems. Spanish and Latin stay short present-tense picture lessons; ratio, scale, km/h, and percent arithmetic stay in the English mathematics. Latin adjectives match (parva / magnum; celer / tardus); centum is indeclinable; Canis Celer from Day 4 is distinguished from today’s adjective celer.

Unit 8 / Level 11 (this chunk)

Level 11 (Days 36–40) follows the same explicit-instruction order and one-page-per-lesson reviews. An unknown is taught Singapore-style with a missing part on a bar, then a letter, then a short equation. A pan balance is the picture for doing the same to both sides; two-step equations still use that habit, then a check. Patterns are rules you can say and use, including a first n → output rule. Planetary data use the NASA Planetary Fact Sheet’s distances and periods; the honest pattern is that a larger distance from the Sun goes with a longer year. No period-from-distance formula and no orbital speed are invented. The Moon’s asterisked figures are kept out of that pattern. The lever is a first model: mass × distance equals mass × distance when it balances, with invented numbers labeled. The Day 40 check is a mastery decision (four out of five or better on each part), not a score, badge, or reward. Notebook pages use because / but / so stems. Spanish and Latin stay short present-tense picture lessons; equations, fact-sheet figures, and lever products stay in the English mathematics. Latin genders are kept honest: pondus neuter (magnum), lībra feminine (aequa), annus and vectis masculine (longus), planēta a borrowed first-declension masculine noun (like nauta).
