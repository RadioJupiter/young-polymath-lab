Level 1 (Days p01–p05) covers numbers to 1,000, addition and subtraction facts, making ten, adding and subtracting tens and hundreds, a planned count of a labeled invented classroom sky, and NASA’s explanation of day and night, with an end-of-level check on Day 5. Level 2 (Days p06–p10) covers place value to 10,000, addition with regrouping (285 + 347), 1,000 − 264, first part-whole and comparison bars, a sourced Saturn V height of 111 meters (363 feet), invented classroom rocket heights labeled invented, and a qualitative look at shadows through the day, with an end-of-level check on Day 10. Level 3 (Days p11–p15) covers multiplication and division facts, equal groups, arrays, 7 × 8, 4 rows of 9, 54 ÷ 6, the two-step bag problem (24 rocks in 3 bags, then 5 bags), four calendar weeks as 28 days, and the NASA synodic month as about 29½ days (from 29.53 days), with an end-of-level check on Day 15. Units 1–7 cover Level 4 (big numbers), Level 5 (multiplication and division), Level 6 (fractions, Moon phases, and gravity on other worlds), Level 7 (metric measurement, perimeter, area, and volume, with a labeled Mars-habitat model), Level 8 (angles, polygons, circumference with a labeled π, symmetry, and a first construction), Level 9 (decimals, negative numbers, and sourced planet temperatures), Level 10 (ratio, scale, rate, and percent, with sourced Earth–Moon sizes and a labeled ISS speed), Level 11 (unknowns and equations, patterns and input-output rules, a sourced planetary-year pattern, and a first lever model), and Level 12 (tallies and tables, bar graphs and pictographs, a line graph of SILSO yearly sunspot means, a first look at fair chance, and measuring with “about” or a range) of the level map. Each day has mental math, a core lesson, challenge problems, a math circle, a short Spanish lesson, a short Latin lesson, and a notebook project (with writing stems and a printable worksheet set).

Math circle topics so far: systematic counting, logic grids, magic squares, parity, the balance puzzle, counting pairs, the pigeonhole principle, divisibility tests, sand-timer puzzles, colouring invariants. Unit 4 adds a comparing argument (is a rounded 6,800 km still honest?), a same-perimeter / different-area observation, a units argument (why a length is not an area), and a last argument about labeling invented models beside sourced facts. Unit 5 adds a reason that vertically opposite angles are equal, a triangle-count reason for (n − 2) × 180°, a scaling reason that C/d is the same for every circle, a first look at why an orbit can be curved (no invented speed), and a last argument about labeling π. Unit 6 adds a grouping reason that each place is ten times smaller than the place on its left, a caution that 0.4 and 0.40 are the same amount but a written measurement can still claim a finer reading, a reason for lining up the decimal point, a reason that a NASA mean may not describe a given place, and a last argument about labeling precision and means. Unit 7 adds a reason that both sides of a ratio change together, a reason that every length in a model uses the same scale, a reason that a speed needs a time and that two rounded NASA figures are not averaged, a reason that a percent needs a named whole, and a last argument about invented mixes, star charts, and sourced wording. Unit 8 adds a reason that the same letter means the same number in one problem, a reason that both sides of an equation change together, a reason that a pattern in a table is not automatically a law, a reason that a longer lever arm can balance a heavier mass, and a last argument about letters, patterns, and labels. Unit 9 adds a reason that tallies group in fives, a reason that a bar graph needs an even scale and a pictograph needs a key, a reason that a rise already on a sourced line is not a law about the next official year, a reason that a short run of heads does not prove a coin is unfair and that “about” is the honest reading of a coarse tool, and a last argument about invented counts, SILSO means, and honest precision.
