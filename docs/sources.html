Unit 3 / Level 6 (additional figures)

  • NASA Moon fact sheet: surface gravity 1.62 m/s² against Earth 9.80 m/s², ratio about 0.165, used in lessons as about 1/6; synodic month 29.53 days, used as about 29½ days; sidereal month 27.3217 days, used as about 27⅓ days. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html
  • NASA Mars fact sheet: mean surface gravity 3.73 m/s² against Earth 9.82 m/s², ratio about 0.380, which is 19/50. 2/5 = 0.4 is taught as a slightly high estimate, and is labeled as an estimate. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html
  • Mass and weight are kept distinct: mass does not change on the Moon; the pull is about 1/6 as strong. First quarter is taught as half the near-side disk lit; “quarter” refers to about a quarter of the synodic cycle, not to a quarter-lit disk.

Unit 4 / Level 7 (additional figures)

  • NASA NSSDCA, Mars Fact Sheet: equatorial radius 3,396.2 km, so equatorial diameter 6,792.4 km, used in lessons as about 6,792 km. “About 6,800 km” is taught only as a labeled rounding to the nearest hundred kilometers. Earth equatorial diameter about 12,756 km (equatorial radius 6,378.1 km). The ratio 6,792 / 12,756 is about 0.532, a little more than half; “exactly half as wide as Earth” is taught as a little low. Mean surface pressure 6.36 mb (at mean radius; it varies by season, 4.0–8.7 mb), compared in lessons with the standard atmosphere at sea level, 1,013.25 mb (101,325 Pa, the conventional standard value), and used as less than 1/100 of Earth's. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html
  • NASA NSSDCA, Earth Fact Sheet: surface pressure listed as 1,014 mb. Lessons use the conventional standard atmosphere 1,013.25 mb (101,325 Pa) for the fraction, and note that 6.36 / 1,014 is also less than 1/100. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html
  • NASA NSSDCA, Earth Fact Sheet (equatorial radius / diameter, as above): https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html
  • BIPM, The International System of Units (SI Brochure), 9th ed.: the meter is the SI unit of length; the liter is accepted for use with the SI, with 1 L = 1 dm³ = 1,000 cm³, and therefore 1 m³ = 1,000 L. This course uses the US spellings meter and liter, and notes the BIPM spellings metre and litre. https://www.bipm.org/en/publications/si-brochure
  • Habitat numbers used in Days 16–20 (a 6 m × 3 m × 2.5 m module, 45 m³; a 240 L tank) are invented classroom design numbers, labeled as invented. They are not NASA specifications and are not used to invent a liters-per-sol or other life-support rate. Circumference is left for Level 8.

Unit 5 / Level 8 (additional figures)

  • π (pi) is the circumference-to-diameter ratio of a circle. It is not a terminating decimal and is not exactly 3.14 or 22/7. Lessons compute with those labeled approximations (3.14, or 22/7 when a diameter is a multiple of 7).
  • NASA NSSDCA, Mars Fact Sheet: equatorial diameter about 6,792 km, as already used in Level 7. Circumference in lessons: 3.14 × 6,792 = 21,326.88 km, written as about 21,300 km, rounded to the nearest hundred, with both π and the rounding labeled. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html
  • NASA NSSDCA, Earth Fact Sheet: equatorial radius 6,378.137 km (lessons have also used about 6,378.1 km, diameter about 12,756 km). Lessons first compute 3.14 × 12,756 = 40,053.84 km (about 40,050 km, or about 40,000 km to the nearest thousand). A closer value, 2π times 6,378.137 km, is about 40,075 km; that is a derived figure from the NASA radius and the mathematical constant π, not a separate classroom measurement. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html
  • NASA Science / Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter pages: Tycho is about 85 kilometers in diameter (also given as about 53 miles). A circumference computed from that figure uses a circle model (3.14 × 85 ≈ 267 km) and is not treated as a surveyed walk around a perfect rim. https://science.nasa.gov/resource/tycho-crater-oblique-view/
  • A first look at why an orbit can be curved follows Newton’s sideways-throw outline only in words: a path that keeps missing a curved ground. Lessons do not invent an orbital speed or treat every orbit as a perfect circle.

Unit 6 / Level 9 (additional figures)

  • NASA NSSDCA, Planetary Fact Sheet (Metric), Mean Temperature (C): Mercury 167, Venus 464, Earth 15, Moon −20, Mars −65. Lessons use these as fact-sheet means, not as the temperature of a given place. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/
  • NASA NSSDCA, Planetary Fact Sheet Notes: a mean is the average temperature over the whole planet’s surface (or, for the gas giants, at the one-bar level). For Mercury and the Moon, “this is an average over the sunlit (very hot) and dark (very cold) hemispheres and so is not representative of any given region on the planet.” Lessons quote that caution and keep means and ranges labeled. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/planetfact_notes.html
  • NASA Science, Mercury facts: sunlit surface temperatures can reach 430°C (800°F); night side can drop to −180°C (−290°F). https://science.nasa.gov/mercury/facts/
  • NASA Science, Venus facts: surface temperatures “about 872 degrees Fahrenheit (467 Celsius).” Lessons keep 464°C as the fact-sheet mean and about 467°C as this Science-page surface figure; they are not averaged into a homemade official number. https://science.nasa.gov/venus/venus-facts/
  • NASA Science, Mars facts: temperature “as high as 70 degrees Fahrenheit (20 degrees Celsius) or as low as about −225 degrees Fahrenheit (−153 degrees Celsius).” https://science.nasa.gov/mars/facts/
  • NASA Science, Moon facts: about 127°C (260°F) in full Sun, and about −173°C (−280°F) in darkness. https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/
  • Classroom lengths and an invented freezer (for example 1.25 m, 0.8 m, −18°C) used in Days 26–30 are invented classroom numbers, labeled as invented. They are not NASA measurements. Lessons do not invent a daily temperature for Mars, or a more precise decimal NASA diameter.

Unit 7 / Level 10 (additional figures)

  • NASA NSSDCA, Planetary Fact Sheet (Metric): diameters Earth 12,756 km, Moon 3,475 km, Mars 6,792 km; Earth–Sun distance 149.6 × 10^6 km (lessons keep the already-taught rounded about 150,000,000 km for light-time and ratio work, and do not replace it with a new official distance). https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/
  • NASA NSSDCA, Moon Fact Sheet (already used in this course): mean Earth–Moon distance 384,400 km; diameter. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html
  • Sun diameter, already taught on Day 5: 1,391,400 km. 1,391,400 ÷ 3,475 ≈ 400.4, about 400 times the Moon’s diameter. 150,000,000 ÷ 384,400 ≈ 390. Lessons do not force the distance ratio to 400.
  • NASA Science, “Eclipses and the Moon”: “The Sun is vastly larger than the Moon ― its diameter is about 400 times the Moon’s. But the Moon is roughly 400 times closer to Earth.” Lessons keep that wording as NASA’s, beside the about-400 and about-390 calculations from already-taught figures, and do not blend them into a homemade official 395. https://science.nasa.gov/moon/eclipses/
  • NASA, “Spot the Station Frequently Asked Questions”: “The International Space Station orbits the Earth once every 90 minutes. It travels at about 17,500 mph (28,000 kilometers per hour).” Lessons compute with about 28,000 km/h, labeled as this FAQ figure. https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/spot-the-station-frequently-asked-questions/
  • NASA, “International Space Station Facts and Figures”: “traveling at a speed of five miles per second, orbiting Earth about every 90 minutes”; “In 24 hours, the space station makes 16 orbits of Earth, traveling through 16 sunrises and sunsets.” Five miles per second is kept as a separate rounded NASA figure (it would be 18,000 mph, coarser than 17,500 mph) and is not averaged with the FAQ speed. 16 orbits in 24 hours agrees with 90 minutes per orbit (1,440 ÷ 90 = 16). https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station/space-station-facts-and-figures/
  • 28,000 km/h × 1.5 h = 42,000 km is taught as the product of two rounded FAQ figures, not as a surveyed NASA orbit-circumference published as its own measurement.
  • Speed of light, already taught: about 300,000 km/s; exact SI value 299,792,458 m/s (BIPM, SI Brochure, 9th ed.).
  • Day 5’s Solar System walk scale, already taught: 1 cm : 70,000 km around a 20 cm Sun. Level 10’s Earth–Moon size model uses a different labeled scale, 1 cm : 1,000 km.
  • Classroom mixes, maps, walks, buses, and collections in Days 31–35 are invented classroom numbers, labeled as invented. Lessons do not invent a NASA rocket-fuel recipe, a cm-to-light-year star-chart scale, a planetary orbital speed, or a NASA composition percent.

Unit 8 / Level 11

  • NASA Planetary Fact Sheet, used for mean distance from the Sun (10^6 km) and orbital period (days): Mercury 57.9, 88.0; Venus 108.2, 224.7; Earth 149.6, 365.2; Mars 228.0, 687.0; Jupiter 778.5, 4331. The Moon’s 0.384 million km and 27.3-day figures are marked with an asterisk on the sheet and are taught as figures about the Moon and Earth, not a year around the Sun. https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/ (page last updated 13 November 2026, as captured for this unit).
  • 687.0 ÷ 365.2 is taught as about 1.88 Earth years for a Mars year, a little less than 2; “exactly 2” is refused. 4331 ÷ 365.2 is about 11.86, spoken as about 12 Earth years, without a long homemade string of figures.
  • Classroom totals, masses, distances, and input-output rules in Days 36–40 are invented classroom numbers, labeled as invented. Lessons do not invent a NASA official balance number, a NASA crane figure, an orbital-speed formula, or a period-from-distance law.

Unit 9 / Level 12

  • Invented classroom meteor watch used in Days 41–42 (and in review items later in the unit): five one-hour watches with counts 4, 8, 2, 10, and 6, total 30. Labeled invented. Not a named meteor shower and not an official rate. No official zenithal hourly rate is invented.
  • Yearly mean total sunspot numbers from WDC-SILSO, Royal Observatory of Belgium, file SN_y_tot_V2.0 (CSV at https://www.sidc.be/SILSO/DATA/SN_y_tot_V2.0.csv; documentation at https://www.sidc.be/SILSO/infosnytot), opened for this unit. Mid-year dates such as 2024.5 are taught as the mean for that year. Figures used: 2008: 4.2; 2019: 3.6; 2020: 8.8; 2021: 29.6; 2022: 83.2; 2023: 125.5; 2024: 154.7; 2025: 123.2. A yearly mean is the arithmetic mean of that year’s daily totals, so one day’s look at the Sun can differ from the yearly mean.
  • A NASA Marshall Solar Physics page on the sunspot cycle (https://solarscience.msfc.nasa.gov/SunspotCycle.shtml, page updated 15 March 2017, as captured) says that monthly averages of the sunspot number “wax and wane with an approximate 11-year cycle,” and that the International Sunspot Number is compiled by the Solar Influences Data Analysis Center in Belgium. That 2017 page is not the source of the yearly numbers in the unit table; those come from the SILSO file. The 11-year spacing between the SILSO 2008 mean (4.2) and 2019 mean (3.6) is taught as one spacing in this official series, not as a promise that every cycle lasts exactly 11 years, and not as a blended “exactly 11” measurement.
  • Classroom coins, dice, spinners, ruler drawings, and extra invented tables in Days 44–45 are labeled invented or are ordinary teaching models. Lessons do not invent an official 2026 yearly mean, an official meteor rate, or a finer length than a centimeter ruler shows.

Level 1 (foundation, Days p01–p05)

  • Place value in this level goes to 1,000 (hundreds, tens, and ones; ten hundreds make 1,000). Addition uses number bonds of 10 and the making-ten strategy (for example 8 + 7 = 8 + 2 + 5 = 15). Subtraction uses the same bond (13 − 6 = 7 because 6 + 7 = 13). Adding and subtracting tens and hundreds changes those places (46 + 30 = 76; 10 less than 300 is 290). These methods follow the Singapore concrete–pictorial–abstract sequence already used in later units. Classroom star and crater counts (for example a 3-by-4 picture of 12 stars) are invented and labeled invented. They are not official counts of the real sky or of the Moon.
  • Day and night follow NASA, “What Is Earth? (Grades 5–8)”: at all times, half of Earth is lighted by the Sun and half is in darkness; areas facing toward the Sun experience daytime, and areas facing away experience nighttime; as the planet spins, most places cycle through day and night once every 24 hours. https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-earth-grades-5-8/ A solar day is taught as about 24 hours. A sidereal day is not begun in this level. No official day length other than that NASA wording is invented. A globe-and-lamp model is an invented classroom demonstration, labeled invented, and is not a measurement of Earth.
  • Spanish and Latin in this level are a first look for students who start here. Captions and phrases are original. Latin is not copied from Ørberg or from Minimus. Instruction remains in English. Students who later begin at Unit 1 (Level 4) still start language at Day 1 of that unit, as the placement page already states.
