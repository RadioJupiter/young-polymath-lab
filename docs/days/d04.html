Unit 1 · Day 4 · How much farther?

Today's big question: Mars is farther from the Sun than Earth, and Jupiter is bigger than Saturn. By how much? Subtraction answers "how much more" questions.

For the guide: the comparison bar model is the key picture today: two bars, one longer, and the question is the difference. Also teach "counting up" (adding on from the smaller number), which is often faster than written subtraction across zeros.

Core lesson: subtraction and the comparison model

45 minutes

1. Concrete. Build 1,000 with base-ten pieces (one thousand-stack). Take away 375. You must first trade the thousand for ten hundreds, then one hundred for ten tens, then one ten for ten ones. Trading down is the same idea as carrying, run backwards.

2. Pictorial: the comparison model. To compare two quantities, draw two bars lined up at the left. The extra piece on the longer bar is the difference.

Earthsame as Mars? (the difference)
Earth's whole bar = 12,756 km
Mars6,792 km

3. Abstract. 12,756 − 6,792. Ones: 6 − 2 = 4. Tens: 5 − 9 does not work, so trade one hundred for ten tens: 15 − 9 = 6. Hundreds: now 6 − 7 does not work, so trade one thousand: 16 − 7 = 9. Thousands: now 1 − 6 does not work, so trade the ten-thousand: 11 − 6 = 5. Answer: 5,964 km.

Check by adding back: 6,792 + 5,964 = 12,756. ✓ Every subtraction can be checked with an addition.

Practice

  1. How much wider is Jupiter (142,984 km) than Saturn (120,536 km)?
  2. Mars is about 228 million km from the Sun and Earth about 150 million km (rounded). How much farther from the Sun is Mars? Why is your answer only approximate?
  3. What must be added to 384,400 to make 1,000,000?
  4. Saturn takes about 10,747 days to go once around the Sun; Jupiter takes 4,331. How many more days does Saturn take? Draw the comparison model.
  5. 3,000 − 1,457
  6. The Moon's distance from Earth changes during each orbit, from about 363,300 km at its closest to about 405,500 km at its farthest. What is the difference?

Challenge problems

25 minutes

  1. I think of a number. I subtract 2,375 and get 6,880. What was my number?
  2. Two numbers add up to 10,000, and one is 1,250 more than the other. Find them. (Draw two bars.)
  3. A rocket's fuel tank holds 9,000 litres. After the first burn, 3,875 litres are left; after the second burn, 1,290 litres are left. How much fuel did the second burn use? How much did both burns use together?

Math circle: odd and even

20 minutes · parity, one of the most powerful ideas in the math circle tradition

  1. Can you find four odd numbers that add up to 25? Try, and then explain why you can or cannot.
  2. Five astronauts meet. Can each one shake hands with exactly three of the others? (Hint: count how many "hands used" there would be in total, and remember that every handshake uses exactly two hands.)

Spanish · El cielo

15 minutes · Method: comprehensible input (Krashen) and Total Physical Response (Asher). Listen and act first; speak when ready. Instructions to the guide are in English.

For the guide: point to the picture, then, if it is dark enough or the Moon is up, look out of a window after the lesson. Review colours: *El sol es amarillo. La luna es blanca.*

The sun, the moon, and a star above a person looking up.

Look and listen.

  • El sol. La luna. Una estrella. El cielo.
  • Veo la luna. Veo el sol. Veo una estrella.
  • El sol es grande y amarillo. La luna es blanca.

Act. Mira el cielo. (look up) Señala el sol. Señala la luna. Señala una estrella.

Exercises

  1. Point: el sol, la luna, una estrella, el cielo.
  2. Say Veo la luna while pointing at the Moon in the picture.
  3. Copy: Veo el sol. El sol es grande.

Latin · Canis Celer

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin reading (in the spirit of Ørberg and of *Minimus*). All text here is original; those books are not copied.

For the guide: act *currit* (run in place) and *dormit* (head on hands). The student may act instead of speaking.

A dog running; a small moon in the sky.

Read, looking at the picture.

  • Celer est canis.
  • Celer est magnus.
  • Celer currit.
  • Celer dormit.
  • Celer est in hortō.

Exercises

  1. Act currit and dormit when you hear them.
  2. Read the five lines.
  3. Complete: Celer est _____. Celer est in _____.

Notebook: the Moon journal begins

60 minutes now, plus two minutes each evening for a month · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html) · [Printable sheets](../printables/d04.html)

How the guide helps today: help set up a week's boxes. For the prediction sentence, use *because*: the student says why the Moon might look bigger, smaller or the same in a week. If tonight is cloudy, draw the last Moon that was actually seen and write "cloudy tonight".

Start a Moon journal: for the next 30 days, whenever the Moon is visible, draw its shape, note the date and time, and sketch where it is in the sky (above which tree, roof or landmark). If clouds hide it, write "cloudy". The Moon is often visible in the daytime too; look for it.

Today, prepare the journal: a page for each week, with a box for each day. Then draw a careful picture of the Moon as it looks tonight (or the last time you saw it).

Never look at the Sun, even when looking for the daytime Moon.

1. Draw today's (or the last seen) Moon, large, with any markings you can really see. Add the week of empty boxes for the journal.

2. Label: date, time, and the shape. Use the word bank.

Word bank: Moon · date · time · sky · cloudy · shape · crescent · half · full · prediction · week

3. Write.

  • Tonight the Moon looks ________.
  • I saw it ________ (where, or "I did not see it").

Then: In one week the Moon will look ________ because ________.

4. You will check the prediction in a week (Day 8 is on that path if you work each school day, but the journal runs every evening, not only on school days).
