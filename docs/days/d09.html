Unit 2 · Day 9 · Messages at the speed of light

Today's big question: When you look at the Moon, you see it as it was more than a second ago. When you look at the Sun's light, it left more than eight minutes ago. How do we know, and what does a swinging weight have to do with time?

For the guide: today joins division with physics. The pendulum experiment needs string, a small weight (a washer, nut or bag of coins), tape and a stopwatch. Timing 10 swings and dividing by 10 is a real scientific technique: it makes reaction-time errors ten times smaller.

Core lesson: light-time, and the pendulum

45 minutes

Light-time. Light travels about 300,000 km every second (the exact value is 299,792,458 metres per second). To find how long light takes to cross a distance, divide the distance by 300,000. A shortcut: divide by 3, then by 100,000.

  • Moon: 384,400 ÷ 300,000 ≈ 1.3 seconds.
  • Sun: about 150,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = 500 seconds = 8 minutes 20 seconds.

So a telescope is a time machine: the farther away something is, the older the light you see from it.

The pendulum (experiment). Galileo noticed that a pendulum's swing takes the same time whether the swing is wide or narrow (as long as it is not too wide). Test two questions: does the weight change the time of a swing? Does the length?

  1. Tie a weight to a string. Measure the length from the pivot to the middle of the weight: start at 25 cm. Tape the top to a table edge.
  2. Pull the weight a little to one side (a small angle) and let go. Time 10 complete swings (away and back is one swing). Divide by 10.
  3. Repeat with lengths 50 cm and 100 cm. Then repeat one length with twice the weight.

Practice

  1. How long does light take to travel 900,000 km?
  2. A radio message travels at the speed of light. When Mars is 60 million km from Earth, how long does a message take to arrive? Give minutes and seconds.
  3. Record your pendulum results in a table: length, time for 10 swings, time for one swing.
  4. When the length was made 4 times longer (25 cm → 100 cm), what happened to the time of one swing?
  5. Did doubling the weight change the time?

Challenge problems

25 minutes

  1. If a 1 m pendulum takes about 2 seconds per swing, how many swings does it make in a minute? In an hour?
  2. A light-year is the distance light travels in a year. Using 300,000 km per second and a 365-day year, work it out step by step: per minute, per hour, per day, per year.
  3. Radio signals to a rover on Mars can take anywhere from about 3 to about 22 minutes, depending on where the planets are. If a driver on Earth sees the rover approach a rock and sends "stop!", what problem is there? How do engineers solve it?

Math circle: the two sand timers

20 minutes

You have a 7-minute sand timer and a 4-minute sand timer. How can you measure exactly 9 minutes? You may turn the timers over whenever you like, and you start them whenever you choose.

Write your method as a timeline (0 min, 4 min, …), and check it step by step. Then: can you measure exactly 1 minute? 2 minutes?

Notebook: graphing the pendulum

60 minutes

Draw a graph of your pendulum results: length (cm) along the bottom, time of one swing (s) up the side. Plot your three points and join them with a smooth curve. Does the curve get steeper or flatter as the pendulum gets longer?

Then predict and test: from your graph, how long should a pendulum be for one swing to take exactly 1.5 seconds? Make it and time 10 swings. Finally, sketch an idea for a clock that uses a pendulum. (Pendulum clocks were invented by Christiaan Huygens in 1656, building on Galileo's discovery, and were the most accurate clocks in the world for nearly 300 years.)
