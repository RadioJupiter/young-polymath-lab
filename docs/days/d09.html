Unit 2 · Day 9 · Messages at the speed of light

Today's big question: When you look at the Moon, you see it as it was more than a second ago. When you look at the Sun's light, it left more than eight minutes ago. How do we know, and what does a swinging weight have to do with time?

For the guide: today joins division with physics. The pendulum experiment needs string, a small weight (a washer, nut or bag of coins), tape and a stopwatch. Timing 10 swings and dividing by 10 is a real scientific technique: it makes reaction-time errors ten times smaller.

Mental math warm-up: time

10 minutes

Learn

Time units: 60 seconds = 1 minute, 60 minutes = 1 hour, so 60 × 60 = 3,600 seconds in an hour. To change seconds to minutes, divide by 60; the remainder is leftover seconds. 500 ÷ 60 is 8 minutes and 20 seconds.

Remember

Minutes = seconds ÷ 60 (remainder in seconds). 1 hour = 3,600 seconds.

Watch me

500 seconds in minutes and seconds.

  1. 60 × 8 = 480, remainder 20.
  2. So 8 minutes 20 seconds.

Together

3 minutes in seconds.

Hint 1: Multiply 3 × 60 = 180.
Hint 2: Answer **180 seconds**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. 120 seconds = how many minutes? 2. Half of 3,600 seconds is how many minutes?

Practice

  1. 120 seconds = how many minutes?
  2. 3 minutes = how many seconds?
  3. 500 seconds = how many minutes and seconds?
  4. 60 × 60
  5. Half of 3,600
  6. 90 minutes = how many hours?
  7. 1,000 seconds = how many minutes and seconds?
  8. 900,000 ÷ 300,000

Core lesson: light-time, and the pendulum

45 minutes

Learn

**Light-time.** Light travels about **300,000 km every second** (the exact value is 299,792,458 metres per second). To find how long light takes to cross a distance, divide the distance by 300,000. A shortcut: divide by 3, then by 100,000. - Moon: 384,400 ÷ 300,000 ≈ **1.3 seconds**. - Sun: about 150,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = **500 seconds = 8 minutes 20 seconds**. So a telescope is a time machine: the farther away something is, the older the light you see from it. **The pendulum (experiment).** Galileo noticed that a pendulum's swing takes the same time whether the swing is wide or narrow (as long as it is not too wide). Test two questions: does the **weight** change the time of a swing? Does the **length**? 1. Tie a weight to a string. Measure the length from the pivot to the middle of the weight: start at 25 cm. Tape the top to a table edge. 2. Pull the weight a little to one side (a small angle) and let go. Time **10 complete swings** (away and back is one swing). Divide by 10. 3. Repeat with lengths 50 cm and 100 cm. Then repeat one length with twice the weight.

Remember

Time = distance ÷ speed. Light travels at about 300,000 km per second. Moon light-time ≈ 384,400 ÷ 300,000 ≈ 1.3 s. Sun light-time ≈ 150,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = 500 s = 8 min 20 s.

Watch me

How long does light take to come from the Sun?

  1. Distance ≈ 150,000,000 km; speed 300,000 km/s.
  2. 150,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = 500 seconds.
  3. 500 = 8 × 60 + 20, so 8 minutes 20 seconds.

Together

How long does light take to come from the Moon (384,400 km)?

Hint 1: Divide 384,400 ÷ 300,000 is a little more than 1.
Hint 2: Answer About **1.3 seconds**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. A radio message travels 600,000 km at 300,000 km/s. How many seconds is that? 2. Change 90 seconds into minutes and seconds.

Practice

  1. How long does light take to travel 900,000 km?
  2. A radio message travels at the speed of light. When Mars is 60 million km from Earth, how long does a message take to arrive? Give minutes and seconds.
  3. Record your pendulum results in a table: length, time for 10 swings, time for one swing.
  4. When the length was made 4 times longer (25 cm → 100 cm), what happened to the time of one swing?
  5. Did doubling the weight change the time?

Challenge problems

25 minutes

Learn

If one swing takes 2 seconds, swings in a minute are 60 ÷ 2. A **light-year** is the distance light travels in one year: multiply 300,000 km/s by the number of seconds in a year, step by step (per minute, per hour, per day, per year). A range of times (3 to 22 minutes to Mars) is a range of distances: multiply each time by the speed.

Remember

distance = speed × time. Build a large product in stages and keep the units.

Watch me

How far does light travel in 2 seconds?

  1. 300,000 × 2 = 600,000 km.

Together

A 1 m pendulum takes about 2 seconds per swing. How many swings in a minute?

Hint 1: A minute is 60 seconds 60 ÷ 2 = 30.
Hint 2: Answer **30 swings**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. How many swings in an hour, at 2 seconds per swing?

Practice

  1. If a 1 m pendulum takes about 2 seconds per swing, how many swings does it make in a minute? In an hour?
  2. A light-year is the distance light travels in a year. Using 300,000 km per second and a 365-day year, work it out step by step: per minute, per hour, per day, per year.
  3. Radio signals to a rover on Mars can take anywhere from about 3 to about 22 minutes, depending on where the planets are. If a driver on Earth sees the rover approach a rock and sends "stop!", what problem is there? How do engineers solve it?

Math circle: the two sand timers

20 minutes

Learn

Two sand timers measure intervals you can add and subtract. The method is to start both together and use the moment one finishes as a new starting mark. The **difference** of 7 and 4 is 3 minutes of leftover sand in the 7-timer; that leftover is a new interval you can use.

Remember

Start together. When the shorter timer ends, the longer still holds the difference. Turning a timer over reuses a known interval.

Watch me

How to measure 3 minutes with a 7-minute and a 4-minute timer.

  1. Start both at once.
  2. When the 4-minute timer ends, 3 minutes of sand remain in the 7-minute timer.
  3. Flip the 7-minute timer (or just watch those 3 minutes run out) to mark 3 minutes from that moment, or use the leftover 3 directly.

Together

Start both timers at 0. When the 4-minute timer ends, how much sand is left in the 7-minute timer? How could that leftover help you make 9?

Hint 1: Leftover 3 minutes of sand remain in the 7-timer.
Hint 2: Use You now have a 3-minute interval. 9 = 3 + 3 + 3, or 4 + 4 + 1 if you later isolate 1 minute. Write a timeline and check every mark.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. If you start a 7-minute and a 4-minute timer together, how much sand is still in the 7-minute timer when the 4-minute timer ends? 2. What interval have you just measured?

Practice

You have a 7-minute sand timer and a 4-minute sand timer. How can you measure exactly 9 minutes? You may turn the timers over whenever you like, and you start them whenever you choose.

Write your method as a timeline (0 min, 4 min, …), and check it step by step. Then: can you measure exactly 1 minute? 2 minutes?

Learn

For the guide: walk to a door or window if you can: *Ana va al jardín. Ana está en el jardín.* Review *tiene, quiere, corre.*

A house and a garden. Ana está en el jardín.

**Look and listen.** - *La casa. El jardín. El cielo.* - *Ana está en el jardín.* *El perro está en la casa.* - *Ana va al jardín. Ana mira la luna.* **Act.** *Camina al jardín.* (walk to a chosen spot) *Para. Mira el cielo.*

*Ana está en el jardín* — Ana is in the garden. *Está* for place; *en* for in.

Watch me

La casa es grande. Ana está en la casa. The first sentence says what the house is like (*es*). The second says where Ana is (*está*).

Together

Exercises

  3. Copy: Ana mira la luna.

Learn

For the guide: these are commands to one person. *Aule!* is how you call Aulus. *Gaia!* stays *Gaia!* Model each command, do it together, then give the command alone, as in the Spanish TPR work.

Notebook: graphing the pendulum

60 minutes · graph, then a short paragraph from an outline · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html) · [Review sheet](../printables/d09-notebook.html)

Learn

For a simple pendulum, a longer string gives a **longer** time for one swing (the period). A graph with length along the bottom and time up the side should rise as you move to the right, usually getting a little flatter. Once three measured points are plotted and joined smoothly, you can **read** a length for a time you did not try (for example 1.5 s) and then test it.

Remember

Longer pendulum → longer period. Read a prediction from the curve, then test it with a new length.

Watch me

Suppose 10 cm took about 0.6 s, 20 cm about 0.9 s, and 40 cm about 1.3 s. A smooth curve through those points would pass near 1.5 s at a length a little over 40 cm. You then cut that length, time ten swings, divide by ten, and see how close you were.

Together

If your curve is still below 1.5 s at 40 cm, should the test pendulum be shorter or longer than 40 cm?

Hint 1: The curve is still rising You need more time, so a longer string.
Hint 2: Answer Longer than 40 cm.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. If you lengthen the pendulum, does one swing take more time or less time?

Practice

How the guide helps today: new writing step: a short paragraph from a tiny outline. First write a topic sentence in full. Then two or three *notes* (not full sentences) and a closing sentence. Draft the paragraph only from that outline. If writing is tiring, the guide may scribe the notes; the student still says the sentences of the draft.

Draw a graph of your pendulum results: length (cm) along the bottom, time of one swing (s) up the side. Plot your three points and join them with a smooth curve. Does the curve get steeper or flatter as the pendulum gets longer?

Then predict and test: from your graph, how long should a pendulum be for one swing to take exactly 1.5 seconds? Make it and time 10 swings. Finally, sketch an idea for a clock that uses a pendulum. (Pendulum clocks were invented by Christiaan Huygens in 1656, building on Galileo's discovery, and were the most accurate clocks in the world for nearly 300 years.)

1. Draw the graph, then a small sketch of a pendulum clock.

2. Label axes (length / time), the three points, and the parts of the clock.

Word bank: pendulum · length · time · seconds · centimetre · graph · curve · clock · Huygens · predict · swing

3. Write. First the outline:

  • Topic sentence: The pendulum's time depends on its length.
  • Notes (not sentences): e.g. longer → slower · 1.5 s ≈ ____ cm · clock uses this
  • Closing sentence: written in full, in the student's words.

Then write a short paragraph (three or four sentences) from the outline.

4. From your graph, how long should a pendulum be for one swing of 1.5 s? Record the test.
