Unit 1 · Day 1 · How far is the Moon?

Today's big question: The Moon looks close enough to touch. How far away is it really, and how do we even write a number that big?

For the guide: work through the blocks in order. Mental math warm-up

10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it

Learn

When two numbers have zeros in the same places, add or subtract the digits that are not zero, then put the zeros back. 300 + 500 is 3 hundreds + 5 hundreds = 8 hundreds = **800**. That is a number bond of 800. To add 10 when there is a string of 9s, the 9s flip to 0 and the next place goes up: 99 + 10 = 109.

Remember

Add or subtract the same places. A bond of 10 (or 100, or 1,000) makes the work easy.

Watch me

Watch 38,400 + 1,000.

10 00010 00010 0001 0001 0001 0001 0001 0001 0001 0001 000

38,400 already has 8 thousands. One more thousand-disc makes 9 thousands. The other discs stay. The sum is 39,400.

Together

70,000 − 20,000. Say how you would do it with discs or a bond, then give the answer.

Hint 1: Which place? Both numbers are made of ten-thousand discs only.
Hint 2: Bond 7 ten-thousands minus 2 ten-thousands leaves 5 ten-thousands.
Hint 3: Answer **50,000**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. 400 + 600 (think in hundreds). 2. What is 10 more than 2,995?

Practice

  1. 300 + 500
  2. 4,000 + 6,000
  3. 38,400 + 1,000
  4. 384,400 + 100,000
  5. 70,000 − 20,000
  6. What is 10 more than 9,995?
  7. What is 1,000 less than 100,000?
  8. How many hundreds are in 3,800?

Core lesson: place value to a million

45 minutes · concrete → pictorial → abstract

Learn

**The fact.** The Moon is, on average, about **384,400 kilometres** from Earth. Earth itself is about **12,756 km** across. These are real measurements, rounded. **1. Concrete — place-value discs.** Use discs (or draw them) labelled 1, 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000, 100,000. Ten discs of one kind are traded for one disc of the next kind. This is a **number bond** of ten:
10
10 ones1 ten
Build **1,384**: one 1,000-disc, three 100-discs, eight 10-discs, four 1-discs. **2. Pictorial — the place-value chart.** Put the digits of the Moon's distance in a chart: | 100,000 | 10,000 | 1,000 | 100 | 10 | 1 | |---|---|---|---|---|---| | 3 | 8 | 4 | 4 | 0 | 0 | Each column is worth **ten times** the column to its right. A number bond can split a number into two parts, for example 384,400 = 300,000 + 84,400.
384,400
300,00084,400
**3. Abstract.** 384,400 is 3 hundred-thousands, 8 ten-thousands, 4 thousands and 4 hundreds. Moving every digit one column left multiplies by 10. Ten hundred-thousands make **one million**: 1,000,000. The Sun is about **1,391,400 km** across.

Remember

Ten of one disc make one of the next disc. Each place is worth ten times the place to its right. ×10 moves every digit one place left. A number bond splits a number into two parts that add to the whole.

Watch me

Show 1,384 with discs, then multiply by 10.

1 00010010010010101010101010101111

One 1,000, three 100s, eight 10s, four 1s. Move every disc one place left: one 10,000, three 1,000s, eight 100s, four 10s = 13,840.

Together

The Moon's distance is 384,400 km. Write a number bond with 300,000 as one part. Then find 10 × 384,400 by shifting the chart.

Hint 1: Bond 384,400 = 300,000 + 84,400.
Hint 2: ×10 Each digit moves one place left: 3,844,000 km.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. Show 3,475 (the Moon's width in km) on a chart. What is 10 × 3,475? 2. Write a number bond: 384,400 = 80,000 + ?

Practice

These problems use only today's skill: discs or a chart, number bonds, and ×10 as a shift of place.

  1. Show 12,756 on a place-value chart (or with discs). Then show 10 × 12,756 on a new chart. What happened to each digit?
  2. The Moon is 384,400 km away. A probe is 10 times as far. How far is the probe?
  3. The Sun is about 1,391,400 km across. Write a number bond: 1,391,400 = 1,000,000 + ?. Then find 10 × 1,391,400.
  4. 38,440 × 10 and 38,440 × 100. What changes on the chart each time?
  5. A length is made of 4 hundred-thousand discs, 8 ten-thousand discs, 4 thousand discs and 4 hundred discs. What length is that?

Challenge problems

25 minutes · try every one; being stuck for a while is part of the work

Learn

A useful heuristic: **try a smaller case**, then use the pattern. To count how many whole numbers have exactly *n* digits, look at the first of them and the last of them, and subtract, remembering to add 1 (the fence-post count).

Remember

How many whole numbers from *A* to *B* inclusive? *B* − *A* + 1. Two-digit numbers run from 10 to 99, so there are 99 − 10 + 1 = 90.

Watch me

How many whole numbers have exactly two digits?

  1. The first two-digit number is 10. The last is 99.
  2. 99 − 10 + 1 = 90.
  3. Or: the first digit can be 1–9 (9 choices) and the second 0–9 (10 choices): 9 × 10 = 90.

Together

How many whole numbers have exactly three digits? Use the same method.

Hint 1: First and last 100 to 999.
Hint 2: Count 999 − 100 + 1 = **900**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. How many whole numbers have exactly four digits?

Practice

  1. How many whole numbers have exactly six digits?
  2. I am a six-digit number whose digits add up to 2. List every number I could be.
  3. A number line runs from 0 to 400,000 and is 40 cm long. Where is 384,400 on it?

Math circle: counting without missing any

20 minutes · the math circle tradition: one rich problem, solved and then explained

Learn

The heuristic today is **make a systematic list** so you cannot miss a case or write a repeat. Fix the first choice, then list every way to finish; then change the first choice and do it again.

Remember

If you use each of *n* different digits once, the number of arrangements is *n* × (*n*−1) × … × 1. A careful list is how you see why.

Watch me

Using the digits 1 and 2 once each, how many two-digit numbers can you make?

  1. First digit 1: the remaining digit is 2, so 12.
  2. First digit 2: the remaining digit is 1, so 21.
  3. Two numbers. That is 2 × 1 = 2.

Together

Start the list for digits 1, 2 and 3 with first digit 1. How many numbers begin with 1?

Hint 1: Remaining digits 2 and 3 can be arranged as 23 and 32.
Hint 2: So Two numbers begin with 1: 123 and 132. The same will happen when 2 or 3 is first.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. For 1, 2 and 3, how many three-digit numbers are there in all, if you use each digit once?

Practice

Using each of the digits 1, 2 and 3 exactly once, how many different three-digit numbers can you make?

Don't guess: make a list in order, so that you can be sure you have every one and no repeats. Then predict: with the four digits 1, 2, 3, 4, how many four-digit numbers are there? Check your prediction by a smart method, not by listing them all.

Spanish · Hola, and acting out words

15 minutes · Method: comprehensible input (Krashen) and Total Physical Response (Asher). Listen and act first; speak when ready. Instructions to the guide are in English.

Learn

We learn Spanish by hearing short, clear phrases and acting them out. Study the words first, then watch the picture and the actions. Explanations are in English; only the Spanish words themselves are spoken in Spanish.

Learn

For the guide: sit facing the student. Say each word clearly, point or act, and wait. Do not translate every word first. Check understanding by asking the student to point or move. If the student stays quiet, that is fine today.

Two people wave.

**Look and listen.** Point to the picture. Say, slowly, more than once: - *Hola.* - *Hola. Me llamo …* (say your name). *¿Cómo te llamas?* - *Sí.* *No.* **Act (TPR).** Model each command, do it together, then give the command alone. Mix the order so it is not a memorised dance. - *Levántate.* (stand up) - *Siéntate.* (sit down) - *Mira.* (look — point to your eyes, then to an object) - *Señala la puerta.* / *Señala la ventana.* / *Señala el lápiz.* (point to the door / window / pencil) - *Camina.* *Para.* (walk; stop) **Then the student may try** giving you one command. Accept any attempt.

Remember

*Hola* is hello; *adiós* is goodbye. A command such as *mira* or *levántate* is something you can do at once. The stressed syllable is the one written in capital letters in 'How to say it'.

Watch me

The guide says hola and waves. That means hello. Then the guide says levántate and stands up. The word names the action. The student stands up too.

Together

The guide says siéntate. What should you do, and what does the word mean?

Hint 1: Meaning *Siéntate* means sit down.
Hint 2: Action You sit down.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What does *gracias* mean? 2. If the guide says *mira*, what do you do?

Exercises

  1. When the guide says Siéntate, Levántate, Mira, Para, Señala el lápiz, do the action (no talking required).
  2. Point to the picture and say Hola.
  3. Answer, with a name: ¿Cómo te llamas?

Latin · Salvē: puer and puella

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin reading (in the spirit of Ørberg and of *Minimus*). All text here is original; those books are not copied.

Learn

We read Latin from a picture: first look, then hear the caption, then say it. Study the words first. Explanations are in English; only the Latin words are spoken as short clips (an Italian voice is used as an approximation).
WordHow to say itMeaning
salvēSAL-wayhello (to one person)
puerPOO-erboy
puellapoo-EL-lahgirl
estestis
AulusOW-loossa boy's name
GaiaGUY-aha girl's name

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Pronounce every letter. *c* is always like *k*; *v* is like English *w*; *ae* is like *eye*. Long marks (macron) mean a longer vowel: *ē* as in *they*.

Learn

For the guide: read each Latin caption aloud, pointing at the picture. Then read it together. A short English meaning may be given after the student has looked and heard, not before. Pronounce every letter; *c* is always like *k*; *v* is like English *w*; vowels are pure (*a* as in *father*, *e* as in *they*, *i* as in *machine*, *o* as in *note*, *u* as in *rude*).

Aulus (a boy) and Gaia (a girl). These names are ordinary Roman names, made up for this course.

**Read, looking at the picture.** - *Salvē!* - *Aulus est puer.* - *Gaia est puella.* - *Aulus est puer. Gaia est puella.* *Salvē* is a greeting to one person. *Est* means "is". *Puer* is a boy; *puella* is a girl.

Remember

*Puer est* means 'he is a boy'. *Puella est* means 'she is a girl'. *Salvē* is hello to one person. *Aulus est puer.* *Gaia est puella.*

Watch me

The picture shows a boy. The caption is Aulus est puer. Aulus is his name, est means is, puer means boy. So the sentence means 'Aulus is a boy.'

Together

The picture shows a girl called Gaia. Say the Latin sentence, then give the English meaning.

Hint 1: Words *Gaia*, *est*, *puella*.
Hint 2: Sentence *Gaia est puella.* — Gaia is a girl.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What does *puer* mean? 2. What does *Salvē* mean?

Exercises

  1. Point to Aulus when you hear puer; to Gaia when you hear puella.
  2. Read the four lines aloud.
  3. Say Salvē to the guide, and hear Salvē back.

Notebook: the Earth–Moon model

60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html) · [Review sheet](../printables/d01-notebook.html)

Learn

Today's scale is **1 cm stands for 1,000 km**. To change a real length in kilometres into a model length in centimetres, divide by 1,000. Earth is 12,756 km across, so the model is 12,756 ÷ 1,000 = 12.756 cm, which we take as about **12.8 cm**. The same rule gives the Moon and the distance between them.

Remember

model cm = real km ÷ 1,000. Keep the same scale for size and for distance, or the model will lie.

Watch me

The Moon is 3,475 km across. At 1 cm for 1,000 km:

  1. 3,475 ÷ 1,000 = 3.475 cm.
  2. About 3.5 cm (close to a ping-pong ball).

Together

The Moon is 384,400 km from Earth. What is that in centimetres, and in metres, at today's scale?

Hint 1: Divide 384,400 ÷ 1,000 = 384.4 cm, about 384 cm.
Hint 2: In metres 100 cm = 1 m, so 384 cm is almost **4 m**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. At this scale, how wide is a 5,000 km moon, in centimetres? 2. A model distance is 10 cm. How many kilometres is that in space?

Practice

How the guide helps today: stay for the writing steps. The student draws first, with no writing. Then labels from the word bank. Then says each stem sentence aloud; you confirm it is a complete sentence before it is written. If handwriting is a struggle, write the student's own spoken sentence in light pencil for tracing or copying. Do not invent sentences for the student.

Build it. Use the scale 1 cm stands for 1,000 km. Then:

  • Earth (12,756 km across) is a ball about 12.8 cm across: about the size of a large grapefruit.
  • The Moon (3,475 km across) is about 3.5 cm across: close to a ping-pong ball (4 cm).
  • The distance between them (384,400 km) is about 384 cm: almost 4 metres.

Find two objects close to these sizes. Hold the "Earth" and ask someone to walk the "Moon" away until it is 3.84 m away. Most people guess the Moon is much closer.

1. Draw. Sketch the two objects with the gap between them (you can show a broken or compressed gap, but write the real model distance).

2. Label, from the word bank. Point and say each word, then write it on the sketch.

Word bank: Earth · Moon · kilometre (km) · centimetre (cm) · metre (m) · model · scale · distance · diameter

3. Write. Say each sentence aloud, then write it.

  • The Earth in the model is about ________.
  • The Moon in the model is about ________.
  • The gap between them is about ________.

Then finish this stem in your own words: What surprised me was ________.

4. Numbers. Write the calculation for each model size (Earth, Moon, distance). Write one question about the Moon that you would like to be able to answer.
