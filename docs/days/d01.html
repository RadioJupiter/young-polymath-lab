Unit 1 · Day 1 · How far is the Moon?

Today's big question: The Moon looks close enough to touch. How far away is it really, and how do we even write a number that big?

For the guide: work through the blocks in order. In the core lesson, do not skip the concrete step even if the student "already knows" it; seeing why each place is worth ten times the next is the foundation for everything later. Core lesson: place value to a million

45 minutes · concrete → pictorial → abstract

The fact. The Moon is, on average, about 384,400 kilometres from Earth. Earth itself is about 12,756 km across. These are real measurements, rounded.

1. Concrete (with your hands). Use base-ten blocks if you have them. If not, make them: a small paper square is 1, a strip of ten squares is 10, a sheet of ten strips is 100, and a stack of ten sheets is 1,000. Build the number 1,384 out of the pieces. Then answer: how many of the 1-squares would it take to build a 1,000-stack? Each new piece is made of ten of the piece before it.

2. Pictorial (draw it). Draw a place value chart:

hundred thousands ten thousands thousands hundreds tens ones
3 8 4 4 0 0

Every column is worth ten times the column to its right. Moving one column left multiplies by 10.

3. Abstract (numbers only). 384,400 = 300,000 + 80,000 + 4,000 + 400. This is the expanded form. In words: three hundred eighty-four thousand, four hundred. The comma separates the thousands, so big numbers can be read in groups of three.

Beyond a hundred thousand. Ten hundred-thousands make one million: 1,000,000. The Sun is about 1,391,400 km across: one million, three hundred ninety-one thousand, four hundred.

Practice

  1. Write 12,756 in expanded form.
  2. What is the value of the digit 9 in 1,391,400?
  3. Mars is about two hundred twenty-eight million kilometres from the Sun. Write that number in digits.
  4. Write 384,400 in words.
  5. Using the digits 3, 8, 4, 4, 0, 0 (each once), make the largest and the smallest six-digit numbers you can. (A number cannot start with 0.)
  6. What is 10 × 1,391? What is 10 × 38,440?

Math circle: counting without missing any

20 minutes · the math circle tradition: one rich problem, solved and then explained

Using each of the digits 1, 2 and 3 exactly once, how many different three-digit numbers can you make?

Don't guess: make a list in order, so that you can be sure you have every one and no repeats. Then predict: with the four digits 1, 2, 3, 4, how many four-digit numbers are there? Check your prediction by a smart method, not by listing them all.

Spanish · Hola, and acting out words

15 minutes · Method: comprehensible input (Krashen) and Total Physical Response (Asher). Listen and act first; speak when ready. Instructions to the guide are in English.

For the guide: sit facing the student. Say each word clearly, point or act, and wait. Do not translate every word first. Check understanding by asking the student to point or move. If the student stays quiet, that is fine today.

Two people wave.

Look and listen. Point to the picture. Say, slowly, more than once:

  • Hola.
  • Hola. Me llamo … (say your name). ¿Cómo te llamas?
  • Sí. No.

Act (TPR). Model each command, do it together, then give the command alone. Mix the order so it is not a memorised dance.

  • Levántate. (stand up)
  • Siéntate. (sit down)
  • Mira. (look — point to your eyes, then to an object)
  • Señala la puerta. / Señala la ventana. / Señala el lápiz. (point to the door / window / pencil)
  • Camina. Para. (walk; stop)

Then the student may try giving you one command. Accept any attempt.

Exercises

  1. When the guide says Siéntate, Levántate, Mira, Para, Señala el lápiz, do the action (no talking required).
  2. Point to the picture and say Hola.
  3. Answer, with a name: ¿Cómo te llamas?

Latin · Salvē: puer and puella

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin reading (in the spirit of Ørberg and of *Minimus*). All text here is original; those books are not copied.

For the guide: read each Latin caption aloud, pointing at the picture. Then read it together. A short English meaning may be given after the student has looked and heard, not before. Pronounce every letter; *c* is always like *k*; *v* is like English *w*; vowels are pure, as in Spanish (*a e i o u*).

Aulus (a boy) and Gaia (a girl). These names are ordinary Roman names, made up for this course.

Read, looking at the picture.

  • Salvē!
  • Aulus est puer.
  • Gaia est puella.
  • Aulus est puer. Gaia est puella.

Salvē is a greeting to one person. Est means "is". Puer is a boy; puella is a girl.

Exercises

  1. Point to Aulus when you hear puer; to Gaia when you hear puella.
  2. Read the four lines aloud.
  3. Say Salvē to the guide, and hear Salvē back.

Notebook: the Earth–Moon model

60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html) · [Printable sheets](../printables/d01.html)

How the guide helps today: stay for the writing steps. The student draws first, with no writing. Then labels from the word bank. Then says each stem sentence aloud; you confirm it is a complete sentence before it is written. If handwriting is a struggle, write the student's own spoken sentence in light pencil for tracing or copying. Do not invent sentences for the student.

Build it. Use the scale 1 cm stands for 1,000 km. Then:

  • Earth (12,756 km across) is a ball about 12.8 cm across: about the size of a large grapefruit.
  • The Moon (3,475 km across) is about 3.5 cm across: close to a ping-pong ball (4 cm).
  • The distance between them (384,400 km) is about 384 cm: almost 4 metres.

Find two objects close to these sizes. Hold the "Earth" and ask someone to walk the "Moon" away until it is 3.84 m away. Most people guess the Moon is much closer.

1. Draw. Sketch the two objects with the gap between them (you can show a broken or compressed gap, but write the real model distance).

2. Label, from the word bank. Point and say each word, then write it on the sketch.

Word bank: Earth · Moon · kilometre (km) · centimetre (cm) · metre (m) · model · scale · distance · diameter

3. Write. Say each sentence aloud, then write it.

  • The Earth in the model is about ________.
  • The Moon in the model is about ________.
  • The gap between them is about ________.

Then finish this stem in your own words: What surprised me was ________.

4. Numbers. Write the calculation for each model size (Earth, Moon, distance). Write one question about the Moon that you would like to be able to answer.
