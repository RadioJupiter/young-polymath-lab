Unit 3 · Day 13 · Adding the same-size parts

Today's big question: If two fractions already use the same size parts, how do you add or subtract them — and what must you not add?

For the guide: the common error is adding denominators (1/6 + 2/6 = 3/12). Use the cubes or the bar first. The 12-day month is only a model. Answers go in the math notebook; check them with the key only after the work is done.

Mental math warm-up: adding equal parts of the same whole

10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it

Learn

When fractions have the same denominator, you are adding (or subtracting) parts of the same size. 1/6 + 2/6 is 3/6, because one sixth-part plus two sixth-parts is three sixth-parts. You add the numerators and keep the denominator. Then you can rename if you wish: 3/6 = 1/2.

Remember

Same denominator: add or subtract the numerators; the denominator stays. Then rename if there is a simpler equivalent fraction.

Watch me

What is 1/8 + 3/8? Then give a simpler name if there is one.

  1. The parts are eighths, so 1 + 3 = 4 eighths: 4/8.
  2. 4/8 = 1/2 (divide top and bottom by 4).
  3. The sum is 4/8, or 1/2.

Together

What is 5/6 − 2/6?

Hint 1: Same size parts

The parts are sixths. 5 − 2 = 3.

Hint 2: The result

3/6, which is also 1/2.

Check

Use only like denominators, then rename if you can.

  1. 1/4 + 2/4 = ?
  2. 5/8 − 1/8 = ?

Practice

  1. 2/6 + 1/6, then a simpler name.
  2. 3/8 + 3/8, then a simpler name.
  3. 7/10 − 3/10.
  4. 4/4 − 1/4 (what is left of one whole?).
  5. 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5.

Core lesson: adding and subtracting like fractions

35–40 minutes · Method: a bar of equal parts, then the written rule, then gravity and phase examples

Learn

Like fractions have the same denominator: the parts are the same size. To add or subtract, you count how many of those parts you have.

1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6, and 2/6 = 1/3. Two “Moon-pulls” on the 6-part Earth bar would be 1/3 of Earth’s pull — that is just addition of like parts, not a new fact about two moons.

A phase-bar example. Imagine a classroom model month of 12 equal days (this is a model, not the real 29½-day synodic month). 3 days are 3/12; 3/12 + 3/12 = 6/12 = 1/2 of the model month. When you want the real month, go back to 29½ and take a fraction of that length (Day 11), rather than pretending the month has 12 days.

The rule. For fractions with the same denominator: a/d + b/d = (a + b)/d, and a/d − b/d = (a − b)/d (when a ≥ b, for now). Then rename if you can.

Concrete step. On a 6-cube train, add 1 cube and 2 cubes: 3/6 of the train, which is 1/2. Do not add the denominators (1/6 + 2/6 is not 3/12).

Remember

Like fractions (same denominator): add or subtract the numerators; keep the denominator. Then rename: 2/6 = 1/3, 3/6 = 1/2. Do not add denominators. A 12-day month is a classroom model, not the real 29½-day synodic month.

Watch me

On the 6-part gravity bar, what is 1/6 + 2/6? Give the unsimplified sum and a simpler name.

  1. The parts are sixths, so 1 + 2 = 3 sixths: 3/6.
  2. 3/6 = 1/2.
  3. That is half of Earth’s pull, not a new kind of gravity.

Together

A student writes 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/12. What went wrong, and what is the correct sum?

Hint 1: The mistake

The denominators were added. The parts are still sixths, not twelfths.

Hint 2: The fix

1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6, which is 1/3.

Check

These questions use only like denominators, then renaming.

  1. 3/8 + 1/8 = ? Give a simpler name if there is one.
  2. On a 12-day model month, 5/12 + 3/12 = ? Is that half the model month?

Practice

  1. 2/5 + 1/5 and 4/5 − 1/5.
  2. 1/6 + 1/6. Name the sum in sixths and in thirds.
  3. A 12-day model month: 4/12 + 2/12, then a simpler name. Then write one sentence that this is a model, not 29½ days.
  4. Which is longer on the same bar: 5/8 − 1/8, or 3/8 + 1/8? Or are they equal?
  5. Write a because-sentence: I do not add the denominators when I add 1/6 + 2/6 because ________.

Challenge problems: several like parts, and a leftover

20 minutes · like fractions, including a leftover of a leftover later only if like parts allow it

Learn

You can add more than two like fractions: 1/8 + 2/8 + 3/8 = 6/8 = 3/4. You can also ask what is left: 1 − 3/8 = 8/8 − 3/8 = 5/8. Today, stay with like parts. (Unlike denominators are tomorrow.)

Remember

Add or subtract as many like parts as you need. A whole is d/d. Rename when you can.

Watch me

A tank is 1 whole. 3/10 is used, then another 2/10. How much is used, and how much is left?

  1. 3/10 + 2/10 = 5/10 = 1/2 used.
  2. Left: 1 − 5/10 = 10/10 − 5/10 = 5/10 = 1/2.
  3. Used and left are each half.

Together

On a 6-part gravity bar, you mark 1/6 (Moon) and then want 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6. What pull, as a fraction of Earth’s, is that?

Hint 1: Add

3/6.

Hint 2: Rename

3/6 = 1/2 of Earth’s pull. That is a math result on the bar, not a claim that some world has half of Earth’s gravity.

Check

Like parts only.

  1. 1 − 1/6 − 1/6 = ?
  2. 5/12 + 5/12 = ? Is that less than, equal to, or more than 1?

Practice

  1. A 12-day model month: 3/12 + 3/12 + 3/12. What fraction of the model has passed, and how many model days are left?
  2. 7/8 − 2/8 − 1/8.
  3. Write a story (two sentences) about a 6-part bar that uses 1/6 + 2/6, and give the sum.
  4. Why is 1 − 1/6 not 0, even though ‘one minus one’ sounds like zero if you ignore the denominator?

Math circle: when a sum is more than one whole

20 minutes · a diagram, then a short argument

Learn

If you add like fractions and the numerator becomes larger than the denominator, the sum is greater than one whole. 5/6 + 2/6 = 7/6, which is 1 and 1/6 (an improper fraction and a mixed number). Both names are valid. A bar that is 6 parts long cannot hold 7 parts; you need a second bar for the extra sixth.

Remember

When the numerator is larger than the denominator, the amount is more than one whole: a/d = (a ÷ d) wholes, with a remainder of parts. 7/6 = 1 1/6.

Watch me

A student says 5/6 + 2/6 is impossible because ‘you cannot have 7 parts if the bar has only 6.’ How would you answer?

  1. The first whole is 6/6, and 1 sixth is left over on a second whole.
  2. 7/6 is a correct sum; it is 1 1/6.
  3. The bar was only a picture of one whole; the amount can go past it.

Together

Write 11/6 as a mixed number, and 2 1/6 as an improper fraction.

Hint 1: 11/6

6/6 is one whole, and 11 = 6 + 5, so 1 5/6 remains after the whole.

Hint 2: 2 1/6

2 wholes are 12/6, plus 1/6 is 13/6.

Check

The skill is going past one whole with like parts.

  1. 5/4 + 1/4 = ? Give an improper fraction and a mixed number.
  2. Which is larger, 9/8 or 1? How do you know?

Practice

  1. Write 9/6 and 10/6 as mixed numbers, then rename 9/6 further if you can.
  2. 3/5 + 4/5.
  3. Draw two 8-part bars and show 13/8.
  4. A two-sentence argument: 7/6 is more than 1 because ________. I can also write it as ________.

Spanish · La luna llena, nueva, and cuarto

15 minutes · Instruction in English; Hear for Spanish words

Learn

Phase words for the Moon, to go with Day 11’s careful pictures. la luna llena (full Moon), la luna nueva (new Moon), el cuarto creciente (first quarter), el cuarto menguante (third / last quarter). Creciente is waxing; menguante is waning. Veo la luna llena. El lunes veo el cuarto creciente. Remember: first quarter still looks half lit; the Spanish name, like the English, tracks the cycle.

Word How to say it Meaning
la luna llena lah LOO-nah YEH-nah the full Moon
la luna nueva lah LOO-nah NWEH-bah the new Moon
el cuarto creciente el KWAR-toh kreh-see-EN-teh first quarter (waxing)
el cuarto menguante el KWAR-toh men-GWAHN-teh third quarter (waning)
veo la luna BEH-oh lah LOO-nah I see the Moon

Remember

la luna llena, la luna nueva, el cuarto creciente, el cuarto menguante. Veo la luna llena. The name cuarto, like ‘quarter’, is about the cycle, not a quarter-lit disk.

Watch me

Say in Spanish: ‘I see the full Moon.’

  1. Veo la luna llena.
  2. luna llena, not luna nueva.
  3. Veo, from earlier days.

Together

Say: ‘On Monday I see the first quarter.’

Hint 1: On Monday

El lunes (Day 11).

Hint 2: First quarter

veo el cuarto creciente.

Check

Use only today’s phase words and known day-words.

  1. Write: I see the new Moon.
  2. Which phrase is first quarter? Which is full?

Exercises

  1. Translate: On Friday I see the third quarter. Today I see the full Moon. (Hoy from Day 11.)
  2. Write two original sentences, each with a different phase word.
  3. In one English sentence, say what cuarto is tracking, so you do not shade only a quarter of a disk.

Latin · Ubi est lūna?

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin; original sentences. Instruction in English.

Learn

Ubi? means ‘where?’. Ubi est lūna? Lūna est in caelō. Ubi sunt stellae? Stellae sunt in caelō. You already know est / sunt and in caelō. Ubi est puer? Puer est in hortō (Day 3’s garden word).

Word How to say it Meaning
ubi? OO-bee where?
Ubi est lūna? OO-bee est LOO-nah Where is the moon?
in hortō in HOR-toh in the garden
in caelō in KAI-loh in the sky

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Remember

Ubi est…? / Ubi sunt…? Answer with est / sunt and a place phrase: in caelō, in hortō.

Watch me

Ask and answer in Latin: ‘Where is the moon?’ — ‘The moon is in the sky.’

  1. Ubi est lūna?
  2. Lūna est in caelō.
  3. est, not sunt, with lūna.

Together

Ask and answer: ‘Where are the stars?’

Hint 1: The question

Ubi sunt stellae?

Hint 2: The answer

Stellae sunt in caelō.

Check

Use ubi, est/sunt, and a place you have been taught.

  1. Write: Where is the boy? The boy is in the garden.
  2. Correct: Ubi sunt lūna?

Exercises

  1. Translate: Where is the night? (A thoughtful answer can be Nox est, or you may write Ubi est nox? and leave a place if you have one; Nox est is enough if no place is taught.) Better: Ubi est puella? Puella est in hortō. Write that pair.
  2. Write two original question-and-answer pairs, one singular and one plural.
  3. Correct: Ubi est stellae?

Notebook: adding parts on a bar, with because / but / so

60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · How the notebook works · Review sheet

Learn

Draw a 6-part bar and show 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2, then write stems that explain the rule (keep the denominator) and one caution (do not add denominators). You may also draw a 12-day model-month bar, clearly labeled as a model.

Remember

Same-size parts: add the numerators, keep the denominator, then rename. Write because / but / so. Label a 12-day month as a model, not as 29½ days.

Watch me

Finish: I keep the 6 when I add 1/6 + 2/6 because ________.

  1. Because the parts are still sixths — the same size parts of the same whole.
  2. The 6 tells the size of each part, not a number to add to the other 6.
  3. A good because names same-size parts.

Together

Finish: 2/6 + 1/6 is 3/6, so ________, but ________.

Hint 1: So

so the sum is also 1/2 (or 3/6 of the bar).

Hint 2: But

but I must not write 3/12 by adding the denominators.

Check

The notebook work is the bar, the sum, and the stems.

  1. What two names should appear under 3/6?
  2. What must a 12-part ‘month’ bar be labeled?

Practice

How the guide helps today: stay for the writing steps. Draw first, then label from the word bank, then say each stem aloud before it is written. Do not invent sentences for the student.

1. Draw. A 6-part bar showing 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2. Optional: a 12-part model-month bar with a sum of like twelfths, labeled model.

2. Label, from the word bank.

Word bank: like fractions · numerator · denominator · 1/6 · 2/6 · 3/6 · 1/2 · bar · model month · rename

3. Write.

  • 1/6 + 2/6 = ________, which is also ________.
  • I keep the denominator because ________.
  • I do not add the denominators, so I do not write ________.
  • A 12-day month is a model, but the real synodic month is about ________.
