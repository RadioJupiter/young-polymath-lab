Unit 1 · Day 2 · Ordering the planets

Today's big question: The planets are at wildly different distances from the Sun. How do we compare huge numbers quickly, and when is a rounded number good enough?

For the guide: rounding is taught with a number line, not a rule to memorise. If the student can say *why* 12,756 rounds to 13,000 (it is closer to 13,000 than to 12,000), the rule follows.

Mental math warm-up

10 minutes

Learn

To compare two numbers, line them up by place and **look from the left** (the largest place first). The first place that differs tells you which is larger. A number with more digits is larger (among whole numbers). Halfway between two numbers is their average, or the lower number plus half the gap.

Remember

Compare from the left. Halfway from *A* to *B* is *A* + (*B*−*A*) ÷ 2.

Watch me

Which is larger, 108,200 or 108,020?

  1. Both have 6 digits, and both start 108.
  2. The thousands place: 2 versus 0. 2 is greater, so 108,200 is larger.

Together

What number is halfway between 12,000 and 13,000?

Hint 1: Gap The gap is 1,000. Half of that is 500.
Hint 2: Answer 12,000 + 500 = **12,500**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. Which is larger, 4,515 or 4,551? How do you know? 2. What number is halfway between 300,000 and 400,000?

Practice

Which is larger? Say how you know without working anything out on paper. 1. 108,200 or 108,020 2. 4,515 or 4,551 3. 99,999 or 100,000 4. 778,500 or 77,850 5. What number is halfway between 12,000 and 13,000? 6. What number is halfway between 300,000 and 400,000?

Core lesson: comparing and rounding big numbers

45 minutes

Learn

**The facts.** Average distance of each planet from the Sun, rounded to the nearest million kilometres (from NASA's planetary fact sheet): | Planet | Distance from the Sun (million km) | |---|---| | Mercury | 58 | | Venus | 108 | | Earth | 150 | | Mars | 228 | | Jupiter | 779 | | Saturn | 1,432 | | Uranus | 2,867 | | Neptune | 4,515 | **Comparing.** A number with more digits is larger. With the same number of digits, compare from the left: the first place where the digits differ decides. **Rounding (concrete → pictorial).** Draw a number line from 12,000 to 13,000 and mark the halfway point, 12,500. Mark Earth's diameter, 12,756. It lies past the halfway point, so it is **closer to 13,000**: 12,756 rounded to the nearest thousand is 13,000. A number exactly at the halfway point is rounded up by convention. **Abstract.** To round to a place, look at the digit just to its right: 5 or more rounds up, 4 or less rounds down. (This is the number line, written as a rule.) **When to round.** NASA's fact sheet lists these distances to only three or four digits, and each planet's distance from the Sun changes as it moves along its oval orbit, so extra digits would not mean much. A rounded number is not "wrong"; it is honest about how precisely we know or need the value.

Remember

Compare from the left. To round to a place, look at the digit immediately to its right: 0–4 stay, 5–9 go up. Round only when a table, a model, or an estimate needs fewer places — not as a drill for its own sake.

Watch me

Earth is 12,756 km across. A table will only show whole thousands of kilometres.

  1. The thousands digit is 2. Look at the hundreds: 7.
  2. 7 is 5 or more, so the 2 goes up to 3 and the rest become 0.
  3. Write 13,000 km. The real diameter has not changed; the table only has room for thousands.

Together

The Moon is 384,400 km away. A scale only needs the nearest 100,000 km. What do you write?

Hint 1: Look at the 8 The hundred-thousands digit is 3; the next digit (ten-thousands) is 8, which is 5 or more.
Hint 2: Answer **400,000 km**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. Which is larger, 778,500 or 77,850? Compare from the left. 2. A table needs Mercury's diameter 4,879 km to the nearest thousand. What do you write?

Practice

  1. Use place value, comparing from the left. Which is wider, Venus (12,104 km) or Earth (12,756 km)? Mercury (4,879 km) or Mars (6,792 km)? Then list the four from narrowest to widest.
  2. A table of planet diameters will only show whole thousands of kilometres. What do you write for Earth's diameter (12,756 km)? Why that thousand, and not 12,000?
  3. A scale model only needs the Moon's distance (384,400 km) to the nearest 100,000 km. What number do you use? Why is that not a mistake?
  4. Which is closer to 50,000 km: Neptune's diameter (49,528 km) or Uranus's (51,118 km)? Show how far each is from 50,000.
  5. How many planets are closer than 200 million km to the Sun? How many are farther than 1,000 million?

Challenge problems

25 minutes

Learn

A number that rounds to a given value sits in a **range**. To the nearest ten thousand, numbers from 375,000 up to (but not including) 385,000 all become 380,000. To find every possibility, draw the number line around the rounding point and mark the halfway marks.

Remember

To the nearest thousand, the numbers that become *N*000 run from *N*000 − 500 up to (but not including) *N*000 + 500.

Watch me

What whole numbers round to 13,000 (nearest thousand)?

  1. Halfway below 13,000 is 12,500. Halfway above is 13,500.
  2. 12,500 rounds up to 13,000. 13,500 would already go to 14,000.
  3. So the numbers are 12,500, 12,501, …, 13,499. Smallest 12,500; largest 13,499.

Together

A number rounds to 380,000 to the nearest ten thousand. What is the smallest it could be?

Hint 1: Halfway below 375,000 is halfway between 370,000 and 380,000.
Hint 2: Answer **375,000**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What is the largest whole number that rounds to 380,000 to the nearest ten thousand?

Practice

  1. A secret number rounds to 380,000 (to the nearest ten thousand) and to 400,000 (to the nearest hundred thousand). What could it be? Is the Moon's distance a possibility?
  2. What is the smallest whole number that rounds to 13,000 (nearest thousand)? The largest?
  3. About how many times farther from the Sun is Jupiter than Earth? Use rounded numbers and explain.

Math circle: the planet puzzle

20 minutes · logic: use every clue, and prove your answer is the only one

Learn

These are **logic-grid** puzzles. Write the places (or days) in a row. Use each clue once. Cross out what cannot be true. A good heuristic is: start with the clue that pins a thing to one exact place.

Remember

Start with the firm placement. Then apply 'next to', 'not at the end', and 'to the left of' one at a time. Check that every clue is still true at the end.

Watch me

Three seats in a row. Moon is at the right end. Sun is not next to Moon. Where is each of Sun, Earth, Moon?

  1. Right end is Moon. Seats are: ?, ?, Moon.
  2. Sun is not next to Moon, so Sun cannot be in the middle. Sun is at the left. Earth is in the middle.
  3. Sun, Earth, Moon.

Together

In today's first puzzle, Jupiter is at the right end, and Venus is next to Jupiter. Where must Venus sit?

Hint 1: Next to the right end Only one seat is next to the right end: the third seat.
Hint 2: Answer Venus is third, just left of Jupiter.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. Earth is not at either end. The ends are already Jupiter (right) and one other. Which two seats are left for Earth and Mars?

Practice

Puzzle 1. Four model planets (Mars, Venus, Earth, Jupiter) sit on a shelf in a row of four places. Jupiter is at the right end. Earth is not at either end. Venus is next to Jupiter. Mars is somewhere to the left of Venus. Where is each?

Puzzle 2. Five space probes, A, B, C, D and E, were launched on five different weekdays, Monday to Friday. C was launched on Wednesday. B was launched the day after A. E was launched before A. D was launched before E. On which day was each launched?

Spanish · People and size

15 minutes · Method: comprehensible input (Krashen) and Total Physical Response (Asher). Listen and act first; speak when ready. Instructions to the guide are in English.

Learn

These words name people and size. *Grande* is large; *pequeño* is small. In Spanish, *el niño* is a boy and *la niña* is a girl; *el hombre* is a man and *la mujer* is a woman.
WordHow to say itMeaning

la mujerlah moo-HAIRthe woman
el niñoel NEE-nyohthe boy
la niñalah NEE-nyahthe girl
grandeGRAHN-dehlarge
pequeñopeh-KEH-nyohsmall

Learn

For the guide: review yesterday's commands first (one minute). Then say the new sentences while pointing at the picture. Use your hands for *grande* (wide arms) and *pequeño* (fingers close together).

A taller child and a smaller child.

**Look and listen.** - *Es un niño.* *Es una niña.* - *Es grande.* *Es pequeño.* / *Es pequeña.* - *Es un niño grande.* *Es una niña pequeña.* Review: *Hola. ¿Cómo te llamas? Me llamo …* **Act.** *Levántate. Siéntate. Señala un niño.* (point to the picture) *Señala una niña. Camina. Para.*

Remember

*El* goes with many words for male people; *la* with many words for female people. *Grande* and *pequeño* tell size.

Watch me

El hombre es grande means 'The man is large.' Es means is. The pattern is: person + es + size.

Together

Say in Spanish: 'The girl is small.'

Hint 1: Words *la niña*, *es*, *pequeña* (the ending becomes *pequeña* to match *niña*).
Hint 2: Sentence *La niña es pequeña.*

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What does *el niño* mean? 2. What does *grande* mean?

Exercises

  1. Point: el niño · la niña · grande · pequeña.
  2. Say, looking at the left figure: Es un niño. Es grande.
  3. The guide says Es pequeña. Which figure is meant?

Latin · Familia

Learn

For the guide: point to each figure as you read. *Fīlius* and *fīlia* have a long *i*: *fee-li-us*, *fee-li-a*. *Māter* has a long *a*.

A family: pater, māter, Aulus (fīlius), Gaia (fīlia), and a dog, Celer.

**Read, looking at the picture.** - *Aulus est fīlius.* - *Gaia est fīlia.* - *Pater est in familiā.* *Māter est in familiā.* - *Celer est canis.* *Fīlius* is a son; *fīlia* is a daughter; *pater* a father; *māter* a mother; *canis* a dog. *In familiā* means "in the family" (the ending changes after *in*; you do not need the case name yet).

Remember

*Aulus est fīlius.* *Gaia est fīlia.* *Pater* and *māter* are father and mother. These are family words, not names of anyone you know.

Watch me

The picture shows a father. The caption pater est means 'he is a father.' Adding a name: Aulus est fīlius — Aulus is a son.

Together

Say in Latin: 'Gaia is a daughter.'

Hint 1: Words *Gaia*, *est*, *fīlia*.
Hint 2: Sentence *Gaia est fīlia.*

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What does *māter* mean? 2. What does *fīlius* mean?

Exercises

  1. Point to each as it is named: pater, māter, fīlius, fīlia, canis.
  2. Read the four captions aloud.
  3. Complete: Aulus est _____. Celer est _____.

Notebook: the Solar System on a strip

60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html) · [Review sheet](../printables/d02-notebook.html)

Learn

Today's scale is **1 cm for every 20 million km**. A distance given in millions of kilometres becomes centimetres when you divide by 20. A short way: divide the millions by 10, then halve the result. Earth is 150 million km from the Sun: 150 ÷ 10 = 15, half of 15 is **7.5 cm**.

Remember

model cm = (distance in million km) ÷ 20. Or: divide the millions by 10, then halve.

Watch me

Mars is 228 million km from the Sun.

  1. 228 ÷ 10 = 22.8.
  2. Half of 22.8 is 11.4. The mark for Mars is 11.4 cm from the Sun on the strip.

Together

Jupiter is 779 million km from the Sun. How far from the Sun on the strip?

Hint 1: Divide by 10 779 ÷ 10 = 77.9.
Hint 2: Halve About **39 cm** (77.9 ÷ 2 = 38.95).

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. Venus is 108 million km from the Sun. How many centimetres on this strip?

Practice

How the guide helps today: help with the long paper strip and the divisions if needed. For writing, the student speaks each stem before writing. Keep the word bank in view. One careful noticing sentence is enough; do not push for a paragraph.

Make a scale strip of planet distances from the Sun using 1 cm for every 20 million km. To find each length: divide the distance in millions by 10, then halve it. (Example: Earth, 150 → 15 → 7.5 cm.)

Use a long strip of paper, or tape sheets together. You need well over 2 metres. Mark the Sun at one end, then each planet. Label each mark with the planet's name and real distance.

1. Draw. In the notebook, sketch the strip in miniature (it will be cramped; that is part of what you will notice).

2. Label the Sun and each planet from the word bank.

Word bank: Sun · Mercury · Venus · Earth · Mars · Jupiter · Saturn · Uranus · Neptune · million · kilometre · inner planets · gap · scale

3. Write. Say each sentence aloud, then write it.

  • The four inner planets are ________.
  • The biggest gaps are ________.

Then finish: I notice that ________.

4. Numbers. Write the eight model lengths you marked on the strip.
