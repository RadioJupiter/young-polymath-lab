Unit 1 · Day 3 · Adding up the worlds

Today's big question: If you lined up planets side by side, how wide would the row be? Adding big numbers accurately is the tool.

For the guide: regrouping ("carrying") should be shown with the base-ten pieces first: ten ones are traded for one ten, ten tens for one hundred. The written method is a record of those trades. Introduce the bar model as a drawing of the problem's *structure*, before any calculation.

Mental math warm-up

10 minutes · look for numbers that make a ten, a hundred or a thousand

Learn

Two mental addition strategies: **make ten** (or make 100, or make 1,000) by splitting one addend; and **compensation** (add a little too much, then take it back). 47 + 53: 47 + 3 = 50, then 50 + 50 = 100. 198 + 57: 200 + 57 = 257, then take back 2, to get 255.

Remember

Make a ten (or a hundred, or a thousand) by splitting. Or add a nice number and compensate.

Watch me

198 + 57, by compensation.

  1. 198 is 2 short of 200. Add 200 + 57 = 257.
  2. You added 2 too many, so take 2 off: 255.

Together

25 + 36 + 75. Which two make a hundred, and what is left?

Hint 1: Bond 25 + 75 = 100.
Hint 2: Then 100 + 36 = **136**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. 47 + 53, by making 100. 2. 2,999 + 1.

Practice

  1. 47 + 53
  2. 380 + 620
  3. 1,250 + 750
  4. 2,999 + 1
  5. 6,500 + 3,500
  6. 198 + 57
  7. 4,999 + 2,345
  8. 25 + 36 + 75

Core lesson: adding big numbers and the part-whole model

45 minutes

Learn

**1. Concrete.** With base-ten pieces, build 1,276 and 475 and push them together. Whenever you have ten of a piece, trade them for one of the next bigger piece. What do you end up with? **2. Pictorial: the part-whole bar model.** When parts are put together to make a whole, draw one bar split into the parts, and a bracket for the whole:
diametersEarth 12,756 kmMoon 3,475
whole = ?
**3. Abstract: column addition.** Line up the places. Add the ones first; if a column makes ten or more, write the ones digit and carry one to the next column. 
   12,756
 +  3,475
 --------
   16,231
Ones: 6 + 5 = 11, write 1, carry 1. Tens: 5 + 7 + 1 = 13, write 3, carry 1. Hundreds: 7 + 4 + 1 = 12, write 2, carry 1. Thousands: 2 + 3 + 1 = 6. Ten thousands: 1. **16,231 km**: Earth and the Moon side by side. **Check with an estimate:** 13,000 + 3,000 = 16,000, so 16,231 is reasonable.

Remember

Part-whole: the parts add to the whole. Line up the places. Add ones first. Ten in a column is traded for 1 in the next column (the same trade you make with discs).

Watch me

Add 12,756 + 3,475 (Earth's diameter plus the Moon's), as on the page above, watching the trades.

  1. Ones: 6 + 5 = 11, write 1, carry 1 ten.
  2. Tens: 5 + 7 + 1 = 13, write 3, carry 1 hundred.
  3. Hundreds: 7 + 4 + 1 = 12, write 2, carry 1 thousand.
  4. Thousands: 2 + 3 + 1 = 6; ten-thousands: 1. The whole is 16,231 km.

Together

Add Mars and Mercury: 6,792 + 4,879. Set them in columns and trade when a column makes ten or more.

Hint 1: Ones and tens 2 + 9 = 11, write 1 carry 1. 9 + 7 + 1 = 17, write 7 carry 1.
Hint 2: Finish 7 + 8 + 1 = 16, write 6 carry 1. 6 + 4 + 1 = 11. The sum is **11,671 km**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What is 1,276 + 475? Trade tens of a place for one of the next. 2. In a part-whole bar, Earth 12,756 km and Moon 3,475 km are the parts. What is the whole?

Practice

  1. Mercury (4,879 km across) and Mars (6,792 km) side by side: how wide? Is that wider or narrower than Earth (12,756 km)?
  2. A spacecraft flies to the Moon and back: 384,400 km each way. How far is the round trip?
  3. A probe travelled 125,600 km on its first day and 98,750 km on its second. Draw the bar model and find the total.
  4. Venus (12,104 km), Earth (12,756 km) and Mars (6,792 km) side by side: how wide?
  5. Estimate first, then calculate: 47,385 + 28,946.

Challenge problems

25 minutes

Learn

Two heuristics today. For a number and its reverse: write AB as 10*A* + *B*, and BA as 10*B* + *A*, and add them (algebra in disguise). To add 1 to 100, **pair** the first with the last: 1 + 100, 2 + 99, and so on. Each pair is the same; count the pairs.

Remember

AB + BA = 11(*A* + *B*). The sum 1 + 2 + … + *n* is *n* pairs of (*n*+1), then divide by 2, or *n*(*n*+1)/2.

Watch me

Add every whole number from 1 to 10, without going one at a time.

  1. Pair 1 with 10, 2 with 9, 3 with 8, 4 with 7, 5 with 6. Five pairs, each 11.
  2. 5 × 11 = 55.

Together

A two-digit number AB plus its reverse BA equals 121. What is *A* + *B*?

Hint 1: The sum AB + BA = 11(*A* + *B*) = 121.
Hint 2: Divide *A* + *B* = 11. Now find digit pairs that add to 11 (you will list them in the problem).

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. Using the pairing method, what is 1 + 2 + … + 20?

Practice

  1. A two-digit number AB plus its reverse BA equals 121. Find every possibility. (AB means the digits A and B, not A × B.)
  2. Add up every whole number from 1 to 100, without adding them one at a time.
  3. Mercury, Venus and Mars have diameters 4,879 km, 12,104 km and 6,792 km. Add them in the order that is easiest for you, and explain why you chose it.

Math circle: the magic square

20 minutes

Learn

A **magic square** of 1 to 9 has every row, column and both diagonals adding to the same total. First find that total: the numbers 1 to 9 add to 45, and the three rows use every number once, so each row is 45 ÷ 3. The centre cell sits on four lines (middle row, middle column, both diagonals), so it must be the middle number.

Remember

For 1 to 9, the line total is **15**. The centre is **5**.

Watch me

1 + 2 + … + 9 = 45. Three rows share that 45, so each row is 15. The pairs that add to 15 with a 5 in the centre are 1 + 9, 2 + 8, 3 + 7, 4 + 6 — those four pairs sit on the four lines through the centre.

Together

If 5 is in the centre, can 9 go in a corner or must it go on a side? Try putting 9 opposite 1 through the centre, and see which lines still work.

Hint 1: Through the centre 9 and 1 already make 10, plus 5 is 15, so 9 and 1 sit on a line through 5.
Hint 2: Corners The two diagonals also go through 5, so the even numbers 2, 4, 6, 8 actually take the sides, and the odd numbers 1, 3, 7, 9 take the corners — you will confirm this as you build it.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What must each row of the 1-to-9 square add to, and which number sits in the centre?

Practice

Place the numbers 1 to 9 in a 3 × 3 grid so that every row, every column and both diagonals add to the same total.

Before you start guessing, think: what do all nine numbers add up to? There are three rows, each with the same total, and together the rows use every number once. So what must each row add to? Then: which number must go in the centre? (Hint: the middle row, middle column and both diagonals all pass through the centre.)

Spanish · Colours

Learn

For the guide: use real objects or coloured pencils on the table as well as the picture. Say *Es rojo* while holding the red one. Review *Hola, grande, pequeño* once.

Four shapes, each with a colour word. Colour them, or point to real objects of those colours.

**Look and listen.** - *Es rojo. Es azul. Es amarillo. Es verde.* - *Blanco.* *Negro.* - *El sol es amarillo. La noche es negra.* (point to a yellow circle and to a dark area) **Act.** *Señala el rojo. Señala el azul. Toca el verde. Mira el amarillo.* Then mix in *Levántate, Siéntate, Para.*

Remember

*El cielo es azul* — the sky is blue. *El sol es amarillo* — the sun is yellow. *Es* + colour.

Watch me

Pointing at a blue shape, the guide says es azul — it is blue. Pointing at the sun in the picture: El sol es amarillo.

Together

Say in Spanish: 'The sky is blue.'

Hint 1: Words *el cielo*, *es*, *azul*.
Hint 2: Sentence *El cielo es azul.*

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What does *verde* mean? 2. What does *blanco* mean?

Exercises

  1. Point to each colour as it is named, using the picture or real objects.
  2. The guide holds up something red and waits. Say Es rojo.
  3. Copy and read: El sol es amarillo.

Latin · Vīlla and hortus

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin reading (in the spirit of Ørberg and of *Minimus*). All text here is original; those books are not copied.

Learn

*Vīlla* is a house; *hortus* is a garden. *In hortō* means in the garden (the ending changes after *in* when it means 'in' or 'on').

hortusHOR-tussgarden

estestis

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Learn

For the guide: *in vīllā* and *in hortō* change their endings after *in* ("in the house", "in the garden"). Point at the place; do not stop for a grammar lecture. *Vīlla* has a long *i*.

A house (vīlla), a garden with a tree (hortus), and a path (via). Gaia stands in the garden.

**Read, looking at the picture.** - *Vīlla est magna.* - *Aulus est in vīllā.* - *Gaia est in hortō.* - *Via est longa.*

Remember

*Gaia est in hortō* — Gaia is in the garden. The form *hortō* is used after *in*.

Watch me

The picture shows a house and a garden. Vīlla est names the house. Gaia est in hortō tells you where Gaia is.

Together

Say in Latin: 'Aulus is in the garden.'

Hint 1: Words *Aulus*, *est*, *in hortō*.
Hint 2: Sentence *Aulus est in hortō.*

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What does *vīlla* mean? 2. What does *in hortō* mean?

Exercises

  1. Point: vīlla, hortus, via.
  2. Who is in the garden? Read the Latin sentence that says so.
  3. Read all four lines aloud.

Notebook: how big is the Sun?

60 minutes · draw first, then label, then *because / but / so* · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html) · [Review sheet](../printables/d03-notebook.html)

Learn

If Earth is drawn **1 cm** across, every other width is 'how many times Earth'. Jupiter is about 142,984 ÷ 12,756 ≈ 11.2 times as wide, so Jupiter is **11.2 cm** across. The Sun is about 1,391,400 ÷ 12,756 ≈ 109 times as wide: **109 cm**. That will not fit on the page, so you draw only a piece of the rim and write the full width beside it.

Remember

At this scale, model cm = real width ÷ Earth's width (12,756 km). The Sun's full circle is more than a metre across.

Watch me

The Moon is 3,475 km across. At this scale (Earth = 1 cm):

  1. 3,475 ÷ 12,756 ≈ 0.27.
  2. The Moon is a circle about 0.27 cm across — a very small dot next to Earth's 1 cm.

Together

About how many times as wide as Earth is the Sun? Use 1,391,400 ÷ 12,756, rounded.

Hint 1: Divide 1,391,400 ÷ 12,756 is a little more than 109.
Hint 2: Scale Draw a 109 cm width, or a piece of that rim, and label it.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. If Earth is 1 cm, about how wide is a planet 25,500 km across?

Practice

How the guide helps today: new writing step: *because / but / so*. Keep the same opening, "The Sun is huge", and finish it three ways: a reason (*because*), a contrast (*but*), and a result (*so*). The student says each full sentence before writing. Help by asking "because *why*?", not by dictating the ending.

Draw Earth as a circle 1 cm across. Then, at the same scale:

  • Jupiter (142,984 km) is about 11 times as wide as Earth (12,756 km): draw it 11.2 cm across with a compass or a traced plate.
  • The Sun (1,391,400 km) is about 109 times as wide as Earth: 109 cm. It will not fit on the page; draw just a piece of its edge as a curve, and write how big the whole circle would be.

1. Draw those three sizes (Earth, Jupiter, a piece of the Sun's edge).

2. Label from the word bank.

Word bank: Earth · Jupiter · Sun · diameter · scale · centimetre · wider · curve · huge · edge

3. Write. First finish the stems:

  • Earth is drawn ________.
  • Jupiter is about ________ times as wide as Earth.
  • The whole Sun at this scale would be ________.

Then finish all three (same opening each time):

  • The Sun is huge because ________.
  • The Sun is huge but ________.
  • The Sun is huge so ________.

4. Numbers. Check the two "times as wide" numbers yourself: is 11 × 12,756 close to 142,984? Is 109 × 12,756 close to 1,391,400? (Use rounding to check, or ask for help with the multiplication; it is coming next week.)
