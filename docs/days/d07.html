Unit 2 · Day 7 · Riding the Earth around the Sun

Today's big question: Right now you are moving around the Sun with the Earth at about 30 kilometres every second. How far do you travel in a minute, an hour, a day?

For the guide: today is about multiplying smartly: by 10, 100 and 1,000 (digits shift left); breaking apart; and doubling and halving. Ask the student to explain each shortcut with the area model, so it is understood, not just used.

Mental math warm-up

10 minutes

Learn

Two strong strategies: **double and halve** (one factor ×2, the other ÷2, the product stays the same — useful when 25 × 4 or 25 × 8 appears), and **compensation** for 99: 99 × 3 = 100 × 3 − 3.

Remember

25 × 4 = 100, so 25 × 8 = 200. 99 × *n* = 100 × *n* − *n*. 15 × 6 = 10 × 6 + 5 × 6.

Watch me

25 × 8 by doubling and halving, or by building on 25 × 4.

  1. 25 × 4 = 100, so 25 × 8 = 25 × 4 × 2 = 200.
  2. Or: double 25 and halve 8, to get 50 × 4 = 200.

Together

99 × 3 by compensation.

Hint 1: Near 100 100 × 3 = 300.
Hint 2: Take back You added one extra 3, so 300 − 3 = **297**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. 15 × 6, by a split. 2. 50 × 18 (think 100 × 9, or 50 × 20 − 50 × 2).

Practice

  1. 25 × 4
  2. 25 × 8
  3. 15 × 6
  4. 99 × 3
  5. 50 × 18
  6. 12 × 11
  7. 30 × 60
  8. 400 × 30

Core lesson: multiplying by tens and by breaking apart

45 minutes

Learn

**The fact.** Earth moves along its orbit at an average of about **29.8 km per second** (NASA). We will round to 30. **Multiplying by 10, 100, 1,000.** Each digit moves one place left for every zero: 30 × 10 = 300; 30 × 100 = 3,000. So 30 × 60 = 30 × 6 × 10 = 180 × 10 = **1,800 km in one minute**. **One hour.** 1,800 × 60 = 18 × 6 × 1,000 = 108 × 1,000 = **108,000 km in an hour** (with the precise 29.8 km/s it is about 107,000 km). **Break apart.** 36 × 7 = 30 × 7 + 6 × 7 = 210 + 42 = 252. The area model shows why: a 36-by-7 rectangle splits into a 30-by-7 piece and a 6-by-7 piece. **Double and halve.** Doubling one factor and halving the other keeps the product the same: 16 × 25 = 8 × 50 = 4 × 100 = 400. (In the area model, the rectangle becomes twice as long and half as tall: same area.)

Remember

For a two-digit factor, split into tens and ones (the area model). When one factor is even and the other ends in 25 or 50, try double-and-halve.

Watch me

36 × 7, split 36.

  1. 30 × 7 = 210.
  2. 6 × 7 = 42.
  3. 210 + 42 = 252.

And 16 × 25: halve 16 and double 25, to get 8 × 50 = 400.

Together

24 × 25. Use double-and-halve.

Hint 1: Halve 24, double 25 12 × 50.
Hint 2: Or once more 6 × 100 = **600**.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. 48 × 25, by the same method. 2. 17 × 6 as 10 × 6 + 7 × 6.

Practice

  1. 47 × 6
  2. 125 × 8
  3. Every 4 years, the calendar has 3 years of 365 days and one leap year of 366. How many days are in 4 years?
  4. Light travels about 300,000 km every second. How far in 4 seconds?
  5. Earth travels about 1,800 km a minute. How far in 10 minutes? In 30 minutes?
  6. Use doubling and halving: 35 × 14.

Challenge problems

25 minutes

Learn

Seconds in a day come from multiplying the units you already know: 60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour, 24 hours in a day. A missing-digit product is solved by undoing: 148 ÷ 4. To compare 25 × 48 and 24 × 50 without finishing either, rewrite both with the same strategies (double-and-halve, or split).

Remember

1 day = 24 × 60 × 60 seconds. You can compare products by rewriting, not only by computing both.

Watch me

How many seconds in 2 minutes?

  1. 2 × 60 = 120 seconds.

Which is larger, 10 × 24 or 12 × 20? 10 × 24 = 240 and 12 × 20 = 240 — they are equal. (12 × 20 is 10 × 20 + 2 × 20, or double-and-halve 10 × 24 by taking 12 × 20.)

Together

Find the missing digit: 3? × 4 = 148.

Hint 1: Undo 148 ÷ 4 = 37.
Hint 2: So The missing digit is 7: 37 × 4 = 148.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. How many seconds are in 5 minutes?

Practice

  1. How many seconds are there in one day? Show your steps.
  2. Find the missing digit: 3? × 4 = 148.
  3. Which is larger, 25 × 48 or 24 × 50? Decide without multiplying either one out, then check.
  4. If Earth moves about 30 km every second, roughly how far does it travel in one day? (Use your answer to question 1 and round sensibly.)

Math circle: the pigeonhole principle

20 minutes

Learn

The **pigeonhole principle**: if you put more items than the number of boxes, and every item goes in a box, then at least one box holds two or more. To be *sure* of a result in the worst case, imagine avoiding it for as long as you can, then take one more.

Remember

To be sure of two of the same kind when there are *k* kinds, take *k* + 1 (one of each, then one more).

Watch me

Red gloves and blue gloves. How many must you take to be sure of two the same colour?

  1. Worst case: first a red, then a blue (one of each).
  2. The next glove, a third one, must match one of them.
  3. So 3 gloves are enough, and 2 are not always enough.

Together

A crew of 13 people. Why must at least two share a birth month?

Hint 1: Boxes There are 12 months.
Hint 2: 13 > 12 Thirteen people into 12 months means at least one month has two people.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. To be sure of two gloves of the same colour (red or blue), how many must you take? 2. A crew of 13: why do at least two share a birth month?

Practice

  1. In a dark cupboard there are many red gloves and many blue gloves, all mixed up. How many must you take out, without looking, to be sure of having two of the same colour?
  2. A crew has 13 people. Prove that at least two of them have birthdays in the same month.
  3. Is it true that among any 4 whole numbers, two of them have a difference that divides exactly by 3? Explain.

Spanish · El perro y el gato

15 minutes · Method: comprehensible input (Krashen) and Total Physical Response (Asher). Listen and act first; speak when ready. Instructions to the guide are in English.

Learn

*Perro* is dog and *gato* is cat. *Corre* means runs; *duerme* means sleeps.

Learn

For the guide: act *corre* and *duerme* the same way as Latin *currit* and *dormit*. If you have a toy dog or cat, use it. Review *grande, pequeño, uno, dos*.

A dog running; a cat sleeping.

**Look and listen.** - *El perro. El gato.* - *El perro es grande. El gato es pequeño.* - *El perro corre. El gato duerme.* *El perro come.* **Act.** *Corre. Duerme. Señala el perro. Señala el gato.*

Remember

*El perro corre* — the dog runs. *El gato duerme* — the cat sleeps. Same pattern as *Ana mira*: someone + what they do.

Watch me

El perro es grande. El perro corre. First we say what it is like, then what it does.

Together

Say in Spanish: 'The cat sleeps.'

Hint 1: Words *el gato*, *duerme*.
Hint 2: Sentence *El gato duerme.*

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. What does *el perro* mean? 2. What does *corre* mean?

Exercises

  1. Point: el perro, el gato. Act: corre, duerme.
  2. Say: El perro es grande. El gato es pequeño.
  3. Copy: El perro corre.

Learn

For the guide: say the Latin numbers with fingers, then with the tally marks. Roman numerals I–X are useful, but the spoken words matter more today.

**Listen and count.** *ūnus, duo, trēs, quattuor, quīnque, sex, septem, octō, novem, decem.* Written: I II III IV V VI VII VIII IX X.

Roman numerals I to X, with the first five Latin number words.

Remember

*Ūnus, duo, trēs, quattuor, quīnque, sex, septem, octō, novem, decem.* I II III IV V VI VII VIII IX X are the same numbers.

Watch me

The guide holds up two fingers and says duo, and may write II. Both names mean 2.

Together

How do you say 5 and 10 in Latin?

Hint 2: 10 *decem*

Notebook: a clock for Mars

60 minutes · invent, then three connected sentences · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html) · [Review sheet](../printables/d07-notebook.html)

Learn

A day on Mars (a **sol**) lasts about **24 hours 39 minutes 35 seconds**, often rounded to 24 hours 40 minutes — about 40 minutes longer than an Earth day. If people on Mars kept a 24-hour clock with Earth-length hours, the clock would drift later by about 40 minutes each day. A Mars clock could instead keep 24 hours and make each hour a little longer, or keep extra minutes at the end of the day. Your job is to choose a design and show why it works.

Remember

One sol ≈ 24 h 40 min. That extra 40 minutes is what a Mars clock must do something with.

Watch me

After three sols, a clock that ignored the extra time would be about 3 × 40 = 120 minutes (2 hours) behind the sky on Mars. That is why engineers who once lived on 'Mars time' shifted their day later each Earth day.

Together

About how many extra minutes pile up in a week of sols (7 sols) if you use Earth hours only?

Hint 1: Multiply 7 × 40 = 280 minutes.
Hint 2: Hours 280 ÷ 60 = 4 hours and 40 minutes late.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

1. About how much longer is a sol than an Earth day?

Practice

How the guide helps today: the student should invent the design, not be given one. For writing, aim at three short sentences that go together: what the design is, one advantage, one problem. Number them 1, 2, 3 if that helps. Oral rehearsal first, as always.

A day on Mars (a sol) lasts about 24 hours and 40 minutes: a little longer than ours. Engineers running rovers on Mars have lived on "Mars time", shifting their working day 40 minutes later each day.

Invent: design a clock face, or a calendar, for people living on Mars. Decide: would you keep 24 hours and make each one a little longer, or have 24 ordinary hours plus a 40-minute "extra" time?

1. Draw your clock or calendar, large, with labels for the hours (and the extra time, if you use it).

2. Label the parts from the word bank, plus any words you need (the guide may write extra words for copying).

Word bank: Mars · sol · hour · minute · clock · calendar · Earth time · extra · advantage · problem

3. Write three connected sentences (say each one first):

  • My Mars clock ________.
  • One advantage is ________.
  • One problem is ________.

4. Numbers. After 3 sols, how far behind Earth time would a Mars-time worker be? After 9 sols?
