Mental math warm-up: in, then out

10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it

Learn

A rule takes a number in and gives a number out. Find the output, or the input.

Remember

Do what the rule says to the input. To find an input, undo the rule.

Watch me

The rule is “multiply by 4, then add 1.” What is the output when the input is 5?

  1. 4 × 5 = 20, then 20 + 1 = 21.
  2. The output is 21.

Together

The same rule. The output is 13. What was the input?

Hint 1: Undo the add

13 − 1 = 12.

Hint 2: Undo the multiply

12 ÷ 4 = 3. Check: 4 × 3 + 1 = 13.

Check

These are input-output facts.

  1. The rule is “add 7.” Input 9: what is the output?
  2. The rule is “halve.” Input 18: what is the output?

Practice

  1. The rule is “multiply by 3.” Output 24: what was the input?
  2. The rule is “subtract 5.” Input 20: what is the output?

Core lesson: a rule as an input and an output, and the lever

40 minutes · Method: a number machine, then a labeled invented lever whose two sides balance as products

Learn

A rule, sometimes called a function in later work, takes an input and gives one output. You can think of it as a machine: a number goes in, the rule is applied, a number comes out.

A classroom rule. The rule “double, then add 3,” or n → 2n + 3:

  • input 4 → 2 × 4 + 3 = 11
  • input 6 → 2 × 6 + 3 = 15

Each input has one output under this rule. To find the input from an output, undo the rule, in reverse order: subtract 3, then halve.

A lever, as a first model. A lever is a bar that turns on a point, the fulcrum. In this course’s first model, a smaller mass farther from the fulcrum can balance a larger mass nearer to it.

All masses and distances in this sitting are labeled invented classroom numbers, not a measured machine and not a NASA crane figure.

The first equation we will use. For this model, the two sides balance when:

mass × distance on the left equals mass × distance on the right.

In the drawing, 2 × 6 = 12 and 3 × 4 = 12, so the invented lever is balanced. If one product is smaller, that side would tip up, in this model.

We will not invent a historical measurement, and we will not invent a NASA figure, to “confirm” the products.

Remember

A rule takes an input and gives one output. Undo in reverse to find an input. For this course’s first lever model, mass × distance on the left equals mass × distance on the right when it balances. Invented masses and distances stay labeled.

Watch me

The rule is n → 2n + 1. What is the output for n = 7, and what input gives the output 11?

  1. 2 × 7 + 1 = 15.
  2. 11 − 1 = 10, and 10 ÷ 2 = 5, so the input is 5.
  3. Check: 2 × 5 + 1 = 11.

Together

A labeled invented lever has a mass of 4 at 3 units on the left. The right mass is 6. How far from the fulcrum must it sit to balance, in this model?

Hint 1: The left product

4 × 3 = 12.

Hint 2: The right distance

6 × d = 12, so d = 2 units. Invented lever.

Check

Use the rule or the lever model, as asked.

  1. The rule is n → 3n. What is the output for 5, and what input gives 18?
  2. In the drawing’s invented lever, why does the mass 2, farther out, balance the mass 3, nearer in?

Practice

  1. The rule is n → n + 8. Input 6: what is the output? Output 20: what was the input?
  2. A labeled invented lever has 5 at 4 units on the left, and 10 on the right. Find the right distance that balances, in this model.
  3. Why is “each input has one output” part of the rule, as taught today?
  4. Why will this lesson not invent a NASA crane figure for the lever?

Challenge problems: undo a rule, and a missing lever mass

25 minutes · an input from an output, and a missing lever number, still labeled invented

Learn

These extend today’s rule and today’s lever model. Invented masses and distances stay labeled.

Remember

Undo a rule in reverse order. For the lever, mass × distance equals mass × distance, in this model, when it balances.

Watch me

The rule is n → 2n − 3. The output is 11. What was the input?

  1. Add 3: 11 + 3 = 14.
  2. Halve: 14 ÷ 2 = 7.
  3. Check: 2 × 7 − 3 = 11.

Together

A labeled invented lever is balanced. The left mass is n at 5 units. The right mass is 10 at 4 units. Find n.

Hint 1: The right product

10 × 4 = 40.

Hint 2: The left mass

n × 5 = 40, so n = 8. Invented lever. Check: 8 × 5 = 40.

Check

Still a check, and still labeled.

  1. The rule is n → 4n + 2. Output 22: what was the input?
  2. A labeled invented lever has 3 at 8 units on the left, and n at 6 units on the right, and it balances. Find n.

Practice

  1. The rule is n → 2(n + 5). Input 3: what is the output? Output 18: what was the input?
  2. If a friend doubles only the mass on one side of a balanced invented lever, and leaves the distances as they were, what happens in this model?
  3. Why is “I multiplied mass by mass, and distance by distance” a miss in this model?

Math circle: why a longer arm can balance a heavier mass

20 minutes · a short argument, then a check of the reason

Learn

Why, in this course’s first lever model, can a smaller mass farther from the fulcrum balance a larger mass nearer to it?

Remember

Each side contributes a product, mass × distance. A smaller mass can make the same product if its distance is larger.

Watch me

In the lesson’s invented drawing, 2 at 6 units balances 3 at 4 units. Why is that not a surprise, in this model?

  1. 2 × 6 = 12 and 3 × 4 = 12.
  2. The smaller mass has the longer arm, so the products match.
  3. The numbers are invented and labeled; the reason is the matching products, not a NASA figure.

Together

What would go wrong if we only compared the two masses, and ignored the distances?

Hint 1: The masses alone

3 is larger than 2, so we might think the 3 must tip that side down.

Hint 2: The model

The model uses both the mass and how far it sits. Distance is part of the balance, not an extra decoration.

Check

These check the reason.

  1. A labeled invented lever has 1 at 12 units on the left and 6 on the right. What right distance would balance, and why can 1 balance 6 at all in this model?
  2. How is a lever like yesterday’s pan balance, and how is it not the same picture?

Practice

  1. Why must the units of distance on both sides be the same kind (both in cm, or both in units of the same size)?
  2. This argument is not a score or a badge.

Spanish · la palanca, el punto, pesado, and ligero

15 minutes · Instruction in English; Hear for Spanish words · present tense only

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
la palanca lah pah-LAHNG-kah the lever
el punto el POON-toh the point (the fulcrum, in today’s picture)
pesado peh-SAH-doh heavy
ligero lee-HEH-roh light

Today’s words name a lever, a point, heavy, and light. Instruction stays in English. cerca and lejos are already known from Day 32 and can be reused. Lever arithmetic stays in the English mathematics; do not say “3 × 6 = 18” in the Spanish.

Original sentences: Hay una palanca. Hay un punto. El peso es pesado. El peso es ligero.

For the guide: point to a drawn lever, the fulcrum, a heavy block, and a light block, without products. Present tense only. el peso is already known from Day 36.

Remember

la palanca; el punto; pesado; ligero. Instruction in English. Hear for Spanish words. No lever products in the Spanish.

Watch me

Say: “There is a lever.”

  1. Hay una palanca.
  2. Hay introduces it. Una palanca is the lever.

Together

How do you say “The weight is heavy”?

Hint 1: Heavy

pesado

Hint 2: The sentence

El peso es pesado.

Check

These use only today’s phrases.

  1. Write: “There is a point.”
  2. Write: “The weight is light.”

Exercises

  1. Hear palanca, punto, and ligero, then use each.
  2. Why does the Spanish not multiply mass by distance?
  3. A pointing retell: a lever, a point, heavy, light.
  4. Copy one model sentence into the notebook.
  5. Correct: *El palanca, if someone used the wrong article.

Latin · vectis and prope

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin; original sentences. Instruction in English.

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
vectis WEK-tis a lever / a bar used as a lever (nominative, masculine)
Vectis longus est WEK-tis LON-gus est The lever is long
prope PRO-peh near
Pondus prope est PON-dus PRO-peh est The weight is near

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Original sentences, not from a textbook: Vectis longus est. Vectis nōn longus est. Pondus prope est. Pondus nōn prope est.

vectis is masculine, so longus matches. prope does not change. pondus is already known from Day 36. Yesterday’s procul (far) can be contrasted, in English, with today’s prope (near), if it helps the picture. Lever products stay in the English mathematics.

For the guide: the student reads as much as possible. Point to a drawn lever, then to a weight near the fulcrum. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation.

Remember

vectis; Vectis longus est; prope; Pondus prope est. Instruction in English. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation. No lever products in the Latin.

Watch me

Read: “The lever is long.”

  1. Vectis longus est.
  2. vectis is the lever. longus matches, because vectis is masculine.

Together

How do you say “The weight is near”?

Hint 1: Near

prope

Hint 2: The sentence

Pondus prope est.

Check

These use only today’s captions.

  1. Write: “The lever is not long.”
  2. Write: “The weight is not near.”

Exercises

Notebook: a lever drawing, and a because-sentence

about 1 hour · a labeled invented lever, then because / but / so

Learn

Draw a labeled invented lever that balances, with the two products shown, then write a because / but / so sentence about why a lighter mass can sit farther out.

Remember

The drawing shows mass × distance on each side. The stems explain the habit: the products match, and the numbers stay labeled invented.

Watch me

Start: A lighter mass can balance a heavier one because ________.

  1. The because names the longer arm, or the matching products.
  2. A sound because: …its distance is larger, so the products can still match…

Together

Finish: A lighter mass can balance a heavier one because ________, but these numbers are invented, so ________.

Hint 1: The but

…they are classroom numbers, not a NASA crane figure…

Hint 2: The so

…I will keep the lever labeled invented, and I will write both products.

Check

The page should show the drawing and complete stems.

  1. Draw a labeled invented lever that balances (you may reuse 2 at 6 units and 3 at 4 units), and write both products.
  2. Write a three-stem explanation (because / but / so) about the longer arm and the label invented.

Practice

  1. Tick the word-bank words you used: lever, fulcrum, mass, distance, invented, product.
  2. The page is a record, not a score.
  3. File the page. It is not a reward.
  4. A note that no NASA crane figure was invented.
