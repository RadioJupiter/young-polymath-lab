Mental math warm-up: equal jumps of distance

10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it

Learn

A rate compares two quantities of different kinds, often a distance and a time. Today’s warm-up practices equal jumps, so “kilometers per hour” is ready.

Remember

If the same distance is covered in each equal time, the rate is that distance per one unit of time. 12 ÷ 3 = 4.

Watch me

An invented walk covers 12 km in 3 hours, at a steady rate. How far in 1 hour?

  1. The 12 km is split into 3 equal hours.
  2. 12 ÷ 3 = 4.
  3. 4 km in 1 hour, or 4 km per hour. The walk is invented.

Together

At 4 km per hour, how far in 2 hours, and how long to walk 8 km?

Hint 1: Two hours

4 × 2 = 8 km.

Hint 2: Eight kilometers

8 ÷ 4 = 2 hours. Still invented.

Check

These use only equal jumps.

  1. 15 km in 5 hours, invented and steady: how far in 1 hour?
  2. At 6 km per hour, how far in 3 hours? (Invented.)

Practice

  1. At 5 km per hour, how long for 20 km? (Invented.)
  2. How many seconds in 2 minutes?

Core lesson: rate, and speed as distance per time

35–40 minutes · Method: concrete (equal jumps of a walk), pictorial (a rate strip), then speed = distance ÷ time, with sourced ISS and light figures labeled

Learn

A rate compares two quantities of different kinds, such as a distance and a time. Speed is a rate: distance per time. We write it with a “per,” for example 4 km per hour, or 4 km/h. The pictured walk is invented: 12 km in 3 hours, in equal jumps of 4 km each hour, so the speed is 4 km/h. It stays labeled invented.

The three related calculations, once the units match:

  • speed = distance ÷ time
  • distance = speed × time
  • time = distance ÷ speed

If a time is given in minutes, we first write it as a fraction of an hour (30 minutes = 0.5 h) or we work in minutes all the way through. We do not mix kilometers per hour with a time still in minutes without that conversion.

Light, already taught. Light travels about 300,000 km every second (the exact SI value is 299,792,458 m/s). Distance = 300,000 × the number of seconds. Time = distance ÷ 300,000. We already used this for the Moon (about 1.3 s) and the Sun (500 s, or 8 minutes 20 seconds). That speed is sourced; we will not invent a new official value of c.

The International Space Station. NASA’s Spot the Station FAQ says: “The International Space Station orbits the Earth once every 90 minutes. It travels at about 17,500 mph (28,000 kilometers per hour).” We will compute with about 28,000 km/h, labeled as that FAQ figure. In 2 hours the ISS goes about 56,000 km; in 30 minutes (0.5 h) about 14,000 km; in 90 minutes (1.5 h) about 42,000 km. That last product is what those two rounded FAQ figures give when we multiply, not a surveyed length of one orbit published as its own NASA measurement.

NASA’s “International Space Station Facts and Figures” page also says a crew is “traveling at a speed of five miles per second, orbiting Earth about every 90 minutes,” and “In 24 hours, the space station makes 16 orbits of Earth.” Sixteen orbits in 24 hours matches 90 minutes per orbit (24 × 60 ÷ 90 = 16). “Five miles per second” is another NASA rounded figure (5 miles per second would be 18,000 mph, coarser than 17,500 mph). We will not average 17,500 mph and 18,000 mph, or 28,000 km/h and a homemade conversion of five miles per second, into a new official speed.

Invented walks stay labeled. A “4 km/h walk” in a practice item is invented, not a NASA figure, and not the ISS.

Remember

Speed = distance ÷ time, with matching units. Distance = speed × time; time = distance ÷ speed. Invented walks stay labeled. Light is about 300,000 km/s, as already taught. The ISS is about 28,000 km/h from NASA’s Spot the Station FAQ; “five miles per second” is another NASA rounded figure, not averaged in. 28,000 km/h × 1.5 h = 42,000 km is a product of two rounded FAQ figures, not a surveyed orbit length.

Watch me

An invented bicycle covers 18 km in 3 hours, steadily. What is the speed, and how far in 5 hours at that speed?

  1. Speed = 18 ÷ 3 = 6 km/h.
  2. Distance in 5 hours = 6 × 5 = 30 km.
  3. The bicycle, the 18 km, and the 30 km are invented, so they stay labeled invented.

Together

The ISS travels at about 28,000 km/h (NASA FAQ). How far, at that labeled figure, in 2 hours?

Hint 1: The formula

Distance = speed × time.

Hint 2: The product

28,000 × 2 = 56,000 km, using the FAQ’s about-28,000 km/h.

Check

These use only today’s rates.

  1. An invented walk is 20 km in 4 hours, steadily. What is the speed, and how long for 10 km at that speed?
  2. How far does light travel in 3 seconds, at the already-taught about 300,000 km/s?

Practice

  1. At 28,000 km/h, how far in 30 minutes? (First write 30 minutes as a fraction of an hour.)
  2. Time = distance ÷ speed. At 28,000 km/h, how many hours for 84,000 km? Label the speed as the FAQ figure.
  3. Why do we not average “about 17,500 mph” with “five miles per second” to make a homemade official speed?
  4. An invented bus goes 12 km in 15 minutes. What is that in km/h? (15 minutes = 0.25 h, or 12 km in a quarter hour is 48 km in one hour.)
  5. 28,000 km/h × 1.5 h = 42,000 km. What is that 42,000 km, and what is it not?

Challenge problems: mixed times, and a labeled orbit product

20 minutes · after the core, or later if the core took the full sitting

Learn

These items mix invented rates and sourced speeds. Minutes must be written in hours when the speed is in km/h. We will not invent an orbital speed for a planet, or a new value of the speed of light.

Remember

Same rate method as the core. Labels stay honest. No invented planetary orbital speed.

Watch me

An invented train travels 150 km in 2.5 hours. What is the speed, and how long for 90 km at that speed?

  1. 150 ÷ 2.5 = 60 km/h.
  2. 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5 hours, or 1 hour 30 minutes.
  3. The train is invented.

Together

Light takes about 1.3 seconds to come from the Moon, as already taught. About how far is that at 300,000 km/s, and how does it sit beside 384,400 km?

Hint 1: The product

300,000 × 1.3 = 390,000 km.

Hint 2: The already-taught distance

The mean distance we use is 384,400 km. 1.3 s is already a rounded light-time, so 390,000 km is a rounded product, not a new official distance. We keep 384,400 km as the distance and 1.3 s as the labeled light-time.

Check

These stay with rates and labels.

  1. At 8 km/h, how far in 45 minutes? (Invented; 45 minutes = 0.75 h.)
  2. How many 90-minute orbits fit in 6 hours?

Practice

  1. At 28,000 km/h, how far in 15 minutes?
  2. A friend says “the ISS is faster than light because 28,000 is a bigger number than 300,000 is small.” What is missing?
  3. Why will this course not write “Earth orbits the Sun at 28,000 km/h” from today’s ISS figure?
  4. An invented run is 2 km in 20 minutes. What is that in km/h?
  5. This wrap is not a second score.

Math circle: a speed needs a time, and a figure needs a label

20 minutes · a short reason, then a check

Learn

A longer distance is not automatically a greater speed, and a shorter time is not automatically a greater speed either. Speed is the rate: distance per time. In the same way, two rounded NASA figures for the ISS are not automatically two votes to average. Each figure keeps its label.

Remember

Speed is distance per time, with matching units. About 28,000 km/h is the FAQ figure we compute with. “Five miles per second” stays a separate rounded NASA figure. We do not invent a planetary orbital speed.

Watch me

Who is faster: an invented walker who goes 8 km in 2 hours, or one who goes 9 km in 3 hours? Why is “9 km is more” not enough?

  1. First walker: 8 ÷ 2 = 4 km/h.
  2. Second walker: 9 ÷ 3 = 3 km/h.
  3. The first walker is faster. The 9 km is a longer distance, but it took more time, and the rate is smaller.

Together

Why do we keep “about 28,000 km/h” and “five miles per second” labeled separately?

Hint 1: They are both rounded

Five miles per second is 18,000 mph, coarser than the FAQ’s about 17,500 mph.

Hint 2: The habit

Averaging them would invent a homemade official speed. We compute with 28,000 km/h, labeled as the FAQ’s metric figure.

Check

These check the reason.

  1. Name the three related calculations for speed, distance, and time.
  2. What must match before we divide a distance by a time?

Practice

  1. Give one reason 28,000 km/h is not faster than 300,000 km/s.
  2. 16 orbits in 24 hours and 90 minutes per orbit: why do those two NASA statements agree?
  3. Why is 42,000 km (28,000 × 1.5) not published here as “NASA’s official orbit length”?
  4. An invented car goes twice as far as another in the same time. What can we say about their speeds?
  5. This argument is not a badge.

Spanish · rápido, despacio, camina, and corre

15 minutes · Instruction in English; Hear for Spanish words · present tense only

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
rápido RAH-pee-doh fast (masculine singular)
despacio des-PAH-syoh slowly
camina kah-MEE-nah he or she walks / you (formal) walk
corre KOH-rreh he or she runs / you (formal) run

Today’s words name fast and slowly, and the present-tense actions camina (walks) and corre (runs). Instruction stays in English. Speed calculations, km/h, and the ISS figure stay in the English mathematics.

Original sentences: Camina despacio. Corre rápido. No corre despacio. No camina rápido.

despacio is an adverb (slowly). rápido is used here as in everyday speech after corre (runs fast). We do not open a side lesson on rápidamente.

For the guide: walk slowly and run in place as you say the words. This is present-tense description, not a rate lesson in Spanish.

Remember

rápido; despacio; camina; corre. Instruction in English. Hear for Spanish words. No km/h arithmetic in the Spanish.

Watch me

Say: “He or she walks slowly.”

  1. Camina despacio.
  2. Camina is the walking. Despacio is slowly.
  3. This is a present-tense pattern.

Together

How do you say “runs,” and “He or she runs fast”?

Hint 1: Runs

corre

Hint 2: The sentence

Corre rápido.

Check

These use only today’s phrases.

  1. Write: “He or she runs slowly.” (using no and despacio if you prefer the model No corre despacio, or write Corre despacio.)
  2. Write: “He or she does not walk fast.”

Exercises

  1. Correct: *Camina rápido despacio as a single mixed command, if it appears; keep one adverb.
  2. Hear camina, corre, and despacio, then use each.
  3. Why does the Spanish not say 28,000 km/h?
  4. A pointing retell: walks, slowly, runs, fast.
  5. Copy one model sentence into the notebook.

Latin · celer and tardus

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin; original sentences. Instruction in English.

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
celer KEH-lehr swift, fast (masculine nominative)
Canis celer est KAH-nis KEH-lehr est The dog is swift
tardus TAR-dus slow (masculine nominative)
Canis tardus nōn est KAH-nis TAR-dus nohn est The dog is not slow

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Original sentences, not from a textbook: Canis celer est. Canis tardus nōn est. Canis nōn celer est. Canis tardus est.

canis is masculine here, so celer and tardus match. You already met Canis Celer as a name on Day 4; today celer is the adjective “swift,” not a proper name. Speed figures, km/h, and the ISS stay in the English mathematics.

For the guide: the student reads as much as possible. Point to a swift dog in a picture, then to a slow one. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation.

Remember

celer; Canis celer est; tardus; Canis tardus nōn est. Adjectives match. celer is an adjective today, not only a name. No km/h in the Latin. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation.

Watch me

Read: Canis celer est. Why is celer not being used as a name here?

  1. It is the adjective “swift,” matching canis.
  2. The sentence says the dog is swift.
  3. Day 4’s Canis Celer was a name. Today’s job is the adjective.

Together

Say: “The dog is not slow.”

Hint 1: The noun

canis, masculine.

Hint 2: The sentence

Canis tardus nōn est.

Check

These use only today’s patterns.

  1. Write: “The dog is not swift.”
  2. Write: “The dog is slow.”

Exercises

  1. Correct: *canis celeris est if someone added a needless ending, or *canis celere est; today’s masculine nominative is celer.
  2. Hear celer and tardus, then use each in a sentence.

Notebook: a rate strip, and a because-sentence

60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · How the notebook works · Review sheet

Learn

Draw a rate strip for one invented steady walk (for example 12 km in 3 hours, 4 km/h) and write one ISS calculation with the FAQ label (for example 28,000 × 2 = 56,000 km). Then complete a because / but / so stem about not averaging “about 17,500 mph” with “five miles per second,” or about units when someone compares 28,000 km/h with 300,000 km/s.

Remember

Draw and label first. Complete stems. Invented walks stay labeled. This page is a record, not a score or a badge.

Watch me

A model sentence: The invented walk is 4 km/h because 12 ÷ 3 = 4, but I will not average five miles per second with 17,500 mph, so I will compute with about 28,000 km/h and leave that as the FAQ’s metric figure.

  1. The because uses speed = distance ÷ time.
  2. The but refuses a homemade official speed.
  3. The so keeps the FAQ label.

Together

Finish: 28,000 km/h is not faster than 300,000 km/s because ________, but the 28,000 looks “bigger” if I forget the units, so ________.

Hint 1: The because

…the units are not the same; in one second the ISS goes only a few kilometers…

Hint 2: The so

…I will match units before I compare speeds.

Check

The page should show the strip, one ISS product, and complete stems.

  1. Draw the invented rate strip, labeled invented, and write one ISS distance with the FAQ label.
  2. Write a three-stem explanation (because / but / so) about labels or about matching units.

Practice

  1. Tick the word-bank words you used: rate, speed, per, invented, FAQ, units, light.
  2. A note that 42,000 km is a product of two rounded figures, if that helps the page.
  3. The page is a record, not a score.
  4. File the page. It is not a reward.
  5. A note that no planetary orbital speed was invented.
