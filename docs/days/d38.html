For the guide: A classroom pattern has a rule you can say and check. The NASA Planetary Fact Sheet (page last updated 13 November 2026) lists distance from the Sun and orbital period. Mental math warm-up: the next number in a pattern

10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it

Learn

A list follows a rule. Name the rule, then give the next number.

Remember

A pattern has a rule you can say, then use. The next number must follow that same rule.

Watch me

3, 6, 9, 12, … What is the next number, and what is the rule?

  1. The rule is “add 3 each time.”
  2. The next number is 15.

Together

40, 32, 24, 16, … What is the next number?

Hint 1: The change

Each term is 8 less than the one before.

Hint 2: The next

16 − 8 = 8.

Check

These are short patterns.

  1. 2, 4, 8, 16, … What is the next number, and what is the rule?
  2. 5, 8, 11, 14, … What is the 6th number, if 5 is the first?

Practice

  1. 1, 5, 9, 13, … What is the rule?
  2. 100, 90, 80, 70, … What is the next number?

Core lesson: a pattern in a table, and planetary years

40 minutes · Method: a rule in a table, then a sourced NASA pattern, without inventing a formula

Learn

A pattern is a rule you can say and use. One way to show it is a table: a position, or an input, and the number that belongs with it.

A classroom pattern first. The rule “start at 4, add 5 each time” gives:

1 → 4, 2 → 9, 3 → 14, 4 → 19.

The 5th number is 24. We can write the 1st number as 4, and the nth as 4 + 5(n − 1), if we want a short rule, and we can check it on a number we already have: when n = 3, 4 + 5 × 2 = 14.

A pattern in planetary data. The NASA Planetary Fact Sheet lists each planet’s mean distance from the Sun and its orbital period (how many days that planet takes to go once around the Sun).

Planet Distance from the Sun (106 km) Orbital period (days)
Mercury 57.9 88.0
Venus 108.2 224.7
Earth 149.6 365.2
Mars 228.0 687.0
Jupiter 778.5 4331

NASA Planetary Fact Sheet, https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/ (page last updated 13 November 2026). Distance is in millions of kilometers. The Moon also appears on that sheet, with a 27.3-day period and a 0.384 million km distance, both marked with an asterisk: those figures are about the Moon and Earth, not a year around the Sun, so they are not mixed into this pattern.

The pattern we can honestly say. As the distance from the Sun gets larger, the orbital period gets longer. Mercury, closest in this list, has the shortest year here (88.0 days). Jupiter, farthest in this list, has the longest (4331 days). The numbers in between follow that same order.

What we will not do. We will not invent a formula that claims to give every period from the distance, and we will not invent an orbital speed. The table is already the sourced record. A homemade rule that “fits” two planets would not automatically be a NASA law.

Remember

A pattern is a rule you can say and use. In the NASA fact-sheet table, a larger distance from the Sun goes with a longer year. We will not invent a formula or an orbital speed. The Moon’s 27.3-day figure is about Earth, not a year around the Sun.

Watch me

Using the table, which planet is farther from the Sun, Venus or Mars, and which has the longer year?

  1. Mars is farther: 228.0 million km, against Venus at 108.2 million km.
  2. Mars has the longer year: 687.0 days, against Venus at 224.7 days.
  3. Both figures come from the fact sheet, not from a homemade formula.

Together

The rule “start at 2, add 6 each time.” What is the 4th number, and how can you check the 2nd?

Hint 1: The list

1st 2, 2nd 8, 3rd 14, 4th 20.

Hint 2: The check

The 2nd should be 2 + 6 = 8.

Check

Use the classroom rule or the fact-sheet table, as asked.

  1. In the table, how do Earth’s 149.6 million km and 365.2 days sit beside Mercury’s figures?
  2. Why will we not put the Moon’s 27.3 days in the same “year around the Sun” pattern?

Practice

  1. The rule “start at 10, add 4 each time.” What is the 5th number?
  2. Which planet in the table has both the smallest distance and the shortest period?
  3. A friend writes “period = distance × 3” from two rounded numbers, and calls it a NASA law. What would you say?
  4. Why will this lesson not invent an orbital speed for Mars?

Challenge problems: a rule with a letter, and an honest table

25 minutes · a short rule, then a careful look at the fact-sheet figures

Learn

These extend today’s pattern work. Classroom rules are invented and can be checked. NASA figures stay labeled as fact-sheet figures.

Remember

A letter can stand for the position in a rule. A sourced table is not a license to invent a formula.

Watch me

The rule is “the nth number is 3n + 1.” What are the first four numbers, and how do you check the 3rd?

  1. n = 1: 4; n = 2: 7; n = 3: 10; n = 4: 13.
  2. The 3rd is 3 × 3 + 1 = 10, which matches the list.
  3. The rule is invented for this exercise, and it can be checked on a term we have.

Together

Earth’s year is 365.2 days and Mars’s is 687.0 days, both from the fact sheet. About how many Earth years is a Mars year, and why is “exactly 2” a miss?

Hint 1: The division

687.0 ÷ 365.2 is a little less than 2, about 1.88.

Hint 2: The wording

“Exactly 2” would force a neater number than the sheet’s figures give.

Check

Stay with the rule or the sheet, as asked.

  1. The nth number is 5n. What is the 6th number, and how do you check the 2nd?
  2. Jupiter’s period is 4331 days on the sheet. About how many Earth years is that, using 365.2 days, and why might you leave it as “about 12” rather than a long string of figures?

Practice

  1. A friend wants to slip the Moon into the table’s “farther from the Sun, longer year” pattern, using 27.3 days and 0.384 million km. Why is that a miss?
  2. The rule “the nth number is 2n − 1.” Find the 8th number.
  3. Why is noticing the order in the table different from inventing a formula?

Math circle: a pattern is not automatically a law

20 minutes · a short argument, then a check of the reason

Learn

Why is noticing a pattern in a table not the same as inventing a formula and calling it a law?

Remember

A pattern we can point to in sourced figures is a fair description. A homemade rule that claims to give every value is a new claim, and it needs a source we do not have here.

Watch me

A friend sees Mercury at 57.9 and 88.0, and Venus at 108.2 and 224.7, and writes “if I double the distance, I should double the year.” What would you say?

  1. 108.2 is not double 57.9, and 224.7 is not double 88.0.
  2. Even if two numbers had happened to double, that would not, by itself, be a NASA law for every planet.
  3. The honest pattern remains: larger distance, longer year, as the fact-sheet figures show.

Together

How is that different from a classroom rule such as “the nth number is 3n + 1”?

Hint 1: The classroom rule

That rule is invented for the exercise, and we can check it on the list we made.

Hint 2: The planets

The fact sheet is a sourced table, not a classroom list we are free to refit.

Check

These check the reason.

  1. What pattern can we honestly state from the table?
  2. Why does the Moon’s 27.3-day figure not belong in that pattern?

Practice

  1. Give one reason this course will not invent an orbital-speed formula today.
  2. This argument is not a score or a badge.

Spanish · el planeta, el año, largo, and corto

15 minutes · Instruction in English; Hear for Spanish words · present tense only

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
el planeta el plah-NEH-tah the planet
el año el AH-nyoh the year
largo LAR-goh long
corto KOR-toh short

Today’s words name a planet, a year, long, and short. Instruction stays in English. más largo and más corto were used in Day 17 for lengths; today they can describe a year in a short picture sentence. NASA periods and distances stay in the English mathematics; do not say 687.0 or 228.0 in the Spanish.

Original sentences: Hay un planeta. El año es largo. El año es corto. El planeta está lejos.

For the guide: point to a simple drawn “nearer” and “farther” planet, without numbers. lejos is already known from Day 32. Present tense only.

Remember

el planeta; el año; largo; corto. Instruction in English. Hear for Spanish words. No NASA figures in the Spanish.

Watch me

Say: “There is a planet.”

  1. Hay un planeta.
  2. Hay introduces it. Un planeta is the planet.

Together

How do you say “The year is long”?

Hint 1: Year

el año

Hint 2: The sentence

El año es largo.

Check

These use only today’s phrases.

  1. Write: “The year is short.”
  2. Write: “The planet is far.”

Exercises

  1. Hear planeta, año, and largo, then use each.
  2. Why does the Spanish not say 687.0 days?
  3. A pointing retell: a planet, a long year, a short year.
  4. Copy one model sentence into the notebook.
  5. Correct: *La planeta, if someone used the wrong article.

Latin · annus and planēta

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin; original sentences. Instruction in English.

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
annus AHN-nus a year (nominative, masculine)
Annus longus est AHN-nus LON-gus est The year is long
planēta plah-NEH-tah a planet (nominative, masculine; a borrowed first-declension noun, like nauta)
Planēta procul est plah-NEH-tah PRO-kul est The planet is far

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Original sentences, not from a textbook: Annus longus est. Annus nōn longus est. Planēta procul est. Planēta nōn procul est.

annus is masculine, so longus matches. planēta is a borrowed first-declension noun, masculine in Latin (like nauta). Today’s second caption uses procul, which does not change. longus was first taught as a length word on Day 16; today it can describe a year. NASA figures stay in the English mathematics.

For the guide: the student reads as much as possible. Point to a simple “near” and “far” drawing, without numbers. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation.

Remember

annus; Annus longus est; planēta; Planēta procul est. Instruction in English. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation. No NASA figures in the Latin.

Watch me

Read: “The year is long.”

  1. Annus longus est.
  2. annus is the year. longus matches, because annus is masculine.

Together

How do you say “The planet is far”?

Hint 1: Far

procul

Hint 2: The sentence

Planēta procul est.

Check

These use only today’s captions.

  1. Write: “The year is not long.”
  2. Write: “The planet is not far.”

Exercises

  1. Why is *Annus longa est a miss?
  2. Hear annus and planēta, then use each.
  3. Why does the Latin not mention 4331 days?
  4. A pointing retell: a long year, a far planet.
  5. Copy one model sentence into the notebook.

Notebook: a short table, and a because-sentence

about 1 hour · a copied fact-sheet extract, then because / but / so

Learn

Copy two or three rows from the Day 38 fact-sheet table (with the source named), then write a because / but / so sentence about the pattern you can honestly state.

Remember

The table is sourced. The stems explain the habit: describe the order the figures show, and do not invent a formula.

Watch me

Start: I can say a farther planet has a longer year because ________.

  1. The because points at the fact-sheet figures, not at a homemade rule.
  2. A sound because: …the table shows larger distances with longer periods…

Together

Finish: I can say a farther planet has a longer year because ________, but I will not invent a NASA formula, so ________.

Hint 1: The but

…the sheet does not give a formula to copy…

Hint 2: The so

…I will describe the pattern and leave unsourced rules unclaimed.

Check

The page should show the copied rows and complete stems.

  1. Copy at least Earth and Mars from the table, with the units and the source.
  2. Write a three-stem explanation (because / but / so) about the pattern and a formula you will not invent.

Practice

  1. Tick the word-bank words you used: pattern, table, fact sheet, year, invented.
  2. Add a one-line note about why the Moon’s 27.3-day figure stays out.
  3. The page is a record, not a score.
  4. File the page. It is not a reward.
