Mental math warm-up: mixed, Level 7

10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it

Learn

This warm-up mixes the mental work of the level: a place-value shift, a rectangle perimeter, a rectangle area, and a liter conversion. Use the official relations, not a guess.

Remember

1 m = 100 cm; P = 2(L+W); A = L×W; 1 L = 1,000 cm³. Name the job and the unit.

Watch me

4 m in centimeters; perimeter of a 5 cm by 3 cm rectangle; area of the same rectangle; 2 L in cm³.

  1. 4 m = 400 cm.
  2. P = 16 cm.
  3. A = 15 cm².
  4. 2 L = 2,000 cm³.

Together

A 2 m by 2 m by 2 m cube: volume in m³ and in liters.

Hint 1: Cubes

2×2×2 = 8 m³.

Hint 2: Liters

8,000 L, because 1 m³ = 1,000 L.

Check

Name the job, then the number and the unit.

  1. 250 cm in meters.
  2. Area of a 6 cm by 4 cm rectangle, and the area of a right triangle with those legs.

Practice

  1. Perimeter of a 10 m by 1 m rectangle.
  2. 3,000 mm in meters.
  3. 0.5 L in cm³.
  4. A 3 cm cube: volume.
  5. Which two of today’s four core jobs (length, perimeter, area, volume) use square or cubic units?

Level 7 check

35–40 minutes · end-of-level mastery check · four out of five or better on each part; not a score or a reward

Learn

This sitting gathers the level: metric length as a place-value shift; perimeter as a walk around (rectangles and L-shapes); area as covering, including a right triangle as half a rectangle; volume of a cuboid; the liter as 1,000 cm³; and a habitat design problem whose box and tank numbers are invented, beside sourced NASA and BIPM figures.

The Together item is practice for layout. The Unit check below is done alone.

Remember

Name the job and the unit. Conversions are official or place-value shifts. Invented habitat numbers stay labeled. Four out of five or better on each part is mastery; this is not a score, a badge, or a reward.

Watch me

A 2 m by 3 m rectangle: name the perimeter and the area, with units, and say why the two numbers are different kinds of quantity.

  1. Perimeter: 2 × (2+3) = 10 m, a walk around.
  2. Area: 2 × 3 = 6 m², a covering.
  3. m and m² are different kinds of unit; the jobs are different.

Together

The Together item is practice for layout. After it, do the Unit check alone. What will you write beside each answer, besides the number?

Hint 1: The unit

cm, m, km, cm², m², cm³, m³, or L, as the job requires.

Hint 2: Invented or sourced

A tag when a habitat number is invented, or a name (NASA, BIPM) when a fact is sourced.

Check

The Together item is only layout practice. The Unit check is the mastery sitting.

  1. Write, as a heading in your book: Part A length, Part B perimeter and area, Part C volume and the habitat model. Then begin Part A when the guide says.
  2. When you finish a part, do not add extra credit items; the five questions are the part.

Unit check

Work alone. Use a sketch when it helps. For Mars, liter, and pressure facts, use the figures taught this unit (NASA or BIPM, with rounding stated). Invented habitat numbers stay labeled invented. Do not use a calculator.

Part A: length and metric shifts

  1. Write 3 km in meters, and 2.5 m in centimeters.
  2. 12 mm in centimeters, and 4 cm in millimeters.
  3. A tape is 1 m 15 cm. Write that length in centimeters.
  4. Mars’s equatorial diameter, as this course used the NASA fact sheet, is about 6,792 km (2 × 3,396.2). “About 6,800 km” is what kind of figure, if you use it?
  5. Earth’s equatorial diameter is about 12,756 km. Is “Mars is exactly half as wide as Earth” a little high, a little low, or exact? Use 6,792 km for Mars.

Part B: perimeter and area

  1. A 7 cm by 4 cm rectangle: find the perimeter and the area, with units.
  2. A 10 cm by 6 cm rectangle has a 3 cm by 3 cm square cut from one corner. Find the perimeter and the area.
  3. A right triangle has legs 8 cm and 5 cm. Find its area, and say why.
  4. A rectangle has area 36 m² and length 9 m. Find the width, then the perimeter.
  5. Why can you not say that 26 cm is “bigger than” 32 cm²?

Part C: volume, liters, and a labeled habitat model

  1. A 5 cm by 4 cm by 3 cm box: find the volume in cm³ and in liters.
  2. The invented module is 6 m by 3 m by 2.5 m. Find its volume in m³ and, as a filled-box conversion only, in liters.
  3. An invented tank is 240 L. Write that volume in m³. (1 m³ = 1,000 L.)
  4. What official relation makes 1 L equal to a 10 cm cube?
  5. Name one sourced fact from this unit (a NASA or BIPM figure) and one invented classroom number, and say why the invented number must stay labeled.

Four out of five or better on each part means this level is mastered. Any part below that is reviewed before Level 8. This is a mastery check, not a score, a badge, or a reward.

Challenge problems: a level wrap

20 minutes · after the check, or later if the check took the full sitting

Learn

These items mix the level, still with official relations and labeled invented plans. They are extra thinking, not extra credit and not a second score.

Remember

Same tools: place-value shifts, P = 2(L+W), A = L×W, half a rectangle, V = LWH, 1 L = 1,000 cm³. Invented plans stay labeled. No extra credit.

Watch me

An invented L-floor is a 6 m by 5 m rectangle with a 2 m by 2 m corner bite, and a 2.5 m wall height everywhere. Find the floor area, then the volume of the room (the missing corner is empty space, not built).

  1. Floor area: 6×5 − 2×2 = 26 m², invented.
  2. Volume: 26 × 2.5 = 65 m³, invented (the bite is not part of the room).
  3. If someone did 6×5×2.5 and forgot the bite, they would have 75 m³, which counts space that is not there.

Together

Write 3 m 40 cm in millimeters (1 m = 1,000 mm).

Hint 1: Meters first

3 m = 3,000 mm.

Hint 2: Centimeters

40 cm = 400 mm, because 1 cm = 10 mm, so 3,400 mm.

Check

These are wrap items, not a second mastery score.

  1. A right triangle with legs 6 m and 3 m is an invented annex. Find its area, then, if the “height” of the room over that floor is 2.5 m, the annex volume (a triangular prism: area of the triangle times the length along which it is extruded — here, the 2.5 m wall height, if the triangle is the floor).
  2. A 1.2 L bottle, official conversion: how many cm³? Is that bottle a sourced crew ration? (Say no, and say why.)

Practice

  1. Why is 65 m³ in Watch not a NASA volume?
  2. Order these from smallest to largest volume: 2 L, 500 cm³, 0.003 m³. (Use one unit.)
  3. Write one sentence that could head a design page: sourced facts listed, invented numbers listed.

Math circle: a last short argument

20 minutes · after the check, or later if the check took the full sitting

Learn

A last argument for the level: why we label. A number without a unit is unfinished (15 could be a length, an area, or a volume). A habitat number without invented or sourced is unfinished in a different way: it might be mistaken for a measurement of Mars. The argument is not that invented numbers are bad — they are how we practice — but that they must be named as what they are.

Remember

Write the unit. Tag invented classroom numbers. Tag sourced facts (NASA, BIPM) when you use them. That is part of the mathematics, not an extra flourish.

Watch me

A page says “the habitat is 45,000 L.” What two tags would make that sentence honest in this course?

  1. The 45,000 L is a filled-box conversion of an invented 45 m³ module, not a water ration.
  2. The 6 m by 3 m by 2.5 m box was invented for the classroom.
  3. With those tags, the conversion 1 m³ = 1,000 L can stay official and useful.

Together

Which of these needs a unit, which needs an invented tag, and which needs a source tag: 18, 6,792 km, 240 L in this unit’s tank problem?

Hint 1: 18

18 needs a unit and a job (18 m? 18 m²? 18 m³?).

Hint 2: The others

6,792 km is the NASA-based diameter (source tag). 240 L is the invented tank (invented tag).

Check

These are about honest labels, not new formulas.

  1. Write one unfinished number from this unit and finish it two different honest ways (different jobs).
  2. Why is “less than 1/100” an honest way to say 6.36 mb against the standard atmosphere 1,013.25 mb, and what would a fake precision look like?

Practice

  1. Give a one-sentence rule you want to keep into Level 8 (circles), about units or about invented models.

Spanish · A short habitat story

15 minutes · Instruction in English; a review of Days 16–19 words

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
¿Cuánto mide? KWAN-toh MEE-deh How long is it?
mide seis metros MEE-deh seys MEH-trohs it measures six meters
más largo que mahs LAR-goh keh longer than
hay agua eye AH-gwah there is water
necesitamos aire neh-seh-see-TAH-mohs EY-reh we need air

This is a review story. The words are from Days 16 to 19: measuring, más largo que, de largo / de ancho, hay agua, necesitamos.

For the guide: tell the story twice, pointing. The third time, pause for the student to fill a word. This is a check of the unit’s Spanish, not a test to pass or fail, and not a science claim. If the 6 m by 3 m measures appear, they are invented classroom numbers; say that in English.

The story. ¿Cuánto mide el cuarto? Tiene seis metros de largo y tres metros de ancho. Este cuarto es más largo que esa mesa. ¿Hay agua? Sí, hay agua. Necesitamos agua y aire.

In English, for the guide: How long is the room? It is six meters long and three meters wide. This room is longer than that table. Is there water? Yes, there is water. We need water and air.

Remember

¿Cuánto mide?; tiene … de largo / de ancho; más largo que; hay agua; necesitamos agua y aire. Instruction stays in English. Classroom measures, if used, are invented — say so in English.

Watch me

Read the story, then close it and retell one sentence: the room’s length and width.

  1. Tiene seis metros de largo y tres metros de ancho.
  2. Those six and three, if they are the habitat numbers, are invented — mention that in English if you use them.
  3. The Spanish is a present-tense description, not a NASA claim.

Together

Answer: ¿Hay agua?

Hint 1: The story

Sí, hay agua.

Hint 2: Or a new answer

No, no hay agua — also grammatical, if you change the story honestly.

Check

This is a check of the unit’s Spanish, not a test to pass or fail.

  1. Write the question ¿Cuánto mide el cuarto? and a de largo / de ancho answer.
  2. Write one sentence with más largo que, and one with necesitamos.

Exercises

  1. Write three sentences: a measure, a comparison, and hay or no hay.
  2. Hear mide, agua, and aire, then use each.
  3. Why does the guide mention “invented” in English beside the six and three?
  4. A silent pointing retell, then, if ready, a spoken retell of the story.
  5. Copy one model sentence into the notebook.

Latin · A short box-and-water story

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin; original review story. Instruction in English.

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
Cista longa est KIS-tah LONG-ah est The box is long
Cista alta est KIS-tah AL-tah est The box is tall
Aqua in cistā est AH-kwah in KIS-tah est Water is in the box
Murus altus est MOO-rus AL-tus est The wall is high
sed sed but

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

This is a review story. The words are from Days 16 to 19: longus, lātus, altus, brevis, cista, aqua, in cistā.

For the guide: the student reads as much as possible; you supply a word only after a real try. This is a check of the unit’s Latin, not a test to pass or fail. No modern liters in the Latin.

The story. Cista longa et lāta est, sed nōn alta. Ubi est aqua? Aqua in cistā est. Murus altus est. Linea brevis est.

In English, for the guide: The box is long and wide, but not tall. Where is the water? The water is in the box. The wall is high. The line is short.

Remember

Cista longa, lāta, nōn alta; aqua in cistā est; murus altus; linea brevis. Adjectives match. No SI units in the Latin. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation. This is a check of the unit, not a test to pass or fail.

Watch me

Read: Cista longa et lāta est, sed nōn alta. Why longa and alta, not *longus and *altus?

  1. cista is feminine.
  2. The adjectives match: longa, lāta, alta.
  3. sed nōn alta contrasts the height with the other two.

Together

Say: “Water is in the box.”

Hint 1: Place where

in cistā, ablative.

Hint 2: Sentence

Aqua in cistā est.

Check

This is a check of the unit’s Latin, not a test to pass or fail.

  1. Write: “The wall is high, but the line is short.”
  2. Write: “Water is not in the box.”

Exercises

  1. Correct: *murus alta est and *aqua in cista est (if the lesson wants cistā).
  2. Read the story aloud twice.
  3. Draw the box and the wall, labeled in Latin.
  4. Write one new sed-sentence.
  5. Why are meters and liters left in the English mathematics?

Notebook: a Level 7 page — a plan, a box, and a short explanation

60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · How the notebook works · Review sheet

Learn

This page gathers the level: a labeled invented floor plan (perimeter and area), a right triangle as half a rectangle, the invented 6 m by 3 m by 2.5 m module (45 m³, 45,000 L if filled — a conversion, not a ration), the official liter relations, Mars’s equatorial diameter about 6,792 km (NASA), and a because / but / so explanation that keeps sourced facts and invented numbers apart.

It is a record of what was mastered, not a decoration and not a score.

Word bank: meter, perimeter, area, volume, liter, invented, NASA, BIPM, rounded, unit, covering, walk around.

Remember

Draw and label first. Tag invented numbers and sourced facts. Complete stems. Four out of five on the check was the mastery rule; the page is not a second score. No points, badges, or extra credit — the page is the work.

Watch me

A model sentence: The module is 45 m³ because 6 × 3 × 2.5 is 45, but those three edges were invented, so 45,000 L is only a filled-box conversion, not a ration.

  1. V = 45 m³, classroom box.
  2. The but names the invention.
  3. The so blocks a misuse of 45,000 L.

Together

Finish: I can say Mars is about 6,792 km across, because ________, but if I say 6,800 km I must ________, so I will not write “exactly half of Earth” without ________.

Hint 1: The source

…the NASA equatorial radius 3,396.2 km, doubled.

Hint 2: The rounding and the comparison

…label 6,800 as rounded to the nearest hundred; …noting that 6,792 is a little more than half of about 12,756 (ratio about 0.532).

Check

The page should show the plan, the box, sourced and invented tags, and complete stems.

  1. Draw the floor plan and the module box, with perimeter, area, and volume, and tag invented or sourced on every number that needs it.
  2. Write a three-stem explanation (because / but / so) that keeps a liter conversion apart from a life-support claim.

Practice

  1. Add the official relations 1 L = 1,000 cm³ and 1 m³ = 1,000 L, tagged BIPM or official.
  2. Add Mars’s diameter, tagged NASA, and a labeled rounding if you use one.
  3. Tick the word-bank words you used.
  4. The page is a record of mastery work, not a badge or a second score.
  5. File the page with the unit check. Neither is a reward.
