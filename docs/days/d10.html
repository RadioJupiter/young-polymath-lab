Mental math warm-up: mixed

10 minutes

Learn

This warm-up mixes the mental strategies of the two units: adding and subtracting same places, double-and-halve, compensation, short sharing, and rounding when a table needs it.

Remember

×25: double-and-halve. 99 × n = 100 × n − n. Time: 1 hour = 60 minutes. Division is inverse multiplication.

Watch me

16 × 25, by double-and-halve.

  1. Halve 16 and double 25: 8 × 50 = 400.

99 × 7 = 100 × 7 − 7 = 693.

Together

4,800 ÷ 6. Split 4,800.

Hint 1: Parts

4,800 = 4,200 + 600, or think 48 ÷ 6 = 8, then put the two zeros back.

Hint 2: Answer

800.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. 16 × 25.
  2. 3 hours = how many minutes?

Practice

  1. 384,400 + 15,600
  2. 1,000,000 − 1
  3. 16 × 25
  4. 4,800 ÷ 6
  5. 99 × 7
  6. 3 hours = how many minutes?
  7. Round 142,984 to the nearest thousand.
  8. 72,000 ÷ 9

Unit check

45 minutes · alone, no calculator

Learn

This is a check of skills already taught in Units 1 and 2, not a new lesson. Use place-value discs or a chart, number bonds, rounding only when a table or model needs fewer places, comparison from the left, column methods, mental splits, and part-whole or comparison bars.

Remember

×10 moves every digit one place left. A number bond splits a whole into parts. Round by looking at the next place. The difference of a whole and a part is the other part.

Unit check

Work alone. Use the methods from these two units: place-value discs and charts, number bonds, rounding when a table or model needs it, comparison from the left, column methods, and bar models.

Part A: number

  1. Jupiter is about 2,867 million km from the Sun (rounded). Write this in kilometers. What is 10 times that distance?
  2. Show 1,632,450 on a place-value chart (or with discs). Write a number bond that has the hundred-thousands part as one piece.
  3. Round 49,528 to the nearest thousand. In a table that only needs the nearest ten thousand, what do you write? Are both answers the same? Why?
  4. Compare from the left: which is smaller, 51,118 or 49,528? Which is larger, 120,536 or 142,984? Then list all four from smallest to largest: 120,536; 51,118; 142,984; 49,528.
  5. What number is 100,000 less than 1,042,000? Think of taking away one hundred-thousand disc.

Part B: calculation

  1. 68,457 + 24,986
  2. 90,000 − 37,465
  3. 248 × 6
  4. 35 × 24 (show your area model)
  5. 952 ÷ 7

Part C: problems (draw a bar model for each)

  1. The Moon is about 384,400 km away. A spacecraft has 129,850 km still to go. How far has it travelled?
  2. A rover drives 125 m each day. How far does it drive in 4 weeks?
  3. Radio messages take 500 seconds to come from a probe. How many minutes and seconds is that?
  4. A planet's year lasts 225 Earth days (close to Venus's 224.7). How many full weeks is that, and how many days left over?
  5. Two moons have diameters that add up to 5,000 km. One is 1,200 km wider than the other. How wide is each?

Challenge problems

25 minutes

Learn

Distance ÷ speed (or ÷ rate) gives time. 384,400 km at 1,000 km/h is 384.4 hours; then convert hours to days (÷24) and leftover hours. For two three-digit numbers using 1–6 once each, the sum is largest when the biggest digits are in the hundreds places of both numbers. Light-time: distance = speed × time, with time in seconds.

Remember

Hours ÷ 24 gives days and leftover hours. Largest sum: biggest digits in the highest places of both addends. distance = 300,000 km/s × seconds.

Watch me

A craft travels 1,000 km every hour. How many whole days to travel 48,000 km, and how many hours left over?

  1. 48,000 ÷ 1,000 = 48 hours.
  2. 48 ÷ 24 = 2 days exactly, no leftover hours.

Together

1 hour 20 minutes at the speed of light (300,000 km/s). First change the time to seconds.

Hint 1: Seconds

1 hour = 3,600 s; 20 minutes = 1,200 s; total 4,800 s.

Hint 2: Distance

300,000 × 4,800 km — you will finish this in the problem.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. A trip of 50 hours: how many whole days and leftover hours?

Practice

  1. A spacecraft leaves Earth and travels 1,000 km every hour. How many whole days would it take to reach the Moon (384,400 km)? How many hours are left over in the last day?
  2. The digits 1 to 6 are each used once to make two three-digit numbers. What is the largest possible sum? The smallest?
  3. A probe sends a photo that takes 1 hour 20 minutes to arrive at the speed of light (300,000 km per second). About how far away is the probe?

Math circle: the broken chessboard

20 minutes · an invariant: something that never changes, whatever you do

Learn

A chessboard is coloured in alternating black and white squares. A domino covers two squares that share a side, so it always covers one black and one white. If you remove two squares of the same color, the remaining board has unequal colors and cannot be covered.

Remember

A domino covers one black and one white. Opposite corners of a chessboard are the same color.

Watch me

A 2 × 2 board, two black and two white. Remove two opposite corners (both the same color, say both black). Two white squares remain, and no blacks. One domino needs one of each, so two leftover whites cannot be covered.

Together

On a 4 × 4 board, how many black and how many white squares are there to start with? What color are two opposite corners?

Hint 1: 4 × 4

16 squares, 8 black and 8 white.

Hint 2: Opposite corners

The same color. After they are removed: 6 of that color and 8 of the other.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. Why can a single domino never cover two opposite corners?

Practice

Draw a 4 × 4 board of squares, coloured like a chessboard. Cross out two opposite corner squares. Can you cover the remaining 14 squares exactly with 7 dominoes, where each domino covers two squares side by side?

Try it. Then count the colors of the remaining squares, and explain what every domino always covers. Does the same argument work for a full 8 × 8 chessboard with opposite corners removed?

Spanish · Review story

15 minutes · Method: comprehensible input (Krashen) and Total Physical Response (Asher). Listen and act first; speak when ready. Instructions to the guide are in English.

Learn

This is a review story, not a new set of words. Read each line, point, and say it. The words you need are the ones from Days 1 to 9.

Word How to say it Meaning
es de noche es deh NOH-cheh it is night
está en el jardín es-TAH en el har-DEEN is in the garden
mira el cielo MEE-rah el SYEH-loh looks at the sky
ve la luna beh lah LOO-nah sees the moon
corre KOH-rreh runs
blanca y grande BLAHN-kah ee GRAHN-deh white and large

For the guide: tell the story twice, pointing. The third time, pause for the student to fill a word. End with a silent pointing walk-through, then, if ready, a spoken retell. This is a check of the two weeks, not a test to pass or fail.

The story.

  1. Es de noche.
  2. Ana está en el jardín.
  3. Ana mira el cielo.
  4. Ana ve la luna. La luna es blanca y grande.
  5. Un perro corre en el jardín.
  6. Ana dice: —¡Hola, luna!

Remember

Está for place; es for what something is like. Mira looks at; ve sees; corre runs.

Watch me

Line 2: Ana está en el jardín. — Ana is in the garden (place, so está). Line 4: La luna es blanca y grande. — what the moon is like (so es).

Together

What does Un perro corre en el jardín mean?

Hint 1: Words

Un perro a dog, corre runs, en el jardín in the garden.

Hint 2: Meaning

A dog runs in the garden.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. Why is it está in Ana está en el jardín, not es?
  2. What does ve mean?

Exercises

  1. Point through the story as the guide reads it out of order; put it back in order.
  2. Retell, in Spanish or with pointing and single words.
  3. Write (or copy) two sentences you can now read alone.

Latin · Review story

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin reading (in the spirit of Ørberg and of Minimus). All text here is original; those books are not copied.

Learn

This is a review story. The words are from Days 1 to 9.

Word How to say it Meaning
puer POO-er boy
puella poo-EL-lah girl
canis magnus KAH-niss MAG-nuss a large dog
in hortō in HOR-toh in the garden
currit KUR-rit runs
canem videt KAH-nem WID-et sees the dog
lūnam spectat LOO-nam SPEK-tat looks at the moon

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

For the guide: the student reads as much as possible; you supply a word only after a real try. Acting currit and spectā is a valid way to show meaning.

The story.

  1. Aulus est puer. Gaia est puella.
  2. Celer est canis magnus.
  3. Aulus et Gaia sunt in hortō.
  4. Celer currit. Aulus canem videt.
  5. Gaia lūnam spectat.
  6. Gaia dicit: —Salvē, lūna! Valē, Aule!

Remember

Videt and spectat both involve seeing; spectat is 'looks at / watches'. The thing seen uses an -em or -am ending here (canem, lūnam).

Watch me

Line 4: Celer currit. Aulus canem videt. Celer runs. Aulus sees the dog. Line 5: Gaia lūnam spectat. Gaia looks at the moon.

Together

What does Aulus et Gaia sunt in hortō mean? (Sunt means 'are', the plural of est.)

Hint 1: Words

Et and, sunt are, in hortō in the garden.

Hint 2: Meaning

Aulus and Gaia are in the garden.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What does currit mean?
  2. What does lūnam spectat mean?

Exercises

  1. Who is in the garden? What does Aulus see? What does Gaia look at?
  2. Read the story aloud.
  3. Copy two lines you can now read alone.

Notebook: design a mission

60 minutes, and as long as you like over the weekend · a short paragraph from an outline · How the notebook works · Review sheet

Learn

A mission page should show a spacecraft (two views), a destination, and at least one calculated fact you have already learned: a distance, a travel time at a stated speed, or a light-time (distance ÷ 300,000 km per second). Labels first, then a short because/but/so explanation of a design choice or a number on the page.

Remember

Draw two views and label them. Include one calculated fact from Units 1–2 (distance, travel time, or light-time). Then write a because/but/so sentence about it.

Watch me

Example fact: the Moon is 384,400 km away. Light-time is 384,400 ÷ 300,000 ≈ 1.3 s. A sentence: 'The crew can talk to Earth almost at once, because the light-time to the Moon is only about 1.3 seconds, but a crew on Mars would wait many minutes for a reply.'

Together

A probe is 150,000,000 km from Earth (about the Sun's distance). About how long does a radio message take, in minutes and seconds?

Hint 1: Seconds

150,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = 500 s.

Hint 2: Time

8 minutes 20 seconds — the same figure as sunlight to Earth.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What is the formula for light-time, and what speed do you use?

Practice

How the guide helps today: the design must be the student's. Help with looking up numbers already in this unit, and with the outline. As on Day 9, notes in the outline are not full sentences. If handwriting is tiring after the unit check, the guide may scribe the paragraph exactly as spoken, and the student copies or traces it.

Choose a destination: the Moon, Mars, Jupiter or a place of your choice. On a double page:

1. Draw your spacecraft from two views (side and front), with labels, the way Leonardo da Vinci drew machines.

2. Label the parts and the destination.

Word bank: spacecraft · destination · Moon · Mars · Jupiter · distance · light-time · travel · power · food · message · problem · question

3. Write. First an outline:

  • Topic sentence (in full): This mission goes to ________.
  • Notes: distance / light-time · one problem and an idea · a second problem and an idea
  • Closing sentence (in full): a question you would need an expert to answer, written as a sentence.

Then write a short paragraph from the outline (four to six sentences is plenty).

4. Numbers. Write the distance (from the unit's tables), the light-time for messages, and a travel time if your craft averages a speed you choose (show the division). List three problems the mission must solve and one idea for each, if they did not all fit in the paragraph.
