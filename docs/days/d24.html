Mental math warm-up: halves, and a diameter as a line of symmetry

10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it

Learn

A line of symmetry folds a figure onto itself. A diameter of a circle is one such line (in fact a circle has many). This warm-up also uses halves you already know. The construction in the core lesson will show how to draw a line of symmetry of a segment.

Remember

A diameter is a line of symmetry of a circle. Half of 180° is 90°. Half of a 10 cm segment is 5 cm on each side of the midpoint.

Watch me

A 12 cm segment is cut at its midpoint. How long is each part?

  1. The midpoint is halfway.
  2. 12 ÷ 2 = 6.
  3. Each part is 6 cm.

Together

A circle has a diameter of 10 cm. How many such diameters can you imagine through the center, and is that “one” or “many”?

Hint 1: The center

Every diameter goes through the center.

Hint 2: How many

Many: any line through the center meets the circle in a diameter. A circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry in this ideal model.

Check

These use halves and the idea of a line of symmetry.

  1. Half of a 90° angle.
  2. A 14 cm segment: length from one end to the midpoint.

Practice

  1. Why is a non-square rectangle’s longer midline a line of symmetry, but a diagonal of that same rectangle is not?

Core lesson: line symmetry, and the perpendicular bisector

35–40 minutes · Method: concrete (fold a drawing), pictorial (the marked triangle), then a compass-and-straightedge construction

Learn

A line of symmetry is a line that folds a figure onto itself, so that every point lands on a matching point. The pictured isosceles triangle has one line of symmetry: from the top vertex to the midpoint of the base. A regular hexagon has six lines of symmetry. A circle has a line of symmetry in every direction through the center (every diameter).

A real crater is not perfectly symmetric. The NASA figure “about 85 km” for Tycho is a diameter of a roughly circular feature. The ideal circle we draw has many lines of symmetry; the real rim does not, and we will say so.

The perpendicular bisector of a segment is the line that cuts the segment at its midpoint and meets it at a right angle. That line is the line of symmetry of the segment. It is also the set of points that are the same distance from both ends of the segment.

A construction (compass and straightedge). To draw the perpendicular bisector of a segment AB: 1. Open the compass to more than half of AB. 2. From A, draw an arc on both sides of AB. 3. From B, with the same opening, draw an arc on both sides, so the arcs cross at two points, call them P and Q. 4. The straight line through P and Q is the perpendicular bisector of AB. It meets AB at the midpoint M, at a right angle.

You should try this with a compass, not only look at a picture. The “same opening” matters: if the two openings differ, the crossings are not on the line of symmetry.

This is a first construction in this lab, not a full course in constructions. We do not yet construct a regular hexagon or a tangent.

Remember

A line of symmetry folds a figure onto itself. A circle has many (every diameter). The perpendicular bisector of AB is the line of symmetry of AB; it meets AB at its midpoint, at a right angle. Construction: same compass opening from each end; the line through the two crossings is the perpendicular bisector. A real crater is not perfectly symmetric.

Watch me

A segment is 10 cm long. Where does its perpendicular bisector meet the segment, and at what angle?

  1. It meets the segment at the midpoint.
  2. The midpoint is 5 cm from each end.
  3. The angle is a right angle, 90°.

Together

Why do the two compass openings have to be the same, in the construction?

Hint 1: Equal distances

Each crossing is meant to be the same distance from A as from B.

Hint 2: If they differ

If the openings differ, a crossing is closer to one end, and the line is not the bisector.

Check

These use only today’s symmetry and construction facts.

  1. How many lines of symmetry does the pictured isosceles triangle have, if only two sides are equal (the two long sides in the figure)?
  2. Name one line of symmetry of a circle, and say why “every diameter” is a fair answer.

Practice

  1. A non-square rectangle: how many lines of symmetry? (Not the diagonals.)
  2. Why is a diagonal of that rectangle not a line of symmetry?
  3. List the four construction steps in your own words, including “same opening.”
  4. Tycho is about 85 km across (NASA). Why should we not say “Tycho has infinitely many lines of symmetry” without a caution?
  5. Draw a segment, construct its perpendicular bisector (or show the two arc crossings), and mark the midpoint and the right angle.

Challenge problems: more symmetry, and a fold that fails

20–25 minutes · the same ideas, with a counter-example

Learn

These problems use line symmetry and the perpendicular bisector. A regular hexagon and a circle are rich in lines of symmetry; a scalene triangle (no equal sides) has none.

Remember

Line of symmetry: a fold onto itself. Perpendicular bisector: midpoint and 90°. Regular hexagon: six lines. Scalene triangle: none. A crater caution still applies.

Watch me

A regular hexagon: how many lines of symmetry, and how do they run?

  1. Six.
  2. Three through opposite vertices, and three through the midpoints of opposite sides.
  3. Each is a fold that lands the hexagon on itself, because all sides and all angles are equal.

Together

A scalene triangle has side lengths 5 cm, 6 cm, and 7 cm (invented). How many lines of symmetry, and why?

Hint 1: What a fold would need

A line of symmetry would have to match sides to equal sides.

Hint 2: The lengths

No two sides are equal, so no such fold. Zero lines of symmetry.

Check

These stay with today’s symmetry facts.

  1. The perpendicular bisector of a diameter of a circle: where does it meet the circle again, and what is that new segment?
  2. Why does folding a drawing of Tycho “in half” not prove the crater has a line of symmetry the way a drawn circle does?

Practice

  1. An isosceles triangle that is not equilateral: how many lines of symmetry?
  2. An equilateral triangle: how many?
  3. Write one sentence you want to keep: model symmetry versus a real crater.

Math circle: why a sideways throw can look like a curve

20 minutes · a first look at a curved path, not a new formula and not a full orbit calculation

Learn

The level map asks why orbits are curved. Here is an honest first look, not a derivation of Newton’s law and not a circumference problem in disguise. If you throw a ball sideways, it falls while it travels forward, so its path is curved (a short arc, in the classroom). If the ground itself curves away — as it does on a sphere — a faster sideways throw travels farther before meeting the ground. Newton’s thought, in outline, is that a fast enough sideways throw would keep missing the ground and go around. That is a reason an orbit can be a closed curve, not a claim that we have computed a satellite’s speed today, and not a claim that every orbit is a perfect circle (most are closer to ellipses, which this level does not compute).

Remember

A sideways throw plus a fall makes a curve. An orbit can be thought of as a throw that keeps missing a curved ground. We do not compute an orbital speed today. Orbits are not all perfect circles.

Watch me

Why is the path of a classroom sideways throw curved, even though “forward” and “down” can be talked about separately?

  1. The ball moves forward and, at the same time, falls.
  2. The two motions together are a curve, not a broken line of “along, then down.”
  3. This is the same idea as a short arc, not a new formula.

Together

What would you still have to say, if you used this idea to talk about a spacecraft?

Hint 1: Not a speed

We have not computed how fast it would have to go.

Hint 2: Not always a circle

A real orbit need not be a perfect circle, and this level does not compute ellipses.

Check

The point is the idea, not a satellite calculation.

  1. Why is “the orbit is a circumference C = πd” not a complete account of an orbit, even for a circular model?
  2. Name one thing this argument does not prove (a speed, a period, or that every orbit is a circle).

Practice

  1. Give one sentence you want to keep: a curve from two motions, not a new NASA speed.

igual ee-GWAHL equal; the same

el medio el MEH-dee-oh the middle

Latin · media linea, and equal sides

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin; original sentences. Instruction in English.

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
media linea MEH-dee-ah LIN-eh-ah a middle line
āequālis eye-KWAH-lis equal
Latera āequālia sunt LAH-teh-rah eye-KWAH-lee-ah sunt The sides are equal
Linea in mediō est LIN-eh-ah in MEH-dee-oh est The line is in the middle

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Original sentences: Media linea in mediō est. Latera āequālia sunt. Latera nōn āequālia sunt.

āequālia is the neuter plural, to match latera. No degree numbers in the Latin.

For the guide: Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation.

Remember

media linea; latera āequālia sunt; linea in mediō est. āequālia matches latera (neuter plural). No degrees in the Latin.

Watch me

Read: Latera āequālia sunt. Why *āequālia, not *āequālis?

  1. latera is neuter plural.
  2. The adjective matches: āequālia.
  3. The sentence says the sides are equal.

Together

Say: “The line is in the middle.”

Hint 1: Place where

in mediō, ablative.

Hint 2: The sentence

Linea in mediō est.

Check

These use only today’s patterns.

  1. Write: “The sides are not equal.”
  2. Write: “The middle line is in the middle.”

Exercises

  1. Correct: *latera āequālis sunt.
  2. Read the model sentences aloud.
  3. Draw a figure with a middle line, labeled in Latin.
  4. Why is the construction left in the English mathematics?
  5. Copy one model sentence.

Notebook: a fold, a construction, and a because-sentence

60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · How the notebook works · Review sheet

Learn

Draw the isosceles triangle with its line of symmetry, and construct (or carefully show) the perpendicular bisector of a segment, with the two arc crossings, the midpoint, and a right-angle mark. Then because / but / so: a circle has a line of symmetry through the center, but Tycho’s rim is not a perfect circle, so I will not give the crater “infinitely many lines of symmetry” without calling that a model.

Word bank: symmetry, fold, midpoint, perpendicular, bisector, compass, diameter, model, crater.

Remember

Draw and construct first. Complete stems. The construction needs a same compass opening. A crater caution belongs on the page. The page is not a badge.

Watch me

A model sentence: The line through the arc crossings is the perpendicular bisector because each crossing is the same distance from both ends, but that is a property of the construction, so I will not skip the “same opening” step.

  1. Equal compass openings make equal distances.
  2. The but names the reason.
  3. The so keeps the step.

Together

Finish: I can call a diameter a line of symmetry of a circle, because ________, but Tycho is only roughly circular, so ________.

Hint 1: The circle

…a fold through the center lands the ideal circle on itself.

Hint 2: The crater

…I will treat “infinitely many lines” as a property of the model, not of the surveyed rim.

Check

The page should show the fold, the construction, and complete stems.

  1. Draw the triangle with its dashed line of symmetry, and a constructed (or fully marked) perpendicular bisector.
  2. Write a three-stem explanation that keeps ideal circle symmetry apart from a real crater.

Practice

  1. List the four construction steps in short form.
  2. Tick the word-bank words you used.
  3. The page is not a score.
  4. A note that a scalene triangle has no line of symmetry, if you have space.
  5. File the page. It is not a reward.
