Mental math warm-up: counting through zero

10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it

Learn

Today we need numbers below zero. On a number line, 0 is the mark between the positives (to the right) and the negatives (to the left). Counting up from −3: −2, −1, 0, 1. The same count works for temperatures in degrees Celsius.

Remember

Numbers to the left of 0 are negative. −7 is less than −2, because it is farther left (colder, if they are Celsius temperatures). 0 is greater than every negative number on this line.

Watch me

Count up 5 from −3.

  1. Start at −3.
  2. One, two, three, four, five: −2, −1, 0, 1, 2.
  3. The landing point is 2.

Together

Which is colder (lesser), −4 or −9?

Hint 1: The line

Both sit to the left of 0. The one farther left is lesser.

Hint 2: Compare

−9 is farther left, so −9 is colder (lesser) than −4.

Check

These use only the number line through zero.

  1. Count down 4 from 1.
  2. Which is greater, −1 or −5?

Practice

  1. Count up 6 from −6.
  2. Name a number that sits between −2 and 1.

Core lesson: negative numbers, and planet temperatures

35–40 minutes · Method: concrete (a drawn thermometer or a marked floor line through 0), pictorial (the Celsius number line), then the sourced NASA figures

Learn

Negative numbers. On a number line, 0 sits in the middle of today’s work. Positive numbers are to the right of 0. Negative numbers are to the left of 0, and we write them with a minus sign: −5 is five below zero. −5 is less than −1, and both are less than 0. “Less than” for a Celsius temperature means colder.

Sourced mean temperatures. NASA’s Planetary Fact Sheet gives a mean temperature in degrees Celsius for each world. A mean here is an average over the whole surface (for the gas giants, at the one-bar level). The figures this course will use, from that table, are:

  • Mercury 167°C
  • Venus 464°C
  • Earth 15°C
  • the Moon −20°C
  • Mars −65°C

The pictured number line shows Mars’s mean, the Moon’s mean, 0, and Earth’s mean. Mercury 167 and Venus 464 sit far off the right-hand side of that drawing; they are still on the same scale, just much hotter.

A mean is not always “the temperature there.” The fact sheet’s own note says that for Mercury and the Moon, the mean averages the sunlit (very hot) and dark (very cold) sides, and is not representative of a given region. NASA Science pages give those swings honestly, as ranges, not as a second mean: Mercury’s sunlit surface can reach 430°C, and the night side can drop to −180°C. The Moon reaches about 127°C in full Sun and about −173°C in darkness. Mars can be as high as 20°C or as low as about −153°C. We will keep a mean and a range labeled as what they are. We will not invent a “typical Tuesday on Mars.”

Venus and the fact-sheet mean. NASA Science also describes Venus’s surface as about 467°C. The fact sheet’s mean is 464°C. Those are close, and they come from different NASA pages; we will use 464°C when we say “fact-sheet mean,” and we will not pretend the two figures are a measurement we made.

How much warmer? To find how many degrees warmer one temperature is than another, count the steps on the number line from the colder mark to the warmer mark. From Mars’s mean −65°C to Earth’s mean 15°C: it is 65 degrees up to 0, then 15 degrees more, so 80 degrees warmer. From the Moon’s mean −20°C to Earth 15°C: 20 + 15 = 35 degrees warmer. From Mercury’s mean 167°C to Venus’s mean 464°C: 464 − 167 = 297 degrees warmer. Venus’s mean is hotter than Mercury’s mean, even though Mercury is the closer planet to the Sun; the numbers are from the same fact sheet, and we will not invent a reason beyond what this lesson has taught.

No invented official temperatures. Classroom examples such as “a freezer at −18°C” must be labeled invented if we use them. The NASA figures above are sourced.

Remember

Negative numbers sit to the left of 0. Lesser is farther left (colder, in °C). Fact-sheet means: Mercury 167°C, Venus 464°C, Earth 15°C, Moon −20°C, Mars −65°C. A Mercury or Moon mean is not the temperature of a given region. Count up through 0 to find how many degrees warmer. Label means, ranges, and invented examples.

Watch me

How many degrees warmer is Earth’s mean (15°C) than Mars’s mean (−65°C)?

  1. Start at −65 and count up to 0: that is 65 degrees.
  2. Then count from 0 to 15: that is 15 degrees more.
  3. 65 + 15 = 80. Earth’s mean is 80 degrees warmer than Mars’s mean. Both figures are NASA fact-sheet means.

Together

How many degrees warmer is Earth’s mean (15°C) than the Moon’s mean (−20°C)?

Hint 1: Through zero

From −20 up to 0 is 20 degrees.

Hint 2: Then up to 15

20 + 15 = 35 degrees warmer. Both are fact-sheet means.

Check

These use the number line and the sourced figures as labeled.

  1. Which is colder, the Moon’s mean (−20°C) or Mars’s mean (−65°C)?
  2. How many degrees warmer is Venus’s mean (464°C) than Mercury’s mean (167°C)?

Practice

  1. Mark −65, −20, 0, and 15 on a Celsius number line, and label Mars, the Moon, and Earth as fact-sheet means.
  2. Which is greater, −180 or −65? (The first is a NASA Science night-side figure for Mercury; the second is Mars’s fact-sheet mean.) Say which is colder.
  3. Why is 167°C not “the temperature of a place on Mercury”?
  4. How many degrees is the swing from Mercury’s night side (−180°C) to a sunlit 430°C, as NASA Science gives those two figures?
  5. An invented freezer is at −18°C. How many degrees warmer is Earth’s mean (15°C) than that freezer? Label the freezer invented.

Challenge problems: ranges and means, and a freezer that is not a planet

20 minutes · after the core, or later if the core took the full sitting

Learn

These items mix means and ranges, and they keep each figure’s source labeled. An invented freezer stays invented. We still do not invent a daily weather report for Mars.

Remember

Count through 0 to find a difference. Keep means, ranges, and invented examples labeled.

Watch me

Mars can be as high as 20°C and as low as about −153°C (NASA Science). About how many degrees is that swing?

  1. From about −153 up to 0 is about 153 degrees.
  2. From 0 up to 20 is 20 degrees more.
  3. About 173 degrees (20 − (−153) = 173). The low is given as about −153°C, so the swing stays “about.”

Together

The Moon’s fact-sheet mean is −20°C. NASA Science gives about 127°C in full Sun and about −173°C in darkness. Why can all three figures be honest at once?

Hint 1: What a mean is

−20°C is an average over the whole surface, including hot and cold sides, and the fact sheet says it is not representative of a given region.

Hint 2: What a range is

127°C and −173°C are sunlit and dark extremes from a NASA Science page. They are a range, not a second mean.

Check

These stay with the number line and labeled sources.

  1. How many degrees warmer is Mercury’s mean (167°C) than Earth’s mean (15°C)?
  2. Which is colder, about −173°C (Moon, darkness, NASA Science) or −180°C (Mercury, night side, NASA Science)?

Practice

  1. Earth’s mean is 15°C; Mars can be as high as 20°C. Does that mean Mars is always warmer than Earth? Say why, using the mean and the range.
  2. How many degrees warmer is about 127°C (Moon, full Sun) than the Moon’s mean of −20°C?
  3. A friend says Venus is hotter than Mercury “because 467 is a bigger number than 430.” What two kinds of figure is that mixing, and what comparison did this lesson actually compute?
  4. An invented classroom thermometer reads −5°C, then later 8°C. How many degrees did it rise? Label the readings invented.
  5. Why will this course not write “Mars was −42.6°C at lunchtime” as a NASA fact from today?

Math circle: why a mean may not describe a place

20 minutes · a short reason, then a check

Learn

An average can be honest and still not be what you would feel if you stood in one place. NASA’s fact sheet says this in so many words for Mercury and the Moon: the mean averages the sunlit and dark sides, and is not representative of a given region. The same caution is why we keep a mean and a range labeled separately, and why we do not invent a daily temperature for Mars.

Remember

A sourced mean and a sourced range can both be honest. The mean is not automatically “the temperature there.” We do not invent a more precise official reading.

Watch me

Mercury’s fact-sheet mean is 167°C, but NASA Science gives 430°C on the sunlit surface and −180°C on the night side. How can 167 sit between those, and still not be “a typical place”?

  1. 167 is an average over the whole surface, including both extremes.
  2. A number between 430 and −180 can be the mean even if almost no region sits at 167°C at a given time.
  3. That is what the fact sheet’s note is warning: the mean is not representative of a given region.

Together

Why is writing “Mercury is 167.00°C” not a more careful NASA measurement from us?

Hint 1: The source

167°C is the fact-sheet mean, a whole number in that table as we used it.

Hint 2: The zeros

Two extra zeros would pretend to a hundredth of a degree we did not measure, and they would still not make 167°C into the temperature of a given place.

Check

These check the mean-versus-range reason.

  1. Name one world whose fact-sheet mean is negative.
  2. Why does this lesson refuse a “typical Tuesday on Mars” temperature?

Practice

  1. Is Earth’s mean of 15°C a promise that every place on Earth is 15°C? Use the fact sheet’s general caution about variation.
  2. Venus’s fact-sheet mean is 464°C, and a NASA Science page says the surface is about 467°C. What do we do with the two figures?
  3. Finish: 167°C is a sourced mean, but it averages very hot and very cold sides, so I will not say ____.
  4. Which comparison is fairer as a mean-to-mean fact: Venus 464 against Mercury 167, or Venus “about 467” against Mercury’s sunlit 430? Say why.
  5. What job does the minus sign do in −65°C?

Spanish · bajo cero, el hielo, and hace mucho frío

15 minutes · Instruction in English; Hear for Spanish words · present tense only

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
bajo cero BAH-hoh SEH-roh below zero
el hielo el YEH-loh the ice
está helado es-TAH eh-LAH-doh it is frozen / icy
hace mucho frío AH-seh MOO-choh FREE-oh it is very cold (you know hace frío from Day 12)
hace mucho calor AH-seh MOO-choh kah-LOR it is very hot

Today’s words name ice, a frozen thing, and weather that is very cold or very hot, including the phrase bajo cero (below zero). You already know hace frío and hace calor from Day 12. Instruction stays in English. Celsius numbers, negative numbers, and the NASA figures stay in the English mathematics.

Original sentences: Hace mucho frío. Hace mucho calor. Está helado. Hay hielo. Está bajo cero.

For the guide: a picture of ice, or a window on a cold day, is enough. Present tense only. Do not put −65 or 464 into the Spanish sentences.

Remember

bajo cero; el hielo; está helado; hace mucho frío; hace mucho calor. Instruction in English. Hear for Spanish words. No Celsius numbers in the Spanish.

Watch me

Say: “It is very cold.”

  1. Hace mucho frío.
  2. Hace frío was Day 12; mucho makes it “very.”
  3. This is a present-tense weather pattern, not a NASA figure.

Together

How do you say “It is below zero”?

Hint 1: The phrase

bajo cero, with está

Hint 2: The sentence

Está bajo cero.

Check

These use only today’s phrases.

  1. Write: “There is ice.”
  2. Write: “It is very hot.”

Exercises

  1. Correct: *Hace helado, if someone used hace with helado.
  2. Hear hielo, bajo cero, and calor, then use each.
  3. Why is está helado closer to está nublado (Day 12) than to hace frío?
  4. A pointing retell: ice, frozen, very cold, very hot, below zero — still without a number.
  5. Copy one model sentence into the notebook.

Latin · aqua frīgida and aqua calida

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin; original sentences. Instruction in English.

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
aqua AH-kwah water (nominative, feminine)
frīgida FREE-gih-dah cold (feminine, to match aqua)
calida KAH-lih-dah warm, hot (feminine)
Aqua frīgida est AH-kwah FREE-gih-dah est The water is cold

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Original sentences, not from a textbook: Aqua frīgida est. Aqua calida est. Aqua nōn frīgida est. Aqua nōn calida est.

aqua is feminine, so frīgida and calida match. Celsius numbers, minus signs, and the NASA figures stay in the English mathematics. No degree numbers and no SI units in the Latin.

For the guide: the student reads as much as possible. A cup of cold water and a cup of warm water, or a picture, is enough. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation.

Remember

aqua; Aqua frīgida est; Aqua calida est. Adjectives match (feminine). No degrees in the Latin. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation.

Watch me

Read: Aqua frīgida est. Why frīgida, not *frīgidus?

  1. aqua is feminine.
  2. The adjective matches: frīgida.
  3. The sentence says the water is cold, not that a masculine noun is cold.

Together

Say: “The water is warm.”

Hint 1: The noun

aqua, feminine.

Hint 2: The sentence

Aqua calida est.

Check

These use only today’s patterns.

  1. Write: “The water is not cold.”
  2. Write: “The water is not warm.”

Exercises

  1. Correct: *aqua frīgidus est and *aqua calidus est.
  2. Read the four model sentences aloud.
  3. Draw cold water and warm water, labeled in Latin.
  4. Why are −65°C and 15°C left in the English mathematics?
  5. Copy one model sentence.

Notebook: a temperature line, two means, and a because-sentence

60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · How the notebook works · Review sheet

Learn

Draw a Celsius number line through 0 and mark the fact-sheet means for Mars (−65°C), the Moon (−20°C), and Earth (15°C). Note, off to the right, that Mercury 167°C and Venus 464°C sit much farther along the same scale. Then complete because / but / so stems: Earth’s mean is 80 degrees warmer than Mars’s mean because it is 65 degrees up to 0 and 15 degrees more, but a mean is not automatically the temperature of a given place, so I will keep means and ranges labeled, and I will not invent a daily report for Mars.

Remember

Draw and label first. Complete stems. Count through 0. Label means, ranges, and invented examples. No points or badges — the page is the work.

Watch me

A model sentence: Mercury’s mean is 167°C because that is the NASA fact-sheet figure, but the sheet says that mean is not representative of a given region, so I will not call 167°C the temperature of a place on Mercury.

  1. 167°C is the sourced mean.
  2. The but is the fact sheet’s own note.
  3. The so refuses a “typical place” claim.

Together

Finish: Earth is 35 degrees warmer than the Moon’s mean, because ________, but −20°C is a mean, so ________.

Hint 1: The count

…it is 20 degrees up to 0 and 15 degrees more…

Hint 2: The so

…I will also remember the NASA Science range (about 127°C in full Sun, about −173°C in darkness), and I will not treat −20°C as “the temperature on the Moon.”

Check

The page should show the line, the labels, and complete stems.

  1. Draw the Celsius line with −65, −20, 0, and 15 marked and labeled as fact-sheet means (except 0).
  2. Write a three-stem explanation (because / but / so) that keeps a mean apart from a place-temperature, or a range.

Practice

  1. Add the 80-degree count from −65 to 15, in two jumps through 0.
  2. Tick the word-bank words you used: negative, mean, range, Celsius, fact sheet, invented, warmer.
  3. The page is a record of today’s skill, not a badge.
  4. A note that a freezer at −18°C, if you include one, is invented.
  5. File the page. It is not a reward.
