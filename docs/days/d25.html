Mental math warm-up: mixed, Level 8

10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it

Learn

This warm-up mixes the mental work of the level: a straight-line angle, a third angle in a triangle, doubling a radius, and 3.14 × 10. Use the facts as taught, with 3.14 labeled if you use it.

Remember

Straight line 180°. Triangle 180°. d = 2r. 3.14 × 10 = 31.4, and 3.14 is an approximation of π when it stands for π.

Watch me

A triangle has angles 90° and 55°. What is the third angle?

  1. The three add to 180°.
  2. 90 + 55 = 145.
  3. 180 − 145 = 35°.

Together

A circle has radius 4 cm. What is the diameter, and, using π ≈ 3.14, what is a first circumference (if you are ready)?

Hint 1: Diameter

d = 8 cm.

Hint 2: Circumference

C ≈ 3.14 × 8 = 25.12 cm, with 3.14 labeled.

Check

These mix the level’s facts.

  1. An angle of 62° on a straight line: the other angle.
  2. 3.14 × 100.

Practice

  1. How many right angles make a full turn?
  2. A regular hexagon’s interior angle, if you remember 720° ÷ 6.

Level 8 check

35–40 minutes · end-of-level mastery check · four out of five or better on each part; not a score or a reward

Learn

This sitting gathers the level: angles as turns (straight line, around a point, vertically opposite); polygons and the triangle sum, including (n − 2) × 180°; radius, diameter, and circumference with a labeled approximation of π; a sourced NASA diameter given a circumference only with π and rounding named; line symmetry; and the perpendicular bisector construction. A crater circumference or a crater’s “lines of symmetry” stays a model, not a survey.

The Together item is practice for layout. The Unit check below is done alone.

Remember

Name the job and the unit (° for a turn, cm or km for a length). Label every approximation of π and every rounding. Invented classroom shapes stay labeled. Four out of five or better on each part is mastery; this is not a score, a badge, or a reward.

Watch me

A circle has diameter 10 cm. Using π ≈ 3.14, find C, and say what must be labeled.

  1. C ≈ 3.14 × 10 = 31.4 cm.
  2. 3.14 is an approximation of π and must be labeled.
  3. The unit is cm, a length (the distance around), not a number of degrees.

Together

The Together item is practice for layout. After it, do the Unit check alone. What will you write beside a circumference, besides the number?

Hint 1: The unit

cm, m, or km, as the job requires — a length, the distance around.

Hint 2: The labels

The approximation of π you used, and any rounding, and a “model” tag if the figure is a crater rim.

Check

The Together item is only layout practice. The Unit check is the mastery sitting.

  1. Write, as a heading in your book: Part A angles and polygons, Part B circles and circumference, Part C symmetry and a construction. Then begin Part A when the guide says.
  2. When you finish a part, do not add extra credit items; the five questions are the part.

Unit check

Work alone. Use a sketch when it helps. For Mars, Earth, and Tycho, use the figures taught this unit (NASA, with π and rounding stated). Invented classroom shapes stay labeled invented. Do not use a calculator.

Part A: angles and polygons

  1. An angle of 47° sits on a straight line with another angle. Find the other angle, and name both (acute or obtuse).
  2. Two lines cross. One angle is 110°. Find the other three.
  3. A triangle has angles 25° and 90°. Find the third.
  4. A quadrilateral has angles 70°, 80°, 95°, and one missing. Find the missing angle.
  5. A regular hexagon: what is each interior angle? (Show the sum first.)

Part B: circles and circumference

  1. A circle has radius 6 cm. Find the diameter.
  2. A circle has diameter 10 cm. Using π ≈ 3.14, find the circumference, and label 3.14.
  3. A circle has diameter 14 cm. Using π ≈ 22/7, find the circumference, and label 22/7.
  4. Mars’s equatorial diameter, as this course used the NASA fact sheet, is about 6,792 km. Using π ≈ 3.14, what circumference does this course write, and what must be labeled?
  5. Why is 3.14 not the same thing as π?

Part C: symmetry, a construction, and a model

  1. How many lines of symmetry does a non-square rectangle have, and where do they run?
  2. How many lines of symmetry does a scalene triangle have, and why?
  3. List the four steps of the perpendicular-bisector construction, including “same opening.”
  4. Tycho is about 85 km across (NASA). If we model the rim as a circle and use π ≈ 3.14, what circumference do we get, and what must the sentence still say?
  5. Name one sourced fact from this unit (a NASA figure) and one invented classroom number, and say why the invented number must stay labeled.

Four out of five or better on each part means this level is mastered. Any part below that is reviewed before Level 9. This is a mastery check, not a score, a badge, or a reward.

Challenge problems: a level wrap

20 minutes · after the check, or later if the check took the full sitting

Learn

These items mix the level, still with labeled π and labeled invented plans. They are extra thinking, not extra credit and not a second score.

Remember

Same tools: angle sums, C = πd with a labeled π, symmetry, the perpendicular bisector. Invented plans stay labeled. No extra credit.

Watch me

An invented regular octagonal pad: find each interior angle. A circular invented tank lid next to it has diameter 7 cm; using 22/7, find C. Label both invented.

  1. Octagon sum (8 − 2) × 180° = 1,080°; each regular interior angle is 135°.
  2. C ≈ (22/7) × 7 = 22 cm, invented lid.
  3. Both are classroom inventions, not NASA hardware.

Together

Using π ≈ 3.14, a circle has circumference 62.8 m. Find the diameter, then the radius.

Hint 1: Backwards

d ≈ 62.8 ÷ 3.14.

Hint 2: The numbers

62.8 ÷ 3.14 = 20, so d = 20 m and r = 10 m.

Check

These are wrap items, not a second mastery score.

  1. Two lines cross. One angle is twice the adjacent angle. Find all four angles.
  2. Why is “Earth’s equator is exactly 3.14 × 12,756 km” a little too sharp?

Practice

  1. A sentence that could head a design page: sourced NASA figures listed, invented shapes listed, π labeled.
  2. Name one fact about orbits from the math circle that this check did not require you to compute.
  3. Construct, or clearly mark, a perpendicular bisector, as a wrap of the construction (not as extra credit).

Math circle: a last short argument

20 minutes · after the check, or later if the check took the full sitting

Learn

A last argument for the level: why we label π. A circumference computed with 3.14 is a number we may use, but the number is only as honest as the label. The same is true of a rounding (about 21,300 km) and of a crater model (about 267 km). The argument is not that approximations are bad — they are how we compute at this level — but that they must be named as what they are.

Remember

Label the approximation of π. Label a rounding. Label a crater model. That is part of the mathematics, not an extra flourish.

Watch me

A page says “Mars is 21,326.88 km around.” What two tags would make that sentence honest in this course?

  1. The 21,326.88 km is 3.14 × 6,792, and 3.14 is an approximation of π.
  2. This course prefers “about 21,300 km,” rounded to the nearest hundred, also labeled.
  3. With those tags, the NASA diameter can stay, and the circumference is not a false exact equator.

Together

Which of these needs a unit, which needs a π label, and which needs a model tag: 120, 31.4 cm, 267 km as a Tycho walk?

Hint 1: 120

120 needs a unit and a job (120°? 120 cm?).

Hint 2: The others

31.4 cm needs the π ≈ 3.14 label if it is a circumference of a 10 cm circle. 267 km as a Tycho walk needs a circle-model tag, and should not be called a surveyed walk.

Check

These are about honest labels, not new formulas.

  1. Write one unfinished number from this unit and finish it two honest ways (different jobs, or a length versus a turn).
  2. Why is “about 40,000 km” an honest rounding for Earth’s equator in this course, and what would a fake exactness look like?

Practice

  1. Give a one-sentence rule you want to keep into Level 9, about π or about models.

Spanish · A short circle-and-turn story

15 minutes · Instruction in English; a review of Days 21–24 words

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
gira a la derecha HEE-rah ah lah deh-REH-chah turns to the right
un triángulo oon tree-AHNG-goo-loh a triangle
un círculo oon SEER-koo-loh a circle
el radio el RAH-dee-oh the radius
se ve igual seh veh ee-GWAHL it looks the same

This is a review story. The words are from Days 21 to 24: a turn, a triangle, a circle, the radius, se ve igual.

For the guide: tell the story twice, pointing. The third time, pause for the student to fill a word. This is a check of the unit’s Spanish, not a test to pass or fail, and not a science claim. If a circumference or a NASA figure appears, keep it in English.

The story. Hay un triángulo y un círculo. El triángulo tiene tres lados. El radio va del centro al círculo. Gira a la derecha. Hay una línea en el medio: se ve igual en los dos lados.

In English, for the guide: There is a triangle and a circle. The triangle has three sides. The radius goes from the center to the circle. It turns to the right. There is a line in the middle: it looks the same on both sides.

Remember

un triángulo; un círculo; el radio; gira a la derecha; se ve igual; una línea en el medio. Instruction stays in English. Measures, if used, stay in English. This is a check of the unit’s Spanish, not a test to pass or fail.

Watch me

Read the story, then close it and retell one sentence: the radius.

  1. El radio va del centro al círculo.
  2. Point to the drawing.
  3. No kilometers in the Spanish.

Together

Answer, from the story: ¿Hay un triángulo?

Hint 1: The story

Sí, hay un triángulo.

Hint 2: And a circle

También hay un círculo.

Check

This is a check of the unit’s Spanish, not a test to pass or fail.

  1. Write the sentence about three sides, and the sentence about se ve igual.
  2. Write one sentence with gira a la derecha or a la izquierda.

Exercises

  1. Write three sentences: a shape, a turn, and se ve igual.
  2. Hear radio, círculo, and lado (or lados), then use each.
  3. Why does the guide keep 3.14 and 6,792 km in English?
  4. A silent pointing retell, then, if ready, a spoken retell of the story.
  5. Copy one model sentence into the notebook.

Latin · A short circle-and-line story

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin; original review story. Instruction in English.

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
Angulus rēctus est ANG-goo-lus RAYK-tus est The angle is right
Triāngulum tria latera habet tree-AHNG-goo-lum TREE-ah LAH-teh-rah HAH-bet The triangle has three sides
Circulus rotundus est KIR-koo-lus roh-TUN-dus est The circle is round
Latera āequālia sunt LAH-teh-rah eye-KWAH-lee-ah sunt The sides are equal
sed sed but

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

This is a review story. The words are from Days 21 to 24: angulus, triāngulum, latera, circulus, āequālia.

For the guide: the student reads as much as possible; you supply a word only after a real try. This is a check of the unit’s Latin, not a test to pass or fail. No modern π or kilometers in the Latin. Keep latus (side) apart from lātus (wide).

The story. Triāngulum tria latera habet. Latera nōn āequālia sunt, sed angulus rēctus est. Circulus rotundus est. Centrum in circulō est.

In English, for the guide: The triangle has three sides. The sides are not equal, but there is a right angle. The circle is round. The center is in the circle.

Remember

triāngulum tria latera habet; latera āequālia; angulus rēctus; circulus rotundus; centrum in circulō. No SI units and no π in the Latin. This is a check of the unit, not a test to pass or fail.

Watch me

Read: Latera nōn āequālia sunt, sed angulus rēctus est. What does sed do here?

  1. It contrasts the sides (not equal) with the right angle.
  2. The triangle can have a right angle without being equilateral.
  3. āequālia matches latera.

Together

Say: “The circle is round.”

Hint 1: The noun

circulus, masculine.

Hint 2: The sentence

Circulus rotundus est.

Check

This is a check of the unit’s Latin, not a test to pass or fail.

  1. Write: “The triangle has three sides.”
  2. Write: “The center is in the circle.”

Exercises

  1. Correct: *latera āequālis sunt and *centrum in circulus est.
  2. Read the story aloud twice.
  3. Draw the triangle and the circle, labeled in Latin.
  4. Write one new sed-sentence.
  5. Why are degrees and kilometers left in the English mathematics?

Notebook: a Level 8 page — a turn, a circle, and a short explanation

60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · How the notebook works · Review sheet

Learn

This page gathers the level: a labeled angle diagram (straight line or a triangle), a circle with r, d, and C = πd, Mars’s circumference about 21,300 km (π ≈ 3.14, rounded, NASA diameter), the Tycho model if you include it, and a because / but / so that keeps approximations and models labeled.

It is a record of what was mastered, not a decoration and not a score.

Word bank: angle, polygon, radius, diameter, circumference, pi, approximation, symmetry, perpendicular bisector, NASA, model, rounded.

Remember

Draw and label first. Tag approximations, roundings, and models. Complete stems. Four out of five on the check was the mastery rule; the page is not a second score.

Watch me

A model sentence: I can write about 21,300 km for Mars because 3.14 × 6,792 is 21,326.88, but 3.14 is not π itself, so I will label the approximation and the rounding.

  1. The multiplication is shown.
  2. The but names the approximation.
  3. The so keeps the labels.

Together

Finish: A crater can have a model circumference, because ________, but the rim is not a perfect circle, so ________.

Hint 1: The model

…we may take NASA’s about 85 km for Tycho as a diameter in a circle model, and use C = πd.

Hint 2: The limit

…I will not call that circumference a surveyed walk around the rim.

Check

The page should show the diagrams, the labels, and complete stems.

  1. Draw an angle or a polygon from the level, and a marked circle with a worked C = πd, with π labeled.
  2. Write a three-stem explanation (because / but / so) that keeps a NASA diameter, an approximation of π, and a rounding apart from a fake exact equator.

Practice

  1. Add the perpendicular bisector, marked, or the isosceles line of symmetry.
  2. Add Tycho as a model, or a clear reason for leaving it off the page.
  3. Tick the word-bank words you used.
  4. The page is a record of mastery work, not a badge or a second score.
  5. File the page with the unit check. Neither is a reward.
