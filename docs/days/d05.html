Unit 1 · Day 5 · Walking the Solar System

Today's big question: If the Sun were a ball you could hold, where would the planets be? Today you build a true scale model, and you check what you have mastered this week.

For the guide: the model walk needs a long straight path: a park, a quiet road with a footpath, or a beach. The inner planets fit in a garden; the outer planets need hundreds of meters. If space is short, walk as far as Jupiter and mark the rest on a map. The unit check below is short and is done without help; anything missed is reviewed on Monday before new work.

Mental math warm-up: doubles and halves

10 minutes

Learn

Double is ×2; half is ÷2. For a number in several places, double or halve each place, then trade if a place makes ten or more (or if a place is odd when you halve, split a one into ten of the next smaller place).

Remember

Double place by place. Half of an even digit is easy; if a digit is odd, split 1 of it into 10 of the next place.

Watch me

Double 384.

  1. Double 300 is 600; double 80 is 160; double 4 is 8.
  2. 600 + 160 + 8 = 768.

Together

Half of 1,500.

Hint 1: Split

Half of 1,000 is 500; half of 500 is 250.

Hint 2: Answer

750.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. Double 2,250.
  2. Half of 778.

Practice

  1. Double 384
  2. Half of 1,500
  3. Double 2,250
  4. Half of 778
  5. Double 6,792
  6. Half of 12,756
  7. Double 45, then double again
  8. Half of 1,000, then half again

Core lesson: scale, and the model walk

45 minutes (longer if you walk the whole model)

Learn

What "to scale" means. In a scale model, every length is divided by the same number. Here the Sun is a ball about 20 cm across, and 1 cm in the model stands for 70,000 km in space. (The Sun is 1,391,400 km across; 1,391,400 ÷ 70,000 is just under 20.)

Using NASA's values, the model is:

Body Model size (across) Model distance from the Sun Something that size
Sun 20 cm — a large melon or a ball
Mercury 0.7 mm 8.3 m a grain of salt
Venus 1.7 mm 15.5 m a peppercorn
Earth 1.8 mm 21.4 m a peppercorn
Mars 1.0 mm 32.6 m a pinhead
Jupiter 20 mm 111 m a large grape or a marble
Saturn 17 mm 205 m a slightly smaller marble
Uranus 7.3 mm 410 m a small bead
Neptune 7.1 mm 645 m about the same as Uranus

The Moon would be a speck 0.5 mm across, 5.5 cm from the Earth peppercorn.

The walk. First measure your own pace: walk 10 normal paces and measure the distance with a tape; divide by 10. Then work out how many paces to each planet. Place the Sun, and walk out, leaving a marker (a card with the planet's name) at each planet.

Remember

A scale keeps every ratio the same. If 1 cm stands for 70,000 km, a real length L km becomes L ÷ 70,000 centimeters. Distances in the model subtract just as they do in space.

Watch me

Earth is 150,000,000 km from the Sun. The scale is 1 cm to 70,000 km.

  1. 150,000,000 ÷ 70,000 = 2,142.8… cm, which is about 21.4 m (as in the table).
  2. The same scale applied to Mars (228,000,000 km) gives about 32.6 m.
  3. Lined up on the same side of the Sun, Earth to Mars in the model is 32.6 − 21.4 = 11.2 m.

Together

Jupiter is 20 cm wide in the model; Earth is 1.8 cm wide. How many times wider is Jupiter in the model?

Hint 1: Divide

20 ÷ 1.8 is a little more than 11.

Hint 2: Scale

The real worlds have the same ratio, because every length was divided by the same number.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. If 1 cm stands for 70,000 km, what real distance is 10 cm in the model?
  2. In the model, Earth is at 21.4 m and Jupiter at 111 m from the Sun. How far is it from Earth to Jupiter?

Practice

  1. In the model, how far is it from Earth to Mars (when they are lined up on the same side of the Sun)?
  2. How many times wider than Earth is Jupiter in the model? Is that the same as in space?
  3. If your pace is 50 cm, how many paces is it from the Sun to Earth in the model? To Jupiter?

Unit check (do alone, 15 minutes)

  1. Saturn is about 1,432 million km from the Sun. Write this in kilometers. Then write a number bond: 1,432,000,000 = 1,000,000,000 + ?.
  2. Round 778,500 to the nearest ten thousand.
  3. 47,806 + 38,597
  4. 100,000 − 61,438
  5. A spacecraft must travel 384,400 km. It has gone 196,750 km. Draw the bar model and find how far it still has to go.

Challenge problems

25 minutes

Learn

When a scale is 1 cm to 70,000 km, a huge real distance becomes a model length of (real km) ÷ 70,000 centimeters, then convert to meters or kilometers if needed. Time to walk a model distance is distance ÷ speed. Light-time is distance ÷ 300,000 km per second.

Remember

model cm = real km ÷ 70,000. Time = distance ÷ speed. Keep the units matching.

Watch me

Neptune is 645 m from the Sun in the model. How long to walk that at 1 meter per second?

  1. Time = 645 s.
  2. 645 = 10 × 60 + 45, so 10 minutes 45 seconds.

Together

Light takes 8 min 20 s to come from the Sun. How many seconds is that?

Hint 1: Minutes to seconds

8 × 60 = 480.

Hint 2: Add

480 + 20 = 500 s.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. At 1 m/s, how many seconds to walk 120 m of the model?

Practice

  1. The nearest star beyond the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about 40 trillion km away (40,000,000,000,000 km). In our model (1 cm for 70,000 km), about how far away would it be? Give your answer in kilometers.
  2. In the model, Neptune is 645 m from the Sun. How long would it take to walk there at 1 meter per second? Give the answer in minutes and seconds.
  3. Light takes about 8 minutes 20 seconds to reach Earth from the Sun. In the model, Earth is 21.4 m away. How fast does "model light" move, in centimeters per second?

Math circle: the heavy meteorite

20 minutes

Learn

A balance scale tells you which side is heavier, or that they balance. The method is to split the group into three equal piles: weigh one pile against another. If one side is heavy, the heavy piece is in that pile. If they balance, the heavy piece is in the pile you left out. Each weighing cuts the search to one third.

Remember

If you already know the odd piece is heavier, n weighings can handle 3^n pieces. Two weighings handle 9 pieces.

Watch me

Three pieces, one heavy, one weighing. Weigh two of them. If one side goes down, that one is heavy. If they balance, the one left out is heavy.

Together

Nine pieces, first weighing: how many in each pan, and how many left out?

Hint 1: Three piles of three

Weigh 3 against 3, leave 3 out.

Hint 2: Next

You now have three suspects. That is the three-piece problem you just watched.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. With two weighings, and knowing the odd piece is heavier, how many pieces can you handle?

Practice

You have 9 meteorite pieces that look exactly alike. Eight weigh the same; one is slightly heavier. You have a balance scale (it only tells you which side is heavier, or that they balance). Find the heavy piece using the scale only twice.

When you have a method, explain why it always works, whichever piece is the heavy one. Then try: what is the largest number of pieces for which three weighings are always enough?

Spanish · A short sky story

15 minutes · Method: comprehensible input (Krashen) and Total Physical Response (Asher). Listen and act first; speak when ready. Instructions to the guide are in English.

Learn

Today's story uses words you already have. Read each line, point at the picture, and say it. Here are the words again.

Word How to say it Meaning
es de noche es deh NOH-cheh it is night
mira MEE-rah looks at
el cielo el SYEH-loh the sky
ve beh sees
la luna lah LOO-nah the moon
blanca BLAHN-kah white
grande GRAHN-deh large
dice DEE-seh says

Learn

For the guide: tell the story twice, pointing at each picture, without translating first. The third time, pause and let the student say a word or point. Review any weak word from the week at the end.

The story. Read slowly, pointing.

  1. Es de noche.
  2. Ana mira el cielo.
  3. Ana ve la luna.
  4. La luna es blanca y grande.
  5. Ana dice: —¡Hola, luna!

Ana looks up at a large moon and waves.

Act. Mira el cielo. Señala la luna. Di hola. (wave)

Remember

A short story is just sentences you already know, one after another. La luna es blanca y grande — the moon is white and large. Y means and.

Watch me

Line 3: Ana ve la luna. Ana sees the moon. Line 4 adds a description: La luna es blanca y grande. Together they tell you what she sees and what it looks like.

Together

What does Ana dice: —¡Hola, luna! mean?

Hint 1: Dice

Dice means says.

Hint 2: Whole

Ana says: 'Hello, moon!'

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What does y mean in blanca y grande?
  2. What does Ana mira el cielo mean?

Exercises

  1. Put the five story sentences in order (the guide may cut them up, or just read them out of order for pointing).
  2. Point and say as much of the story as you can. Pointing through it silently is acceptable if speech is not ready yet.
  3. Copy the sentence you like best.

Latin · A short family story

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin reading (in the spirit of Ørberg and of Minimus). All text here is original; those books are not copied.

Learn

Today's story uses words you already have. Read, point, and say.

Word How to say it Meaning
puer POO-er boy
puella poo-EL-lah girl
canis KAH-niss dog
in hortō in HOR-toh in the garden
currit KUR-rit runs
dicit DEE-kit says
salvē SAL-way hello

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Learn

For the guide: read the story twice, finger under each word. Then the student reads with you. End by acting currit and Salvē.

The story.

  1. Aulus est puer. Gaia est puella.
  2. Celer est canis.
  3. Gaia est in hortō.
  4. Celer currit in hortō.
  5. Gaia dicit: —Salvē, Celer!

Remember

Gaia dicit: —Salvē, Celer! means Gaia says: 'Hello, Celer!' Dicit is 'says', like Spanish dice.

Watch me

Line 3: Gaia est in hortō. Gaia is in the garden. Line 4: Celer currit in hortō. Celer runs in the garden. The same place, two sentences.

Together

What does Celer est canis mean?

Hint 1: Words

Celer is the name, est is, canis dog.

Hint 2: Meaning

Celer is a dog.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What does dicit mean?
  2. What does in hortō mean?

Exercises

  1. Who is in the garden? What is the dog doing?
  2. Read the story aloud.
  3. Copy: Gaia est in hortō.

Notebook: the model walk map

60 minutes · draw, label, then expand a kernel · How the notebook works · Review sheet

Learn

A map of the scale walk should show the start (the Sun), the path, and each planet marker you reached, in order and with roughly the right spacing. Labels come from the word bank. After the labels, a because/but/so sentence explains something you noticed (for example, how empty the space between worlds felt).

Remember

Draw first, then label from the word bank, then write one because/but/so sentence about a fact on the map.

Watch me

A model sentence: 'Jupiter sat much farther down the path than Mars, because the real gap from Mars to Jupiter is many times the gap from the Sun to Mars.' The word because gives the reason. The map should show that large gap.

Together

Finish: 'The inner planets looked close together, but …'

Hint 1: Contrast

Use but to name something that was not close, or that surprised you.

Hint 2: Example

…but Jupiter was a long walk farther on.

Check

These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.

  1. What three things must the map show before you start writing sentences?

Practice

How the guide helps today: new writing step: sentence expansion. Start with the kernel "The walk was surprising." Add when or where, then why. Ask those questions one at a time. The student says the longer sentence before writing it.

After the walk, draw a map of your route with the planet markers on it.

1. Draw the map: start (the Sun), the path, and each planet you reached.

2. Label the planets you placed, plus "Sun" and "start".

Word bank: Sun · Mercury · Venus · Earth · Mars · Jupiter · Saturn · map · pace · scale · empty · surprising · orbit

3. Write. Finish:

  • The part of the walk that surprised me most was ________.
  • Books do not draw the Solar System to scale because ________.

Then expand the kernel The walk was surprising.

  • Add where (or when): The walk was surprising ________.
  • Add why: The walk was surprising ________ because ________.

4. Write one question you now want answered.
