Unit 2 · Day 6 · Laps around the Earth

Today's big question: The International Space Station circles the whole Earth in about an hour and a half. How many laps is that in a day, a week, a year? Multiplication is fast, organised adding of equal groups.

For the guide: Begin with Monday review if the unit check showed gaps. Today's concrete step is equal groups of counters, then an array (rows and columns), then the area model. The array makes it visible why 4 × 7 = 7 × 4, and the area model prepares long multiplication and, much later, algebra.

Core lesson: equal groups, arrays and the area model

45 minutes

The fact. The International Space Station orbits about 400 km above Earth at roughly 28,000 km per hour, and completes about 16 orbits a day (NASA gives about 15.5).

1. Concrete. With counters, make 4 groups of 7. Now arrange the same counters as an array: 4 rows of 7. Turn the array a quarter turn: it is now 7 rows of 4. Same counters, so 4 × 7 = 7 × 4.

2. Pictorial: the area model. Draw a rectangle 14 squares long and 6 squares high. Split the long side into 10 and 4. The rectangle becomes two smaller ones: 10 × 6 = 60 and 4 × 6 = 24. Together 84. So 14 × 6 = 84.

3. Abstract: the break-apart rule. 14 × 6 = (10 + 4) × 6 = 10 × 6 + 4 × 6. Any number can be broken into tens and ones before multiplying. This rule has a name, the distributive property, and it is used for the rest of your mathematical life.

Practice

  1. Using 16 orbits a day, how many orbits does the ISS make in a week?
  2. Draw the area model for 23 × 4 and find the product.
  3. A planetarium has 7 rows of 12 seats. How many seats?
  4. How many hours are in a week?
  5. A made-up space station has 6 solar wings, each with 8 panels. How many panels?
  6. Draw the area model for 15 × 13 (split both numbers: 10 + 5 and 10 + 3).

Math circle: how many high fives?

20 minutes

After a mission, a crew of 6 astronauts celebrate: every astronaut gives every other astronaut exactly one high five. How many high fives happen?

Solve it two different ways: (1) by listing systematically, and (2) by a counting trick. Then find a rule for a crew of any size, and check it on crews of 2, 3 and 4.

Spanish · Numbers 1–10

15 minutes · Method: comprehensible input (Krashen) and Total Physical Response (Asher). Listen and act first; speak when ready. Instructions to the guide are in English.

For the guide: count on fingers, then count stars on the page, then count a few real objects. Keep it oral first; writing the words can wait until the student can say them.

Look and listen. Count slowly, showing fingers:

uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez.

Eight stars to count. Veo ocho estrellas.

Act. Cuenta. (count) Toca tres lápices. Señala cinco estrellas. Levántate. Siéntate.

Review: Veo la luna. El sol es amarillo.

Exercises

  1. Count from uno to diez with fingers.
  2. The guide shows 4, then 7, then 10 fingers. Say the number in Spanish.
  3. Point and say: Veo ocho estrellas.

Latin · Aulus canem videt

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin reading (in the spirit of Ørberg and of *Minimus*). All text here is original; those books are not copied.

For the guide: new idea, shown by the picture, not by a lecture: *canis* becomes *canem* when the dog is the one being seen. Draw a small arrow from Aulus's eyes to the dog. The student does not need the word "accusative" today.

Aulus looks at the dog. Aulus canem videt.

Read, looking at the picture.

  • Aulus canem videt.
  • Gaia lūnam videt.
  • Celer Aulum videt.

Videt means "sees". The one who sees stays Aulus or Gaia; the one seen takes a different ending (canem, lūnam, Aulum).

Exercises

  1. Point to who sees, then to what is seen, in each sentence.
  2. Read the three lines.
  3. Look at the Moon picture from Spanish (or out of the window) and read: Gaia lūnam videt.

Notebook: spotting the Station

60 minutes · draw, label, *because / but / so* · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html) · [Printable sheets](../printables/d06.html)

How the guide helps today: the ISS drawing is tiny (4 mm out from a 12.8 cm Earth). Help with the measurement. For writing, do *because / but / so* on "The Station looks slow." If you look up a pass on NASA's Spot the Station, do that together; the student should not go online alone.

The ISS is often visible to the naked eye as a bright, steady dot crossing the sky in a few minutes, around dawn or dusk. With an adult, look up the next visible pass for your area using NASA's "Spot the Station" service. Plan to watch it.

1. Draw. Continue the Moon journal (a small box is enough). Then draw Earth as a circle 12.8 cm across (the Day 1 scale: 1 cm for 1,000 km) and draw the ISS orbit at the correct height above it: 400 km is 0.4 cm, just 4 mm above the surface.

2. Label Earth, the orbit, and the Station.

Word bank: Earth · ISS · orbit · kilometre · millimetre · scale · slow · fast · sky · pass · horizon

3. Write.

  • The Station's orbit on this scale is only ________ above Earth.
  • The Moon, on the same scale, would be ________ away (from Day 1).

Then all three:

  • The Station looks slow because ________.
  • The Station looks slow but ________.
  • The Station looks slow so ________.

4. If you have a pass time, write it in the notebook.
