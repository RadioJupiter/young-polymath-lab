Unit 7 · Day 32 · Equivalent ratios, and a scale

Today's big question: How do we write equivalent ratios, and how is a scale a ratio?

For the guide: Equivalent ratios scale both sides by the same factor. A scale is a ratio for every length. Mental math warm-up: scaling both parts

10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it

Learn

Yesterday a ratio was a comparison by equal parts. Today we write equivalent ratios by multiplying or dividing both parts, and we use that for a scale.

Remember

To keep a ratio the same, change both sides by the same factor. 2 : 3 = 4 : 6 = 6 : 9.

Watch me

Write a ratio equivalent to 2 : 5 by doubling both sides.

  1. Double 2 is 4.
  2. Double 5 is 10.
  3. 2 : 5 = 4 : 10.

Together

2 : 3 = 8 : □. What is the missing number?

Hint 1: The factor

2 × 4 = 8, so the factor is 4.

Hint 2: The other side

3 × 4 = 12. So 2 : 3 = 8 : 12.

Check

These use only equivalent ratios.

  1. 3 : 4 = 9 : □.
  2. Simplify 8 : 20.

Practice

  1. Write 1 : 6 with both sides times 5.
  2. 5 : 10 = 1 : □.

Core lesson: equivalent ratios, and a scale

35–40 minutes · Method: pictorial (equivalent bars; a size model), then the scale as a ratio, with sourced lengths labeled

Learn

Equivalent ratios are the same comparison, written with different part sizes. 2 : 3 = 4 : 6, because both sides of 2 : 3 have been multiplied by 2. The second picture uses smaller parts, but the first bar is still two-thirds as long as the second, and the comparison is unchanged.

A missing part. If 2 : 5 = 8 : □, find the factor that takes 2 to 8 (that factor is 4), then multiply 5 by the same factor: □ = 20. You can also think “8 is 4 times 2, so the other side is 4 times 5.”

A scale is a ratio. In a scale drawing or a scale model, 1 unit on the drawing stands for a stated real length. We write a scale as a ratio, for example 1 cm : 2 m (an invented classroom map of a yard, labeled invented). Every length in the drawing uses the same scale. You already used this idea on Day 5, when 1 cm stood for 70,000 km so a 20 cm ball could stand for the Sun. Today we name that idea as a ratio, and we use more than one scale, each labeled for its job.

An Earth–Moon size model. Take a new scale, 1 cm : 1,000 km, built from diameters this course already uses. Earth is 12,756 km across, so in the model it is 12,756 ÷ 1,000 = 12.756 cm, about 12.8 cm to the nearest tenth of a centimeter. The Moon is 3,475 km across, so it is 3,475 ÷ 1,000 = 3.475 cm, about 3.5 cm. The mean Earth–Moon distance this course already uses is 384,400 km, so the gap would be 384,400 ÷ 1,000 = 384.4 cm, which is 3.844 m, about 3.84 m. That gap is too wide for this page, so the picture shows the two sizes to the same scale and tells the gap, rather than squeezing the gap in. This is a different scale from Day 5’s 1 cm : 70,000 km, which was chosen so the whole Solar System walk would fit. Both scales are honest; they do different jobs.

The sky, and a 400-to-1 look. A printed star chart is usually a map of directions in the sky (angles), not a scale model of how far the stars are. We will not invent a “1 cm to so many light-years” scale for a constellation we did not measure. There is one honest ratio NASA states about the sky we see: “The Sun is vastly larger than the Moon ― its diameter is about 400 times the Moon’s. But the Moon is roughly 400 times closer to Earth.” That is why they can appear nearly the same size. Our already-taught figures agree in outline, and they are not exactly 400 for both jobs: the Sun’s diameter 1,391,400 km ÷ the Moon’s 3,475 km is about 400 (about 400.4). The already-taught Earth–Sun distance, about 150,000,000 km, ÷ 384,400 km is about 390. We will keep NASA’s “about / roughly 400” as NASA’s wording, and we will not force 150,000,000 ÷ 384,400 to equal 400.

Remember

Equivalent ratios scale both sides by the same factor. A scale is a ratio: 1 unit on the drawing stands for a stated real length; every length uses the same scale. At 1 cm : 1,000 km, Earth is about 12.8 cm across, the Moon about 3.5 cm, and the gap about 3.84 m, too wide for the page. A star chart is usually angles, not a distance scale. NASA: the Sun’s diameter is about 400 times the Moon’s, and the Moon is roughly 400 times closer; our figures give about 400 and about 390, and we will not force them to match.

Watch me

2 : 7 = 6 : □. Find the missing number.

  1. 2 × 3 = 6, so the factor is 3.
  2. 7 × 3 = 21.
  3. 2 : 7 = 6 : 21.

Together

On an invented classroom map the scale is 1 cm : 2 m. A path on the map is 5 cm long. How long is the real path?

Hint 1: The scale

Each centimeter stands for 2 meters.

Hint 2: Five centimeters

5 × 2 = 10 m. The map and the 10 m are invented, so they stay labeled invented.

Check

These use only today’s equivalent ratios and scales.

  1. 5 : 8 = 15 : □.
  2. At the 1 cm : 1,000 km model, how many centimeters across is a 6,792 km Mars? (Use the fact-sheet diameter already taught, and say if you leave it exact or rounded.)

Practice

  1. 3 : 5 = □ : 20.
  2. An invented classroom plan uses 1 cm : 5 m. A real wall is 15 m. How long is the wall on the plan? Label the plan invented.
  3. Why is the Earth–Moon gap not drawn at the same scale on today’s picture?
  4. Using 1,391,400 km and 3,475 km, about how many times as wide as the Moon is the Sun? Why do we still say “about 400,” not “exactly 400”?
  5. Why will this course not print “1 cm on this star chart stands for 4 light-years” as a sourced scale?

Challenge problems: a missing length, and two honest scales

20 minutes · after the core, or later if the core took the full sitting

Learn

These items mix equivalent ratios and scale. Day 5’s 1 cm : 70,000 km and today’s 1 cm : 1,000 km are both honest; they are different jobs. Invented maps stay labeled. We will not force the Sun–Moon distance ratio to 400.

Remember

Same methods as the core. Two scales can both be right. Labels stay honest.

Watch me

4 : 9 = 12 : □. Then, at 1 cm : 1,000 km, how many centimeters would stand for 384,400 km?

  1. 4 × 3 = 12, so □ = 9 × 3 = 27.
  2. 384,400 ÷ 1,000 = 384.4 cm (3.844 m, about 3.84 m).
  3. The first item is a pure ratio; the second uses the already-taught mean distance.

Together

On Day 5, 1 cm stood for 70,000 km. At that scale, how many centimeters would Earth’s 12,756 km diameter be, and why is that a different job from today’s 12.8 cm Earth?

Hint 1: Day 5

12,756 ÷ 70,000 ≈ 0.18 cm, a very small disc.

Hint 2: The job

Day 5 had to fit the whole Solar System around a 20 cm Sun. Today’s 1 cm : 1,000 km is for seeing Earth and the Moon as sizes. Both scales are labeled; neither is “the official scale.”

Check

These stay with scale and equivalent ratios.

  1. 1 : 8 = 5 : □.
  2. An invented map is 1 cm : 10 km. A real 35 km road is how many centimeters on the map? Label the map invented.

Practice

  1. Why is 2 : 3 = 4 : 6, but 2 : 3 is not equal to 2 : 6?
  2. About 150,000,000 ÷ 384,400 is about 390. NASA says the Moon is “roughly 400 times” closer than the Sun. How can both be honest?
  3. At 1 cm : 1,000 km, a length of 2.5 cm stands for how many kilometers?
  4. A friend wants to draw the 3.84 m gap as 4 cm “so it fits.” What is lost if the gap no longer uses the same scale as the sizes?
  5. This wrap is not a second score.

Math circle: every length uses the same scale

20 minutes · a short reason, then a check

Learn

A scale is a ratio that applies to every length in the model. If we shrink the gap so it fits the page, but leave the planet sizes as they were, the model has two scales at once, and a measured centimeter no longer has one meaning. Area is not a length: today’s check stays with lengths, and we do not treat a scale factor for length as an automatic scale for area.

Remember

One scale for every length. A star chart is usually angles, not a distance scale. NASA’s “about / roughly 400” is kept as NASA’s wording beside our about-400 and about-390 figures.

Watch me

Why must the Earth size, the Moon size, and the gap use the same scale, even when the gap will not fit on the page?

  1. A scale is one ratio: 1 cm stands for 1,000 km, for every length.
  2. If the gap used a different scale, a centimeter would mean two different real lengths.
  3. Telling the gap in words (about 3.84 m) keeps the scale honest when the page is too small.

Together

Why is a printed star chart not automatically a scale model of distances?

Hint 1: What it shows

It usually shows directions in the sky — where to look — not how many kilometers away a star is.

Hint 2: What we will not invent

A “1 cm to so many light-years” label would be a homemade scale unless we had measured and sourced it.

Check

These check the reason.

  1. Name the two honest scales used in this unit so far, and one job for each.
  2. What do we write instead of squeezing a 3.84 m gap onto a small page?

Practice

  1. Give one reason 1 cm : 1,000 km and 1 cm : 70,000 km can both be right.
  2. If we doubled every length in a model, would a square’s area also merely double? (A first look: draw a 1-by-1 square and a 2-by-2 square.)
  3. How can NASA’s “roughly 400 times closer” sit beside our about-390 calculation without a fight?
  4. Why is “exactly 400 : 1” for both diameter and distance a neater story than the figures support?
  5. This argument is not a badge.

Spanish · el mapa, el dibujo, cerca, and lejos

15 minutes · Instruction in English; Hear for Spanish words · present tense only

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
el mapa el MAH-pah the map
el dibujo el dee-BOO-hoh the drawing
cerca SEHR-kah near
lejos LEH-hohs far

Today’s words name a map, a drawing, near, and far. Instruction stays in English. Scale arithmetic and the Earth–Moon model stay in the English mathematics.

Original sentences: Hay un mapa. Hay un dibujo. El mapa está cerca. El dibujo no está lejos.

For the guide: hold a simple classroom map or drawing, then point near and far. This is present-tense description, not a scale lesson in Spanish. está is already a familiar “is (located)” pattern if it has been used with bajo cero; if it is new in this sense, say it is the form of estar for where something is, and keep the four new words as the day’s load.

Remember

el mapa; el dibujo; cerca; lejos. Instruction in English. Hear for Spanish words. No scale arithmetic in the Spanish.

Watch me

Say: “There is a map.”

  1. Hay un mapa.
  2. Hay introduces it. Un mapa is the map (mapa is masculine).
  3. This is a present-tense pattern.

Together

How do you say “far,” and “The map is near”?

Hint 1: Far

lejos

Hint 2: The sentence

El mapa está cerca.

Check

These use only today’s phrases.

  1. Write: “There is a drawing.”
  2. Write: “The drawing is not far.”

Exercises

  1. Correct: *La mapa está cerca, if someone used the wrong article.
  2. Hear mapa, dibujo, and lejos, then use each.
  3. Why does the Spanish not say 12.8 cm or 3.84 m?
  4. A pointing retell: a map, a drawing, near, far.
  5. Copy one model sentence into the notebook.

Latin · imāgō and forma

15 minutes · Method: picture-based Latin; original sentences. Instruction in English.

Learn

Word How to say it Meaning
imāgō ih-MAH-goh an image, a picture (nominative, feminine)
Imāgō parva est ih-MAH-goh PAR-wah est The image is small
forma FOR-mah a shape, a form (nominative, feminine)
Forma magna est FOR-mah MAG-nah est The shape is large

The Hear-word button uses an Italian voice as an approximation of Latin, not the speech of ancient Rome.

Original sentences, not from a textbook: Imāgō parva est. Forma magna est. Imāgō nōn parva est. Forma nōn magna est.

imāgō and forma are both feminine, so parva and magna match. Scale figures and NASA diameters stay in the English mathematics.

For the guide: the student reads as much as possible. Point to a small picture and a large shape. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation. Yesterday’s pars / tōtum can be pointed to again, but they are not required in today’s check.

Remember

imāgō; Imāgō parva est; forma; Forma magna est. Adjectives match (parva / magna). No scale numbers in the Latin. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation.

Watch me

  3. The sentence says the image is small.

Check

  2. Write: “The shape is not large.”

Exercises

Notebook: an Earth–Moon size model, and a because-sentence

60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · How the notebook works · Review sheet

Learn

Draw Earth and the Moon to the same size scale (about 12.8 cm and about 3.5 cm is ideal if the page can hold a smaller similar pair; a clearly labeled smaller pair with the scale 1 cm : 1,000 km written beside it is also honest). Write the gap as about 3.84 m, too wide for the page. Then complete a because / but / so stem about two honest scales, or about NASA’s “roughly 400” beside our about-390.

Remember

Draw and label first. Complete stems. Do not squeeze the gap in at a second scale. This page is a record, not a score or a badge.

Watch me

A model sentence: Earth is about 12.8 cm across in this model because 12,756 ÷ 1,000 = 12.756, but the 3.84 m gap will not fit on the page, so I will write the gap in words and keep one scale for every length.

  1. The because uses the scale as a ratio.
  2. The but names the gap honestly.
  3. The so keeps one scale for every length.

Together

Finish: Day 5 used 1 cm : 70,000 km because ________, but today’s 1 cm : 1,000 km has a different job, so ________.

Hint 1: The because

…that scale let a 20 cm ball stand for the Sun on a Solar System walk…

Hint 2: The so

…I will keep both scales labeled, and I will not call either “the official scale.”

Check

The page should show the sizes, the written gap, and complete stems.

  1. Draw a same-scale Earth and Moon, with 1 cm : 1,000 km labeled, and write the gap as about 3.84 m.
  2. Write a three-stem explanation (because / but / so) about one scale for every length, or about about-400 and about-390.

Practice

  1. Tick the word-bank words you used: scale, ratio, model, gap, invented, star chart, about.
  2. A note that a star chart is usually angles, not a distance scale, if that helps the page.
  3. The page is a record, not a score.
  4. File the page. It is not a reward.
  5. A note that both scales in the unit stay labeled.
