Challenge problems: ranges and means, and a freezer that is not a planet

Check. 1. 152 degrees (167 − 15). Both are fact-sheet means. 2. −180°C is colder (farther left). The two figures come from NASA Science range pages, not from the fact-sheet mean row.

  1. No. 20°C is a high in Mars’s range, not its mean. The fact-sheet mean for Mars is −65°C, which is much colder than Earth’s mean of 15°C. A world can have a hot hour and a cold mean.
  2. 127 − (−20) = 147 degrees, with 127 labeled about and −20 labeled as a mean.
  3. About 467°C is a NASA Science surface figure for Venus; 430°C is a NASA Science sunlit high for Mercury, not Mercury’s mean (167°C). This lesson’s mean-to-mean comparison was 464°C against 167°C, which is 297 degrees.
  4. 13 degrees (8 − (−5) = 13). Both readings invented.
  5. No such lunchtime reading was sourced today. It would be an invented report, not a fact-sheet mean and not a NASA Science range.

Math circle: why a mean may not describe a place

Check. 1. The Moon (−20°C) or Mars (−65°C). 2. We have a mean and a range, not a sourced daily report. Inventing one would pretend to a measurement we do not have.

  1. No. The fact-sheet note says there will tend to be variations from equator to poles, from day to night, and with the seasons, on most worlds — as with Earth.
  2. Keep them labeled by source: 464°C as the fact-sheet mean; about 467°C as the Science page’s surface figure. We do not average them into a homemade official number.
  3. …that 167°C is the temperature of a given place on Mercury.
  4. Venus 464 against Mercury 167, because those two sit in the same fact-sheet mean row. About 467 and 430 are different kinds of Science-page figures (a surface figure and a sunlit high), not a matched pair of means.
  5. It marks that the temperature is 65 degrees below zero (to the left of 0 on the line), not a subtraction sign waiting for a second number in that writing.
