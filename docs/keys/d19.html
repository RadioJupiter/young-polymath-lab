Core lesson: volume of a box, and the liter

Check. 1. 200 cm³, which is 0.2 L (because 1,000 cm³ = 1 L). 2. 0.24 m³.

  1. 8 m³ = 8,000 L.
  2. Because a crew water use would need a sourced figure, and this course will not invent one.
  3. 6.36 / 1,013.25 is about 0.0063, which is less than 1/100 (and 6.36 / 1,014, the NASA Earth-sheet figure, is also less than 1/100). It is not a volume of air, and not a life-support budget.
  4. 1 × 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1 m³ = 100 L; or 100 × 50 × 20 = 100,000 cm³ = 100 L.
  5. A caption that names 6 m by 3 m by 2.5 m and 45 m³ as a classroom design problem, not a NASA specification.

Math circle: a liter is a cube, not a guess

Check. 1. 20 cm by 20 cm by 20 cm (8,000 cm³ = 8 L). 2. 125 cm³, which is 0.125 L, or 1/8 of a liter.

  1. 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 of the 10 cm cubes, which also matches 1 m³ = 1,000 L.
  2. A posted sentence naming 1 L = 1,000 cm³ and the 10 cm cube.
  3. Because no sourced figure was given for a person’s air use, and the course will not invent one. The pressure fact (6.36 mb) is a different kind of fact.
