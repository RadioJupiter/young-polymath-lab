  1. 0.05, 0.45, 0.5.
  2. The same amount (1 tenth = 10 hundredths).

Core lesson: comparing decimals, and 0.4 = 0.40

Check. 1. 0.6 is greater: 60 hundredths against 58 hundredths. 2. 0.50; yes, they are the same amount.

  1. 0.04, 0.39, 0.4, 0.40. (0.4 and 0.40 are the same amount, so they tie; writing them next to each other is fine if that is said.)
  2. A mark halfway between 0.2 and 0.3.
  3. 0.09 is 9 hundredths; 0.1 is 10 hundredths.
  4. As amounts they are equal. If they are scale readings, 0.40 may mean “to the nearest hundredth” and 0.4 may mean “to the nearest tenth.” That is a caution, not a claim that 0.40 is a larger amount.
  5. 0.7 m is longer (70 hundredths of a meter against 65). Both lengths invented.

Challenge problems: close decimals, and a reading that is not a NASA size

Check. 1. 0.95, 1.05, 1.5. 2. 1 is greater, by 1 hundredth (1 = 1.00; 0.99 is 99 hundredths).

  1. For example 0.41, or 0.45, or 0.49 — any hundredth from 0.41 through 0.49.
  2. Yes, the same amount: 2 tenths.
  3. 0.8 is 80 hundredths; 0.75 is 75 hundredths; 0.8 is greater even though it can be written with fewer digits.
  4. 0.8 L is much closer to 1 L (2 tenths away) than 0.08 L is (0.92 L away). Both marks invented.
  5. Writing two zeros after the point does not make a new measurement. We did not measure Mars to the nearest hundredth of a kilometer in this lesson.

Math circle: same amount, different claimed precision

Check. 1. Yes. 2. It can show the length was written to the nearest hundredth, not only to the nearest tenth.

  1. No. Tenths only would not distinguish 0.35 from 0.3 or 0.4 as a hundredth. The scale cannot honestly claim a hundredth it cannot mark.
  2. It is still a labeled approximation. Writing two decimal places does not make 3.14 into π.
  3. We did not measure a new hundredth of a kilometer. The extra zeros would pretend to a precision we do not have.
  4. No. They mark the same point.
  5. …claim a finer reading (when the number is a measurement)…
