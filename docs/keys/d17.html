Core lesson: perimeter of rectangles and of an L-shaped figure

Check. 1. 26 cm. (2 × (9+4) = 26.) 2. Overall: 10 cm wide, 6 cm tall, with a 3 cm by 3 cm bite from the top right. Walk: 10 (base) + 6 (left) + 7 (top, 10−3) + 3 (down the cut) + 3 (along the cut) + 3 (remaining right side, 6−3) = 32 cm. Check: a 10×6 rectangle had perimeter 32 cm, and a rectangular bite in a corner leaves the perimeter the same (you replace two outer edges of 3 and 3 with two new edges of 3 and 3). So P is still 32 cm.

  1. 48 m, invented courtyard, labeled.

Challenge problems: a fence around a module, and missing sides

Check. 1. P = 20 = 2 × (6 + W), so 10 = 6 + W, W = 4 m. 2. Still 20 m. A rectangular bite from a corner replaces two outer edges with two new edges of the same lengths, so the perimeter does not change.

  1. Fencing might skip a door, or include a gate, or sit away from the wall; those are extra facts. The floor perimeter alone may not be the fence length.
  2. 34 cm, which is 0.34 m.
  3. Horizontal edges must sum to the total width; vertical edges must sum to the total height. The missing edge equals that total minus the known edges in that direction.
  4. A caption that names 8 m by 4 m as invented classroom measures, and 24 m as the floor outline.
  5. The rectangle’s perimeter is 2 × 12 = 24 cm, so the square’s side is 24 ÷ 4 = 6 cm.

Math circle: does a bite change the perimeter?

Check. 1. A rectangular bite from a corner leaves the perimeter unchanged; a bay in the middle of a side is a different cut and usually lengthens the walk. 2. 2 × (8+5) = 26 cm, and the corner bite does not change it, so 26 cm.

  1. Because a corner bite can leave the walk the same length, and a bay can make it longer — cutting is not “using up” perimeter.
  2. For example, a 2 m wide and 1 m deep bay in a long side: remove 2 m, add 1+2+1=4 m, so P = 18 + 2 = 20 m, invented module. Other labeled bays are fine if the arithmetic matches.
  3. A short, standalone remove-and-add argument.
