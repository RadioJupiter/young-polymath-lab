The notebook

Leonardo da Vinci filled notebooks for most of his life, and thousands of pages survive. On a single page he might sketch a bird's wing, work out a calculation, design a machine and write a question to answer later, often in his famous mirror-writing from right to left. The notebooks were not for showing off; they were how he thought. This notebook works the same way.

What you need

A notebook with plain pages (A4 or a bit smaller), a pencil, an eraser, a ruler, a small measuring tape, and coloured pencils if you like. Date every page.

What goes on a page

  1. The question. What are you trying to find out or make?
  2. Observation. Draw what you actually see, not what you think it should look like. Label it.
  3. Measurement. Numbers with units, and how you measured them.
  4. Calculation. Worked in full, so you or anyone else can check it.
  5. Invention or explanation. A design, a diagram that explains, or a prediction.
  6. What next. A new question, or something to test.

Weekly rhythm

Day Notebook focus
Monday Observe: the sky, shadows, the Moon, something in nature
Tuesday Measure: a length, a time, a weight, a speed
Wednesday Draw from life: one object, slowly and carefully
Thursday Invent: design a machine, a tool, a model or an experiment
Friday Explain: teach one idea from the week with a diagram

The daily lessons give a specific notebook task. When there is extra time, follow the weekly rhythm.

Ongoing projects

  • The Moon journal (begun on Day 4): one entry each clear evening for a month.
  • The sky log: with a guide, note the planets you can see without a telescope (Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn are often visible) and where they are each week.
  • Observing with a telescope: if a telescope is available, sketch the Moon's craters near the line between light and dark, where shadows are longest, and Jupiter's four largest moons, which Galileo first saw in January 1610 and which change position from night to night.

Safety, always: never look at the Sun, with or without a telescope or binoculars. Solar observing needs special certified filters and an adult.

Looking after the notebook

Never tear out a page, even a "bad" one; cross out and keep going. Leonardo's notebooks are full of crossings-out and second tries. A notebook full of honest attempts is worth far more than a tidy one.
