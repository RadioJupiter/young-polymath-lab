How the notebook works

Leonardo da Vinci filled notebooks for most of his life, and thousands of pages survive. On a single page he might sketch a bird's wing, work out a calculation, design a machine and write a question to answer later, often in his famous mirror-writing from right to left. The notebooks were not for showing off; they were how he thought. This notebook works the same way, with one difference: writing is built in small, supported steps, because clear thinking on paper should not have to wait for handwriting to be easy.

The order of work, every day

Do the steps in this order. Do not start with a blank paragraph.

  1. Draw first. Draw what is actually there, or the model or invention for today's project. Take time over the picture. Drawing is thinking, and it gives the words something to attach to.
  2. Label next. Add short labels from the day's word bank (and any careful spelling the student already knows). Point to each part and say the word aloud, then write it.
  3. Make sentences from stems. The lesson gives unfinished sentences (and, as the days go on, because / but / so prompts, then sentence expansion, then a short paragraph). Say the whole sentence aloud first. Then write it. One good sentence is better than a long, unfinished one.
  4. Numbers and "what next." Do the measurements and calculations in full, with units. End with a question or a prediction.

The daily lessons name the exact drawing, word bank and stems. Each notebook hour has its own short review sheet, linked from the lesson: see Review sheets.

How the guide helps

The guide (a parent or tutor) sits nearby for this hour, especially at first. The aim is that the student does more and more of the writing independently, but help is not a failure.

  • Before drawing: read the task aloud together. Check that the materials are to hand. Do not start writing yet.
  • While labelling: point to the word bank. If a word is needed that is not in the bank, write it clearly on a scrap for the student to copy. Do not turn labelling into a spelling test.
  • Before each sentence: ask the student to say the finished sentence out loud. If the sentence is complete and makes sense, say "Yes, write that." If it is a fragment, help by asking a question ("The Moon is what?"), not by supplying a whole sentence.
  • If writing itself is the hard part: the guide may write the student's own spoken sentence in light pencil, and the student traces or copies it in pen or darker pencil. This is a temporary scaffold. Fade it as soon as the student can write a short sentence unaided. Never write ideas the student did not say.
  • Because / but / so, and expansion: these are from The Writing Revolution (Hochman and Wexler, 2017). For because / but / so, keep the same opening clause and finish it three ways: a reason, a contrast, and a result. For expansion, start with a short kernel ("The Moon shines") and add when, where and why, one piece at a time.
  • When a short paragraph appears (later in Unit 2): first make a tiny outline (a topic sentence written in full, two or three detail notes that are not full sentences, and a closing sentence). Draft only from that outline. The guide may scribe the outline notes if needed.
  • Do not correct every error in the first draft. If a fact is wrong, ask a question and let the student fix it. Spelling can be tidied from the word bank after the sentences are down. Praise a complete, honest sentence.

What you need

A notebook with plain pages (A4 or Letter, or a bit smaller), a pencil, an eraser, a ruler, a small measuring tape, and coloured pencils if you like. Date every page. The printable sheets can be kept in a folder or pasted into the notebook.

Weekly rhythm

Day Notebook focus
Monday Observe: the sky, shadows, the Moon, something in nature
Tuesday Measure: a length, a time, a weight, a speed
Wednesday Draw from life: one object, slowly and carefully
Thursday Invent: design a machine, a tool, a model or an experiment
Friday Explain: teach one idea from the week with a diagram and sentences

The daily lessons give a specific project. When there is extra time, follow the weekly rhythm, still using draw → label → stems.

Ongoing projects

  • The Moon journal (begun on Day 4): one entry each clear evening for a month. A drawing of the shape, the date and time, and one short sentence is enough.
  • The sky log: with a guide, note the planets you can see without a telescope (Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn are often visible) and where they are each week.
  • Observing with a telescope: if a telescope is available, sketch the Moon's craters near the line between light and dark, where shadows are longest, and Jupiter's four largest moons, which Galileo first saw in January 1610 and which change position from night to night.

Safety, always: never look at the Sun, with or without a telescope or binoculars. Solar observing needs special certified filters and an adult.

Looking after the notebook

Never tear out a page, even a "bad" one; cross out and keep going. Leonardo's notebooks are full of crossings-out and second tries. A notebook full of honest attempts is worth far more than a tidy one.
