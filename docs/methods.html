Method

This page explains how the course teaches a strong young mathematician, and why. The short version: solid foundations, taught with understanding, at the pace of mastery, with hard problems every day, all inside a world of real science.

The language of instruction is English. Spanish and Latin are taught as short daily subjects.

1. Mastery and acceleration, with depth

The student moves on when a level is mastered, not when a calendar says so. The research supports this: a large review of a century of studies found that academic acceleration has clear positive effects on the achievement of high-ability students (Steenbergen-Hu, Makel & Olszewski-Kubilius, 2016), and the Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth, which has followed mathematically talented young people for decades, argues that appropriately challenging opportunities, acceleration among them, help such talent develop (Lubinski & Benbow, 2006). Acceleration alone can turn into racing through procedures, so every lesson pairs pace with depth: challenge problems and the math circle are never skipped.

2. Concrete → pictorial → abstract (CPA)

Every new idea is first built with objects (base-ten pieces, counters), then drawn (place value charts, arrays, area models, bar models), then written in symbols. This sequence follows Jerome Bruner's account of three modes of representation (enactive, iconic, symbolic) and is the backbone of Singapore's primary mathematics curriculum, which adopted the CPA approach in its 1981 curriculum (Kaur, 2019). Strong students may move through the concrete step quickly, but it is never skipped: the picture is what makes a method understood, and it is what lets you rebuild a method you have forgotten.

3. Bar models for problem solving

The Singapore model method, introduced in 1983 by the team led by Dr Kho Tek Hong, draws the quantities in a word problem as bars: part-whole, comparison and change models (Kaur, 2019). The bar model turns a story into a structure, and later becomes algebra: a bar labelled "?" is an unknown x.

4. Problem solving as the main event

Following the approach of Beast Academy, the elementary curriculum from Art of Problem Solving, problems that cannot be solved by applying a just-taught procedure are a daily part of the work, not an extra. Being stuck, trying another way, and finally seeing it is the experience that builds a mathematician. Hints are given in stages and only after real effort.

5. The Russian math circle

Each day ends its mathematics with one rich problem from the math circle tradition, as collected in Mathematical Circles (Russian Experience) by Fomin, Genkin and Itenberg (AMS, 1996): logic puzzles, systematic counting, parity, invariants, the pigeonhole principle, weighings, divisibility. These topics are rarely in school curricula, but they are the heart of mathematical reasoning: every circle problem ends with explaining why, which is the beginning of proof.

6. Mental math and retrieval

Ten minutes of mental calculation opens each day. Fluent facts and strategies (making tens, doubling and halving, breaking apart) free attention for harder thinking, and recalling earlier material from memory strengthens it (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Strategies are always explained aloud, never just raced.

7. Space and physics, with real numbers

Every unit is set in astronomy or physics: distances in the Solar System, the speed of light, orbits, pendulums, the Moon's phases. All numbers come from authoritative sources (NASA's planetary fact sheets, the international definitions of the meter and the astronomical unit) and are rounded honestly, with the rounding stated. Experiments use household materials.

8. Spanish, by comprehensible input

Fifteen minutes a day of Spanish, understood from pictures, gestures and context rather than from a grammar list. This follows Stephen Krashen's input hypothesis: language is acquired when the learner understands messages a little beyond the current level, with context to help (Principles and Practice in Second Language Acquisition, 1982). Commands are acted out first, after James Asher's Total Physical Response (Learning Another Language Through Actions): the student shows understanding by moving, and speaks when ready. All stories and exercises here are original.

9. Latin, by picture-based reading

Fifteen minutes a day of short Latin captions and stories, understood from pictures and repetition, with brief English only as support. This is the idea behind Hans Ørberg's Lingua Latina per se Illustrata (read Latin as Latin from the first page) adapted to a beginner pace, in the same spirit as a primary course such as Barbara Bell's Minimus: Starting out in Latin (Cambridge University Press, 1999, illustrated by Helen Forte). Neither book is reproduced. Minimus is a useful optional companion if a published course is wanted later.

10. The notebook, with writing built in steps

The hour of notebook and project time, modeled on Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks, is where mathematics meets the world: observing, measuring, drawing from life and inventing. Because writing is often the hard part, the hour always goes draw → label from a word bank → sentences from stems, then gradually because / but / so, sentence expansion, and short paragraphs, using the sentence-level activities of The Writing Revolution (Hochman and Wexler, 2017). The guide's role at each step is set out on How the notebook works.

11. What is deliberately left out

  • No points, badges, streaks or leaderboards. Motivation comes from interesting problems and from visibly being able to do more than before.
  • No babyish framing. Lessons speak directly and respectfully, and the guide notes speak to the adult.
  • No age or grade labels. The level map is the only measure of where the student is.

The guide's role

The guide (a parent or tutor) reads the guide notes, runs the concrete step, gives hints in stages, checks answers with the keys, and decides when a unit check shows mastery. The guide does not need to be a mathematician; the keys explain every solution. For Spanish and Latin, the guide speaks or reads slowly, points at the pictures, and acts out commands with the student. For the notebook, the guide follows How the notebook works.

How a lesson is taught

Every lesson follows a short explicit-instruction sequence, after Barak Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction (2012): Learn (a clear explanation and the fact, rule or method), Remember (the compact form of that rule), Watch me (one or two fully worked examples), Together (a guided problem with hints), Check (two or three questions that use only the skill just taught), then independent practice. The Check and the review sheet never introduce a new skill.

A Listen button reads the explanation in English. In Spanish and Latin lessons, the explanations are still in English; only the words being taught have a short Hear-word clip in that language. Nothing is sent to a network: the browser's own voice is used, and if a voice is missing the page can still be read.

What the mathematics is, and what it is not

The mathematics follows the Singapore method, not American standardised-test habits. Place value is taught with discs and a chart, then with number bonds and ×10 as a shift of place. Word problems use part-whole, comparison and change bar models. Mental work uses make-ten, compensation, splitting, and double-and-halve. Harder problems use named heuristics (a smaller case, a systematic list, work backwards, draw a diagram). Practice is varied on the same skill, not a battery of test-item types.

We do not set writing a number "in words", a named "expanded form" drill, "what is the value of the digit …", "make the largest and smallest numbers from these digits", or a bare "round to the nearest ten, hundred and thousand" list, as things to learn. Those are test habits. A place-value sum that records disc values (300,000 + 80,000 + 4,000 + 400) may appear in the abstract step, because that is how the discs are written down, not because "expanded form" is a goal.

Review sheets

Each lesson has its own short review sheet (one page, at most two), linked from the lesson. It is for retention after the teaching, and it mirrors the Remember card and the Check. There are no combined unit packets.
