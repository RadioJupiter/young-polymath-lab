# Level 1 · Day 1 · Counting a classroom sky

**Today's big question:** How do we write a number as big as 1,000, and how can we count a set of stars without missing any?

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> Work through the blocks in order. Do not skip the concrete step in the core lesson, even if the student already seems quick: discs, a ten-frame, or a bond are the foundation. Answers go in the math notebook; check them with the key only after the work is done. If the placement check started the student here, this is the first mathematics of the course, and Spanish and Latin also begin here. Students who later move to Unit 1 (Level 4) will meet a short recap of greetings there; that recap is for students who skipped these foundation levels.</p>

## Core lesson: numbers to 1,000, and a classroom sky {#core}

<p class="meta">45 minutes · concrete → pictorial → abstract</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>


A number tells **how many**. We write numbers with **places**: **ones**, **tens**, and **hundreds**. Ten ones make 1 ten. Ten tens make 1 hundred. Ten hundreds make **1,000**.

The number **247** is 2 hundreds, 4 tens, and 7 ones.

## Challenge problems {#challenge}

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

A 0 in a place is not “nothing to think about.” In 305, the 0 says there are **no tens**, and it holds the tens place so that the 3 can stay as hundreds. 35 is a different number: 3 tens and 5 ones.

</div>

<div class="check">

### Practice

1. Write 1 hundred, 10 tens, and 0 ones as a three-digit number, after you regroup.
2. What is 10 less than 200? Use places, not a guess.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. 720 is greater: it has 7 hundreds and 2 tens; 702 has 7 hundreds and 0 tens. The tens place decides once the hundreds are equal.
2. 20 stars (5 × 4, or 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4).

1. 10 tens make 1 more hundred, so this is 2 hundreds: **200**.
2. 200 is 2 hundreds. 10 less is 1 hundred and 9 tens: **190**. (Or: 200 − 10 = 190.)

## Math circle: counting without missing any {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · one rich problem, solved and then explained</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

To be sure you have counted every object, and have not counted one twice, you need a **plan**. A good plan is an order that does not jump around: rows, or columns, or a path that visits each object once.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

A count you can trust has a plan. “I just looked and saw 12” is not a plan.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>How would you count the 12 invented stars in today’s picture so that you can prove the total?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>Count left to right along the first row (4), then the second row (4 more, running total 8), then the third (4 more, 12).</li>
<li>Or count in columns: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12. Either plan works if it visits each star once.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>An invented picture has 4 rows of 4 stars. A student counts 15, then 16, then 17, by pointing at random. What is wrong, and what is the true total if there are really 4 rows of 4?</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: The plan</summary>

Pointing at random can miss a star or count one twice, so the three totals disagree.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Equal groups</summary>

4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 16, or 4 groups of 4.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These are about a counting plan, not about official stars.</p>

1. Why can two careful people get the same total for the same invented picture?
2. If a row is hidden, can you still be sure of the total? What must you know?

</div>

### Practice

1. Invent (and label invented) a 2-by-6 star picture. Count it two ways and show that both ways agree.
2. Why is “count the real sky tonight and get a number for all the stars” not this lesson’s job?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. They use a plan that visits each star once, so they are counting the same set.
2. Only if you know how many are in the hidden row (or that the rows are equal and you know how many rows and how many in a visible row). Guessing the hidden row is not a count.

1. 2 rows of 6, or 6 columns of 2: both 12. Other invented equal-group pictures accepted if both counts agree and the picture is labeled invented.
2. The real sky is not an invented classroom picture, and this lesson does not invent an official star count. We count a set we can plan for.

## Notebook: a classroom sky, counted in order {#notebook}

<p class="meta">60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>


Draw first: copy today’s invented 12-star picture (3 rows of 4), or make your own invented sky of equal rows, and **label it invented**. Then label: star, row, count. Then write from stems. Do not start with a blank paragraph.

**Word bank:** star, row, count, invented, twelve (or your total), order.

**Stems** (say each finished sentence aloud, then write it):

1. I counted the stars in order, because …
2. The picture is invented, but …
3. I counted ___ stars, so …

End with a number sentence for your count (for example 4 + 4 + 4 = 12) and one “what next” question.


</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Draw, label, then write. Count in a planned order. Label invented pictures invented. One complete sentence is better than a long unfinished one.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Finish this stem aloud: “I counted the stars in order, because …”</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>A model: “I counted the stars in order, because I did not want to miss one or count one twice.” Other complete, honest finishes are accepted.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>Finish: “The picture is invented, but …” What must the but-clause not do?</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: A contrast</summary>

A model: “The picture is invented, but I can still count it with a plan.”

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Official counts</summary>

Do not treat the total as an official count of the real sky.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These check the notebook order and the stems.</p>

1. In what order do you work: write first, or draw first?
2. Write one number sentence for 3 rows of 4.

</div>

### Practice

1. Finish all three stems in writing, after you have drawn and labeled.
2. Write one “what next” question about counting a sky picture.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. Draw first, then label, then write.
2. 4 + 4 + 4 = 12, or 3 × 4 = 12 if you already write it that way; addition of equal groups is enough.

1. Complete, honest sentences accepted if they follow the stems and do not treat the picture as an official sky count.
2. A model: “What if I used 4 rows of 4? Would my plan still work?” Other honest questions accepted.
