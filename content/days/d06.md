# Unit 2 · Day 6 · Laps around the Earth

**Today's big question:** The International Space Station circles the whole Earth in about an hour and a half. How many laps is that in a day, a week, a year? Multiplication is fast, organised adding of equal groups.

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> Begin with Monday review if the unit check showed gaps. Today's concrete step is equal groups of counters, then an array (rows and columns), then the area model. The array makes it visible why 4 × 7 = 7 × 4, and the area model prepares long multiplication and, much later, algebra.</p>

## Core lesson: equal groups, arrays and the area model {#core}

<p class="meta">45 minutes</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**The fact.** The International Space Station orbits about 400 km above Earth at roughly 28,000 km per hour, and completes about **16 orbits a day** (NASA gives about 15.5).

**1. Concrete.** With counters, make 4 groups of 7. Now arrange the same counters as an array: 4 rows of 7. Turn the array a quarter turn: it is now 7 rows of 4. Same counters, so 4 × 7 = 7 × 4.

**2. Pictorial: the area model.** Draw a rectangle 14 squares long and 6 squares high. Split the long side into 10 and 4. The rectangle becomes two smaller ones: 10 × 6 = 60 and 4 × 6 = 24. Together 84. So 14 × 6 = 84.

**3. Abstract: the break-apart rule.** 14 × 6 = (10 + 4) × 6 = 10 × 6 + 4 × 6. Any number can be broken into tens and ones before multiplying. This rule has a name, the **distributive property**, and it is used for the rest of your mathematical life.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

The area model: split each factor into tens and ones (or other useful parts), find the four (or two) smaller rectangles, and add. This is the same as the distributive law.

</div>

## Math circle: how many high fives? {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

If every person in a group gives every other person exactly one high five, you can count by a **systematic list**, or by the trick: each of *n* people gives *n*−1 high fives, but each high five was counted twice (once from each side), so the total is *n*(*n*−1)/2.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

The number of pairings among *n* people is *n*(*n*−1)/2.

</div>

### Practice

After a mission, a crew of 6 astronauts celebrate: every astronaut gives every other astronaut exactly one high five. How many high fives happen?

Solve it two different ways: (1) by listing systematically, and (2) by a counting trick. Then find a rule for a crew of any size, and check it on crews of 2, 3 and 4.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. 5 × 4 / 2 = **10**.

**15.** Way 1: the first astronaut high-fives 5 others; the second has already done the first, so 4 new; then 3, 2, 1: 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15. Way 2: each of the 6 high-fives 5 others, giving 6 × 5 = 30, but that counts every high five twice (once for each person in it), so 30 ÷ 2 = 15. Rule: for n astronauts, n × (n − 1) ÷ 2. Check: 2 → 1, 3 → 3, 4 → 6.
