## Core lesson: meters, centimeters, and the size of Mars {#core}

<p class="meta">35–40 minutes · Method: concrete (a meter stick or a 10 cm strip), pictorial (a place-value shift), then the written conversion · NASA figures, honestly rounded</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

<div class="figure" markdown="0">

**The meter is the SI unit of length.** This course uses the US spelling *meter*. (The BIPM’s international spelling is *metre*.) 1 meter (m) = 100 centimeters (cm) = 1,000 millimeters (mm). 1 kilometer (km) = 1,000 m.

**A conversion is a place-value shift.** To change meters to centimeters, the digits move two places toward the smaller unit (×100). To change kilometers to meters, they move three places (×1,000). You are not inventing a new kind of multiplying; you are renaming the same length.

**A sourced size.** The NASA Mars fact sheet gives Mars’s equatorial radius as 3,396.2 km, so the equatorial diameter is 2 × 3,396.2 = 6,792.4 km, which we will say as **about 6,792 km**. Earth’s equatorial diameter, from the same sheet (radius 6,378.1 km), is about **12,756 km**. The ratio 6,792.4 ÷ 12,756.2 is about **0.532**, a little more than half. “Mars is about half as wide as Earth” is a fair first sentence if you also say “a little more than half.”

A friendlier rounding, **about 6,800 km**, is to the nearest hundred kilometers and must be labeled as rounded. We will compute with 6,792 km when we want the nearer figure, and we will not invent a circumference (that waits for a later level, when the circle is taught).

**A sol**, already in the sources, is the Martian solar day: about 24 hours 39 minutes 35 seconds. That is a time, not a length; it belongs on a mission clock, not on today’s meter stick.


</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

1 km = 1,000 m; 1 m = 100 cm = 1,000 mm. Conversions are place-value shifts. Mars’s equatorial diameter is about 6,792 km (NASA fact sheet: 2 × 3,396.2). About 6,800 km is a rounding to the nearest hundred and must be labeled. Mars is a little more than half as wide as Earth (ratio about 0.532). No circumference today.

## Math circle: same length, different names {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · a short argument, not a new unit</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Two names can be the same length: 2 m and 200 cm, or 1 km and 1,000 m. A useful circle question: *How do you know they are the same, without a new kind of tool?* The reason is the definition (the place-value shift), not a measurement of Mars. If someone says 2 m is longer than 200 cm “because meters are bigger,” that confuses the size of the *unit* with the size of the *length*. A big unit can name a short length (0.5 m) or a long one (5,000 m).

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

The size of the unit is not the same as the size of the length. 2 m = 200 cm, even though a meter is a larger unit than a centimeter.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>A student says, ‘200 cm must be shorter than 2 m because centimeters are small.’ What is the mix-up, and what would you say?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>The student has mixed up the size of the unit with the size of the length.</li>
<li>200 cm is exactly 2 m, by the definition 1 m = 100 cm.</li>
<li>A small unit can still add up to a large length.</li>
</ol>

</div>

Original sentences: *Murus longus est. Via nōn est lāta.
