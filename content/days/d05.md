# Unit 1 · Day 5 · Walking the Solar System

**Today's big question:** If the Sun were a ball you could hold, where would the planets be? Today you build a true scale model, and you check what you have mastered this week.

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> the model walk needs a long straight path: a park, a quiet road with a footpath, or a beach. The inner planets fit in a garden; the outer planets need hundreds of metres. If space is short, walk as far as Jupiter and mark the rest on a map. The unit check below is short and is done without help; anything missed is reviewed on Monday before new work.</p>

## Core lesson: scale, and the model walk {#core}

<p class="meta">45 minutes (longer if you walk the whole model)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**What "to scale" means.** In a scale model, *every* length is divided by the same number. Here the Sun is a ball about **20 cm** across, and **1 cm in the model stands for 70,000 km** in space. (The Sun is 1,391,400 km across; 1,391,400 ÷ 70,000 is just under 20.)

Using NASA's values, the model is:

| Body | Model size (across) | Model distance from the Sun | Something that size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sun | 20 cm | — | a large melon or a ball |
| Mercury | 0.7 mm | 8.3 m | a grain of salt |
| Venus | 1.7 mm | 15.5 m | a peppercorn |
| Earth | 1.8 mm | 21.4 m | a peppercorn |
| Mars | 1.0 mm | 32.6 m | a pinhead |
| Jupiter | 20 mm | 111 m | a large grape or a marble |
| Saturn | 17 mm | 205 m | a slightly smaller marble |
| Uranus | 7.3 mm | 410 m | a small bead |
| Neptune | 7.1 mm | 645 m | about the same as Uranus |

The Moon would be a speck 0.5 mm across, **5.5 cm** from the Earth peppercorn.

**The walk.** First measure your own pace: walk 10 normal paces and measure the distance with a tape; divide by 10. Then work out how many paces to each planet. Place the Sun, and walk out, leaving a marker (a card with the planet's name) at each planet.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

A scale keeps every ratio the same. If 1 cm stands for 70,000 km, a real length *L* km becomes *L* ÷ 70,000 centimetres. Distances in the model subtract just as they do in space.

</div>

### Practice

1. The nearest star beyond the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about 40 trillion km away (40,000,000,000,000 km). In our model (1 cm for 70,000 km), about how far away would it be? Give your answer in kilometres.
2. In the model, Neptune is 645 m from the Sun. How long would it take to walk there at 1 metre per second? Give the answer in minutes and seconds.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. **120 s**, which is 2 minutes.

2. 645 seconds = **10 minutes 45 seconds**.
3. 8 min 20 s = 500 s. 2,140 cm ÷ 500 s ≈ **4.3 cm per second**: far slower than you walk, even though real light is the fastest thing there is.

## Math circle: the heavy meteorite {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

A **balance scale** tells you which side is heavier, or that they balance. The method is to split the group into **three** equal piles: weigh one pile against another. If one side is heavy, the heavy piece is in that pile. If they balance, the heavy piece is in the pile you left out. Each weighing cuts the search to one third.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

If you already know the odd piece is heavier, *n* weighings can handle 3^*n* pieces. Two weighings handle 9 pieces.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Three pieces, one heavy, one weighing. Weigh two of them. If one side goes down, that one is heavy. If they balance, the one left out is heavy.</p>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>Nine pieces, first weighing: how many in each pan, and how many left out?</p>

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Three piles of three</summary>

Weigh 3 against 3, leave 3 out.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Next</summary>

You now have three suspects. That is the three-piece problem you just watched.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.</p>

1. With two weighings, and knowing the odd piece is heavier, how many pieces can you handle?

</div>

### Practice

You have 9 meteorite pieces that look exactly alike. Eight weigh the same; one is slightly heavier. You have a balance scale (it only tells you which side is heavier, or that they balance). Find the heavy piece using the scale only **twice**.

When you have a method, explain why it always works, whichever piece is the heavy one. Then try: what is the largest number of pieces for which three weighings are always enough?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. **9**, because 3 × 3 = 9.

Split the 9 into three groups of 3. **Weighing 1:** group A against group B. If one side is heavier, the heavy piece is in that group; if they balance, it is in group C. Either way, you now have 3 pieces. **Weighing 2:** put one of the 3 against another. The heavier side holds it; if they balance, it is the third piece. It works because every weighing has three possible results, so it can split the candidates into three equal groups. With three weighings: 3 × 3 × 3 = **27 pieces**.
