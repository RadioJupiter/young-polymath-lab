## Core lesson: radius, diameter, and the distance around a circle {#core}

<p class="meta">35–40 minutes · Method: concrete (string around a circular lid, then compare to the diameter), pictorial (the marked circle), then C = πd with a labeled approximation of π</p>

**A circle** is the set of points at one fixed distance from a center. That distance is the **radius** *r*. The **diameter** *d* is a segment through the center, from one side of the circle to the other, and **d = 2r**.

**Circumference** is the distance around the circle. If you measure several different circles (a lid, a wheel, a drawn circle) and divide each circumference by its diameter, the quotient is a little more than 3, and it is the same number, as near as honest measuring can tell. That number is **π** (pi). π is not a terminating decimal; it is not 3.14 exactly, and it is not exactly 22/7. In this level we **compute with a labeled approximation**: usually **π ≈ 3.14**, and sometimes **π ≈ 22/7** when the diameter is a multiple of 7, because the sevens cancel cleanly. We write the approximation we used.

**The formulas.** **C = πd** and, because d = 2r, **C = 2πr**. For a circle of diameter 10 cm, using 3.14: C ≈ 3.14 × 10 = 31.4 cm. For a circle of diameter 14 cm, using 22/7: C ≈ (22/7) × 14 = 44 cm.

**Sourced sizes, now with a circumference.** Level 7 used NASA’s equatorial diameters and refused a circumference, because the circle had not been taught. Today we may compute one, if we name π and the rounding. Mars’s equatorial diameter, as this course used the NASA fact sheet, is about 6,792 km. Using π ≈ 3.14: C ≈ 3.14 × 6,792 = 21,326.88 km, which we will say as **about 21,300 km**, rounded to the nearest hundred, and we will label both the π and the rounding. Earth’s equatorial diameter is about 12,756 km. Using π ≈ 3.14: C ≈ 3.14 × 12,756 = 40,053.84 km, **about 40,050 km** (or **about 40,000 km** if we round to the nearest thousand). A closer value, using a more precise π with NASA’s equatorial radius 6,378.137 km, is about 40,075 km; that closer figure is still 2π times a NASA radius, not a new laboratory measurement of our own.

**A crater is not a perfect circle.** NASA pages for Tycho, on the Moon, give a diameter of **about 85 km**. If we *model* the rim as a circle of diameter 85 km, then with π ≈ 3.14, C ≈ 3.14 × 85 = 266.9 km, about 267 km. That is a model. A real rim is not a perfect circle, so we will not call 267 km “the measured walk around Tycho.”

## Math circle: why C/d is the same for every circle {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · a short argument, not a new unit</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Why should a lid and a planet, if both are circles, share the same C/d? The short reason is scaling. If you enlarge a circle so that every length is multiplied by the same factor k, the diameter becomes k d and the circumference becomes k C. The quotient (k C)/(k d) is still C/d. That common quotient is π. The argument assumes the two figures are the same shape (circles), honestly enlarged. It is not a laboratory proof that our 3.14 is exact.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

If all lengths scale by k, C/d does not change. That stable quotient is π. 3.14 remains an approximation.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>A circle of diameter 2 cm has some circumference C. A second circle is an exact enlargement, diameter 6 cm. What is its circumference, in terms of C, and what is the same about C/d?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>The scale factor is 3, because 6 = 3 × 2.</li>
<li>The new circumference is 3C.</li>
<li>The new C/d is 3C / 6 = C / 2, which is the same as the first C/d.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>Why does this argument not let us write “π = 3.14 exactly”?</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: What the argument gives</summary>

It gives that C/d is the same for all circles, and that number is π.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: What 3.14 is</summary>

3.14 is a rounded stand-in we chose for computing, not the exact value of that quotient.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>The point is the scaling reason, not a new formula.</p>

1. If someone measures one lid carelessly and gets C/d = 2.9, should they invent a new π for lids? Why not?
2. Why was a circumference of Mars not a different π from a circumference of a 10 cm circle?

</div>
