An **equation** says that two sides are equal. A **pan balance** is a useful picture of that. When the balance is **level**, the two pans have the same weight, in this classroom model.

The drawing shows 8 on the left and 5 + *n* on the right, and the balance is level. So:

**n + 5 = 8**

All masses in this lesson’s exercises are **labeled invented** classroom numbers, not a measured lab weight and not a NASA figure.

**Keeping both sides equal.** If you take 5 from the right pan, you must take 5 from the left pan too, or the balance will tip. In symbols: subtract 5 from both sides.

n + 5 − 5 = 8 − 5, so **n = 3**. Check: 3 + 5 = 8.

**The same habit for multiplication.** If 3n = 12, there are three equal n’s on one side, making 12. Divide both sides by 3, and n = 4. Check: 3 × 4 = 12.

Whatever you do to one side, do to the other, so the two sides stay equal.

<div class="check">

### Practice

1. A labeled invented left pan holds n + 9. The right pan holds 20, and the balance is level. Find n.
2. Why is “I moved 5 from one side only, and then divided” a miss?
3. 2(n + 3) = 14. Find n. (2(n + 3) means two lots of n + 3.)

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. Subtract 6: 4n = 20, so n = 5. Check: 4 × 5 + 6 = 26.
2. Subtract n from both sides: 7 = n + 1, so n = 6. Check: 6 + 7 = 13 and 2 × 6 + 1 = 13.

1. n + 9 = 20, so n = 11. Invented. Check: 11 + 9 = 20.
2. The two sides would no longer be equal after the first move, so the second move would be working on a different, untrue picture.
3. n + 3 = 7, so n = 4. Check: 2 × (4 + 3) = 14.

## Math circle: why both sides change together {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · a short argument, then a check of the reason</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Why must we do the same thing to both sides of an equation?

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

An equation says the two sides are equal. A move that changes only one side makes that sentence untrue.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>A friend has n + 6 = 10, takes 6 from the left only, and writes n = 10. What would you say?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>The left became n, but the right was left as 10, so the two sides are no longer the same.</li>
<li>The true move is to subtract 6 from both sides: n = 4.</li>
<li>Check: 4 + 6 = 10, not 10 + nothing.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>How does the pan-balance picture help you see the same reason?</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: A level balance</summary>

A level balance is a picture of two sides that match.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: One pan</summary>

Taking a mass from one pan only tips the balance, so the picture of “equal” is gone.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These check the reason.</p>

1. In 5n = 20, why do we divide both sides, not only the side that has n?
2. Give one reason a check (putting n back) catches a one-sided move.

</div>

### Practice

1. n − 4 = 9. What happens if you add 4 to the left only?
2. This argument is not a score or a badge.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. 5n means five equal parts that together make 20. To find one part, both sides have to be split the same way, or the equality is lost.
2. If you changed only one side, putting the number back will not give the original right-hand side.

1. The left becomes n, the right stays 9, and n = 9 would fail the check: 9 − 4 is 5, not 9. The true n is 13.
2. A note that the circle is a reason to talk through, not a reward.
