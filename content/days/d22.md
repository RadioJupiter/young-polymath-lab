**A polygon** is a closed shape made of straight sides. A **triangle** has 3 sides, a **quadrilateral** 4, a **pentagon** 5, a **hexagon** 6. A polygon is **regular** when all sides are equal and all interior angles are equal. An invented hexagonal landing pad in this lesson is a classroom shape, and must be labeled invented.

**The angles of a triangle add to 180°.** One picture: tear the three corners of a paper triangle and place them on a straight line; they fill the straight angle. Another: the triangle in the figure is right-angled at one vertex, with 40° in a second corner, so the third is 50°, because 90 + 40 + 50 = 180.

**A quadrilateral** can be cut into two triangles, so its interior angles add to 2 × 180° = 360°.

**A regular hexagon** can be split, from the center, into six equilateral triangles. Each interior angle of the hexagon is 120° (two 60° angles from two of those triangles). The same 120° comes from the general rule: the interior angle sum of an *n*-gon is (*n* − 2) × 180°, so a hexagon has (6 − 2) × 180° = 720°, and 720° ÷ 6 = 120° if it is regular. (The math circle will say why the (*n* − 2) count is the number of triangles.)

A crater rim is not a polygon: it is not made of straight sides. We will not pretend a Moon crater is a hexagon.

## Math circle: why the angle sum is (n − 2) × 180° {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · a short argument, not a new unit</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

The formula (*n* − 2) × 180° is not a memory trick. From one vertex of a simple convex n-gon you can draw diagonals to all the other vertices except itself and its two neighbors. Those diagonals cut the polygon into (*n* − 2) triangles. Each triangle’s angles add to 180°, and together they make up the interior angles of the polygon.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

A convex n-gon splits into (n − 2) triangles from one vertex. That is why the interior sum is (n − 2) × 180°.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>For a hexagon, how many triangles do you get from one vertex, and what is the angle sum?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>From one vertex you skip itself and two neighbors, so you draw diagonals to the three vertices left? A hexagon has 6 vertices; from one vertex there are 6 − 3 = 3 vertices you may join, but the number of triangles is 6 − 2 = 4.</li>
<li>Four triangles: 4 × 180° = 720°.</li>
<li>If the hexagon is regular, each interior angle is 720° ÷ 6 = 120°, which matches today’s core lesson.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>How many triangles from one vertex of a pentagon, and what is the sum?</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: The count</summary>

n − 2 = 5 − 2 = 3 triangles.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: The sum</summary>

3 × 180° = 540°.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>The point is the count of triangles, not a new formula.</p>

1. Why is a triangle the (n − 2) = 1 case, and why is that a check, not a surprise?
2. What would go wrong if you tried this cut on a crater outline?

</div>

<strong>A word to keep apart.</strong> You already know *lātus, lāta, lātum* (“wide”). Today’s *latus, lateris* (plural *latera*) means “a side.” They are different words. The macron on *lātus* (“wide”) is part of how this course writes the difference.

Original sentences: *Triāngulum tria latera habet.* *Quadrātum quattuor latera habet.* *Triāngulum nōn quattuor latera habet.*

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> no degree numbers in the Latin. Italian-voice Hear, labeled as an approximation.</p>
