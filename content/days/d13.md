# Unit 3 · Day 13 · Adding the same-size parts

**Today's big question:** If two fractions already use the same size parts, how do you add or subtract them — and what must you not add?

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> the common error is adding denominators (1/6 + 2/6 = 3/12). Use the cubes or the bar first. The 12-day month is only a model. Answers go in the math notebook; check them with the key only after the work is done.</p>

## Mental math warm-up: adding equal parts of the same whole {#mental}

<p class="meta">10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

When fractions have the **same denominator**, you are adding (or subtracting) parts of the same size. 1/6 + 2/6 is 3/6, because one sixth-part plus two sixth-parts is three sixth-parts. You add the numerators and keep the denominator. Then you can rename if you wish: 3/6 = 1/2.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Same denominator: add or subtract the numerators; the denominator stays. Then rename if there is a simpler equivalent fraction.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>What is 1/8 + 3/8? Then give a simpler name if there is one.</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>The parts are eighths, so 1 + 3 = 4 eighths: **4/8**.</li>
<li>4/8 = 1/2 (divide top and bottom by 4).</li>
<li>The sum is 4/8, or 1/2.</li>
</ol>

</div>

## Core lesson: adding and subtracting like fractions {#core}

<p class="meta">35–40 minutes · Method: a bar of equal parts, then the written rule, then gravity and phase examples</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**Like fractions** have the same denominator: the parts are the same size. To add or subtract, you count how many of those parts you have.

**1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6**, and 2/6 = 1/3. Two “Moon-pulls” on the 6-part Earth bar would be **1/3** of Earth’s pull — that is just addition of like parts, not a new fact about two moons.

**A phase-bar example.** Imagine a classroom **model month** of **12 equal days** (this is a model, not the real 29½-day synodic month). 3 days are 3/12; 3/12 + 3/12 = 6/12 = 1/2 of the model month. When you want the real month, go back to 29½ and take a fraction of that length (Day 11), rather than pretending the month has 12 days.

**The rule.** For fractions with the same denominator: a/d + b/d = (a + b)/d, and a/d − b/d = (a − b)/d (when a ≥ b, for now). Then rename if you can.

**Concrete step.** On a 6-cube train, add 1 cube and 2 cubes: 3/6 of the train, which is 1/2. Do not add the denominators (1/6 + 2/6 is not 3/12).

</div>

## Challenge problems: several like parts, and a leftover {#challenge}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · like fractions, including a leftover of a leftover later only if like parts allow it</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

You can add more than two like fractions: 1/8 + 2/8 + 3/8 = 6/8 = 3/4. You can also ask what is left: 1 − 3/8 = 8/8 − 3/8 = 5/8. Today, stay with like parts. (Unlike denominators are tomorrow.)

</div>

## Math circle: when a sum is more than one whole {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · a diagram, then a short argument</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

If you add like fractions and the numerator becomes larger than the denominator, the sum is **greater than one whole**. 5/6 + 2/6 = 7/6, which is 1 and 1/6 (an **improper fraction** and a **mixed number**). Both names are valid. A bar that is 6 parts long cannot hold 7 parts; you need a second bar for the extra sixth.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

When the numerator is larger than the denominator, the amount is more than one whole: a/d = (a ÷ d) wholes, with a remainder of parts. 7/6 = 1 1/6.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>A student says 5/6 + 2/6 is impossible because ‘you cannot have 7 parts if the bar has only 6.’ How would you answer?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>The first whole is 6/6, and 1 sixth is left over on a second whole.</li>
<li>7/6 is a correct sum; it is 1 1/6.</li>
<li>The bar was only a picture of one whole; the amount can go past it.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>Write 11/6 as a mixed number, and 2 1/6 as an improper fraction.</p>

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: 11/6</summary>

6/6 is one whole, and 11 = 6 + 5, so 1 5/6 remains after the whole.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: 2 1/6</summary>

2 wholes are 12/6, plus 1/6 is 13/6.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>The skill is going past one whole with like parts.</p>

2. Which is larger, 9/8 or 1? How do you know?

</div>
