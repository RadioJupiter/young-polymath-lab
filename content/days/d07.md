## Core lesson: multiplying by tens and by breaking apart {#core}

<p class="meta">45 minutes</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**The fact.** Earth moves along its orbit at an average of about **29.8 km per second** (NASA). We will round to 30.

**Multiplying by 10, 100, 1,000.** Each digit moves one place left for every zero: 30 × 10 = 300; 30 × 100 = 3,000. So 30 × 60 = 30 × 6 × 10 = 180 × 10 = **1,800 km in one minute**.

**One hour.** 1,800 × 60 = 18 × 6 × 1,000 = 108 × 1,000 = **108,000 km in an hour** (with the precise 29.8 km/s it is about 107,000 km).

**Break apart.** 36 × 7 = 30 × 7 + 6 × 7 = 210 + 42 = 252. The area model shows why: a 36-by-7 rectangle splits into a 30-by-7 piece and a 6-by-7 piece.

**Double and halve.** Doubling one factor and halving the other keeps the product the same: 16 × 25 = 8 × 50 = 4 × 100 = 400. (In the area model, the rectangle becomes twice as long and half as tall: same area.)

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

For a two-digit factor, split into tens and ones (the area model). When one factor is even and the other ends in 25 or 50, try double-and-halve.

</div>

## Math circle: the pigeonhole principle {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

The **pigeonhole principle**: if you put more items than the number of boxes, and every item goes in a box, then at least one box holds two or more. To be *sure* of a result in the worst case, imagine avoiding it for as long as you can, then take one more.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

To be sure of two of the same kind when there are *k* kinds, take *k* + 1 (one of each, then one more).

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Red gloves and blue gloves. How many must you take to be sure of two the same color?</p><ol class='steps'><li>Worst case: first a red, then a blue (one of each).</li><li>The next glove, a third one, must match one of them.</li><li>So 3 gloves are enough, and 2 are not always enough.</li></ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>A crew of 13 people. Why must at least two share a birth month?</p>

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Boxes</summary>

There are 12 months.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: 13 > 12</summary>

Thirteen people into 12 months means at least one month has two people.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.</p>

1. To be sure of two gloves of the same color (red or blue), how many must you take?
2. A crew of 13: why do at least two share a birth month?

</div>

### Practice

1. In a dark cupboard there are many red gloves and many blue gloves, all mixed up. How many must you take out, without looking, to be *sure* of having two of the same color?
2. A crew has 13 people. Prove that at least two of them have birthdays in the same month.
3. Is it true that among any 4 whole numbers, two of them have a difference that divides exactly by 3? Explain.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. **3** (one of each color, then one more).
2. 13 people and 12 months: at least one month has two people.

1. **Three.** Two might be one of each color; a third must match one of them.
2. There are 12 months (the "pigeonholes") and 13 people (the "pigeons"). If every month had at most one person, there would be at most 12 people. So some month has two. ∎
3. **Yes.** Every whole number leaves a remainder of 0, 1 or 2 when divided by 3: three pigeonholes. Four numbers means two share a remainder, and the difference of two numbers with the same remainder divides exactly by 3 (the remainders cancel). ∎

## Notebook: a clock for Mars {#notebook}

<p class="meta">60 minutes · invent, then three connected sentences · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html) · [Review sheet](../printables/d07-notebook.html)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

A day on Mars (a **sol**) lasts about **24 hours 39 minutes 35 seconds**, often rounded to 24 hours 40 minutes — about 40 minutes longer than an Earth day. If people on Mars kept a 24-hour clock with Earth-length hours, the clock would drift later by about 40 minutes each day. A Mars clock could instead keep 24 hours and make each hour a little longer, or keep extra minutes at the end of the day. Your job is to choose a design and show why it works.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

One sol ≈ 24 h 40 min. That extra 40 minutes is what a Mars clock must do something with.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>After three sols, a clock that ignored the extra time would be about 3 × 40 = 120 minutes (2 hours) behind the sky on Mars. That is why engineers who once lived on 'Mars time' shifted their day later each Earth day.</p>

### Practice

<p class="note"><strong>How the guide helps today:</strong> the student should invent the design, not be given one. For writing, aim at three short sentences that go together: what the design is, one advantage, one problem. Number them 1, 2, 3 if that helps. Oral rehearsal first, as always.</p>

A day on Mars (a **sol**) lasts about 24 hours and 40 minutes: a little longer than ours. Engineers running rovers on Mars have lived on "Mars time", shifting their working day 40 minutes later each day.

**Invent:** design a clock face, or a calendar, for people living on Mars. Decide: would you keep 24 hours and make each one a little longer, or have 24 ordinary hours plus a 40-minute "extra" time?

**1. Draw** your clock or calendar, large, with labels for the hours (and the extra time, if you use it).

**2. Label** the parts from the word bank, plus any words you need (the guide may write extra words for copying).

**Word bank:** Mars · sol · hour · minute · clock · calendar · Earth time · extra · advantage · problem

**3. Write** three connected sentences (say each one first):

- My Mars clock ________.
- One advantage is ________.
- One problem is ________.

**4. Numbers.** After 3 sols, how far behind Earth time would a Mars-time worker be? After 9 sols?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. About **40 minutes** (more precisely 39 minutes 35 seconds).

After 3 sols: 3 × 40 = 120 minutes = **2 hours** behind. After 9 sols: 360 minutes = **6 hours**. (A precise sol is 24 h 39 min 35 s.) Any design that is explained clearly with one real advantage and one real drawback is a good answer. The three sentences should connect (they are about the same design).
