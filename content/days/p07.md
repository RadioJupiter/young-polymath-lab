## Core lesson: adding with regrouping, and a part-whole bar {#core}

<p class="meta">45 minutes · concrete → pictorial → abstract</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>


When ones make 10 or more, they make a new ten. That is **regrouping** (or trading), the same idea as making ten, now written in columns.

**285 + 347.**

Concrete: 5 ones + 7 ones = 12 ones, which is 1 ten and 2 ones. 8 tens + 4 tens + 1 ten = 13 tens, which is 1 hundred and 3 tens. 2 hundreds + 3 hundreds + 1 hundred = 6 hundreds. The sum is **632**.

**Pictorial: a part-whole bar.** One bar is split into the two parts; a bracket names the whole.

<div class="barmodel">
<div class="row"><span class="lbl">parts</span><span class="seg" style="width:45%">285</span><span class="seg u" style="width:55%">347</span></div>
<div class="brace">whole = ?</div>
</div>


**Abstract: columns.** Line up the places. Add ones first.

```
    285
  + 347
  -----
    632
```

Ones: 5 + 7 = 12, write 2, carry 1 ten. Tens: 8 + 4 + 1 = 13, write 3, carry 1 hundred. Hundreds: 2 + 3 + 1 = 6. **632**.

Estimate: 300 + 350 = 650, so 632 is reasonable.

An invented story, labeled invented: 285 cards in one box and 347 in another. The total is the whole. This is not a NASA count.

<p class="note">The Saturn V height used in this level is from NASA, “What Was the Saturn V?”: the Saturn V rocket was 111 meters (363 feet) tall. https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-was-the-saturn-v/ Classroom rocket heights (for example 63 m, and 18 m shorter) and classroom shadow lengths are invented and labeled invented. They are not official NASA measurements. This level does not average 111 m with any other figure.</p>


</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Add ones first. When a column makes 10 or more, write the ones and carry 1 to the next place. A part-whole bar shows two parts and the whole. 285 + 347 = 632. Estimate to check.

</div>

## Math circle: why we carry, and why we estimate {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · one rich problem, solved and then explained</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Carrying is not a trick. 12 ones are 1 ten and 2 ones, because ten ones make a ten. An estimate (300 + 350 = 650) does not replace the exact sum, but it can catch a miss such as 5,312.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Carry when a place makes 10 or more. Estimate to see whether an answer is reasonable, not as a second official sum.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Why do we write 2 and carry 1 when the ones are 5 + 7, instead of writing 12 in the ones place?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>12 ones are 1 ten and 2 ones.</li>
<li>The 1 ten belongs in the tens place, so we carry it. Writing 12 in the ones place would break the places.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>A student gets 522 for 285 + 347, having forgotten a carry. How does an estimate show that 522 is not reasonable?</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

300 + 350 = 650, or 280 + 350 = 630.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

522 is about 100 less than that estimate, so a carry was likely missed.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These defend carrying and estimating.</p>

1. What does an estimate not claim, in this lesson?
2. Why is a part-whole bar a good picture for 285 + 347?

</div>

### Practice

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. It is not a second official exact sum. It is a nearby round calculation to check reasonableness.
2. The two numbers are parts that go together to make one whole, the sum.

1. Ones 5 + 7 = 12, write 2, carry 1 ten; tens 8 + 4 + 1 = 13, write 3, carry 1 hundred; hundreds 2 + 3 + 1 = 6. The sum is 632.
2. It is an invented classroom story for the same addition. Official NASA figures in this level are labeled from the Saturn V page; invented counts are labeled invented.
