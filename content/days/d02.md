# Unit 1 · Day 2 · Ordering the planets

**Today's big question:** The planets are at wildly different distances from the Sun. How do we compare huge numbers quickly, and when is a rounded number good enough?

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> rounding is taught with a number line, not a rule to memorise. If the student can say *why* 12,756 rounds to 13,000 (it is closer to 13,000 than to 12,000), the rule follows.</p>

## Mental math warm-up {#mental}

<p class="meta">10 minutes</p>

Which is larger? Say how you know without working anything out on paper.
1. 108,200 or 108,020
2. 4,515 or 4,551
3. 99,999 or 100,000
4. 778,500 or 77,850
5. What number is halfway between 12,000 and 13,000?
6. What number is halfway between 300,000 and 400,000?

### Answer key

1. 108,200 (same until the hundreds: 2 > 0) · 2. 4,551 (tens: 5 > 1) · 3. 100,000 (more digits) · 4. 778,500 (more digits) · 5. 12,500 · 6. 350,000

## Core lesson: comparing and rounding big numbers {#core}

<p class="meta">45 minutes</p>

**The facts.** Average distance of each planet from the Sun, rounded to the nearest million kilometres (from NASA's planetary fact sheet):

| Planet | Distance from the Sun (million km) |
|---|---|
| Mercury | 58 |
| Venus | 108 |
| Earth | 150 |
| Mars | 228 |
| Jupiter | 779 |
| Saturn | 1,432 |
| Uranus | 2,867 |
| Neptune | 4,515 |

**Comparing.** A number with more digits is larger. With the same number of digits, compare from the left: the first place where the digits differ decides.

**Rounding (concrete → pictorial).** Draw a number line from 12,000 to 13,000 and mark the halfway point, 12,500. Mark Earth's diameter, 12,756. It lies past the halfway point, so it is **closer to 13,000**: 12,756 rounded to the nearest thousand is 13,000. A number exactly at the halfway point is rounded up by convention.

**Abstract.** To round to a place, look at the digit just to its right: 5 or more rounds up, 4 or less rounds down. (This is the number line, written as a rule.)

**When to round.** NASA's fact sheet lists these distances to only three or four digits, and each planet's distance from the Sun changes as it moves along its oval orbit, so extra digits would not mean much. A rounded number is not "wrong"; it is honest about how precisely we know or need the value.

### Practice

1. Order these diameters from smallest to largest: Mars 6,792 km; Mercury 4,879 km; Venus 12,104 km; Earth 12,756 km.
2. Round Earth's diameter (12,756 km) to the nearest ten, the nearest hundred and the nearest thousand.
3. Round the Moon's distance (384,400 km) to the nearest ten thousand and the nearest hundred thousand.
4. Which is closer to 50,000: Neptune's diameter (49,528 km) or Uranus's (51,118 km)?
5. How many planets are closer than 200 million km to the Sun? How many are farther than 1,000 million?

### Answer key

1. Mercury, Mars, Venus, Earth
2. 12,760; 12,800; 13,000
3. 380,000; 400,000
4. Neptune: 50,000 − 49,528 = 472, but 51,118 − 50,000 = 1,118.
5. Three closer than 200 million (Mercury, Venus, Earth); three farther than 1,000 million (Saturn, Uranus, Neptune). 1,000 million is one **billion**.

## Challenge problems {#challenge}

<p class="meta">25 minutes</p>

1. A secret number rounds to 380,000 (to the nearest ten thousand) and to 400,000 (to the nearest hundred thousand). What could it be? Is the Moon's distance a possibility?
2. What is the smallest whole number that rounds to 13,000 (nearest thousand)? The largest?
3. About how many times farther from the Sun is Jupiter than Earth? Use rounded numbers and explain.

### Answer key

1. Rounding to 380,000 means it is from 375,000 up to 384,999. Rounding to 400,000 means from 350,000 up to 449,999. Both are true from **375,000 to 384,999**. Yes: 384,400 is in that range.
2. Smallest **12,500**; largest **13,499**.
3. About **5 times**: 150 × 5 = 750, close to 779. (A more precise answer is about 5.2.)

## Math circle: the planet puzzle {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · logic: use every clue, and prove your answer is the only one</p>

**Puzzle 1.** Four model planets (Mars, Venus, Earth, Jupiter) sit on a shelf in a row of four places. Jupiter is at the right end. Earth is not at either end. Venus is next to Jupiter. Mars is somewhere to the left of Venus. Where is each?

**Puzzle 2.** Five space probes, A, B, C, D and E, were launched on five different weekdays, Monday to Friday. C was launched on Wednesday. B was launched the day after A. E was launched before A. D was launched before E. On which day was each launched?

### Answer key

**Puzzle 1.** Jupiter is in place 4, so Venus is in place 3. Earth is not at an end, so Earth is in place 2, and Mars takes place 1 (to the left of Venus, as required). **Mars, Earth, Venus, Jupiter.**

**Puzzle 2.** C is Wednesday. A and B are on two days in a row that avoid Wednesday: Monday–Tuesday or Thursday–Friday. If A were Monday, nothing could be before it, but E must be. So A is **Thursday** and B **Friday**. D and E take Monday and Tuesday, and D is before E: **D Monday, E Tuesday**.

## Notebook: the Solar System on a strip {#notebook}

<p class="meta">60 minutes</p>

Make a scale strip of planet distances from the Sun using **1 cm for every 20 million km**. To find each length: divide the distance in millions by 10, then halve it. (Example: Earth, 150 → 15 → **7.5 cm**.)

Use a long strip of paper, or tape sheets together. You need well over 2 metres. Mark the Sun at one end, then each planet. Label each mark with the planet's name and real distance.

Then, in your notebook: sketch the strip in miniature, and write what you notice about where the planets are crowded together and where the gaps are huge.

### Answer key

Positions from the Sun: Mercury 2.9 cm · Venus 5.4 cm · Earth 7.5 cm · Mars 11.4 cm · Jupiter 38.9 cm · Saturn 71.6 cm · Uranus 143.4 cm · Neptune 225.8 cm. (Using the unrounded NASA distances: 57.9, 108.2, 149.6, 228.0, 778.5, 1,432.0, 2,867.0 and 4,515.0 million km.) The four inner planets crowd into the first 12 cm, then the gaps grow enormously.
