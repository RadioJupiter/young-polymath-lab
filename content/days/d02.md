# Unit 1 · Day 2 · Ordering the planets

**Today's big question:** The planets are at wildly different distances from the Sun. How do we compare huge numbers quickly, and when is a rounded number good enough?

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> rounding is taught with a number line, not a rule to memorise. If the student can say *why* 12,756 rounds to 13,000 (it is closer to 13,000 than to 12,000), the rule follows.</p>

## Mental math warm-up {#mental}

<p class="meta">10 minutes</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

To compare two numbers, line them up by place and **look from the left** (the largest place first). The first place that differs tells you which is larger. A number with more digits is larger (among whole numbers). Halfway between two numbers is their average, or the lower number plus half the gap.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Compare from the left. Halfway from *A* to *B* is *A* + (*B*−*A*) ÷ 2.

</div>

## Core lesson: comparing and rounding big numbers {#core}

<p class="meta">45 minutes</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**The facts.** Average distance of each planet from the Sun, rounded to the nearest million kilometers (from NASA's planetary fact sheet):

| Planet | Distance from the Sun (million km) |
|---|---|
| Mercury | 58 |
| Venus | 108 |
| Earth | 150 |
| Mars | 228 |
| Jupiter | 779 |
| Saturn | 1,432 |
| Uranus | 2,867 |
| Neptune | 4,515 |

**Comparing.** A number with more digits is larger. With the same number of digits, compare from the left: the first place where the digits differ decides.

**Rounding (concrete → pictorial).** Draw a number line from 12,000 to 13,000 and mark the halfway point, 12,500. Mark Earth's diameter, 12,756. It lies past the halfway point, so it is **closer to 13,000**: 12,756 rounded to the nearest thousand is 13,000. A number exactly at the halfway point is rounded up by convention.

**Abstract.** To round to a place, look at the digit just to its right: 5 or more rounds up, 4 or less rounds down. (This is the number line, written as a rule.)

**When to round.** NASA's fact sheet lists these distances to only three or four digits, and each planet's distance from the Sun changes as it moves along its oval orbit, so extra digits would not mean much. A rounded number is not "wrong"; it is honest about how precisely we know or need the value.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Compare from the left. To round to a place, look at the digit immediately to its right: 0–4 stay, 5–9 go up. Round only when a table, a model, or an estimate needs fewer places — not as a drill for its own sake.

</div>

### Practice

1. Use place value, comparing from the left. Which is wider, Venus (12,104 km) or Earth (12,756 km)? Mercury (4,879 km) or Mars (6,792 km)? Then list the four from narrowest to widest.
2. A table of planet diameters will only show whole thousands of kilometers. What do you write for Earth's diameter (12,756 km)? Why that thousand, and not 12,000?
3. A scale model only needs the Moon's distance (384,400 km) to the nearest 100,000 km. What number do you use? Why is that not a mistake?
4. Which is closer to 50,000 km: Neptune's diameter (49,528 km) or Uranus's (51,118 km)? Show how far each is from 50,000.
5. How many planets are closer than 200 million km to the Sun? How many are farther than 1,000 million?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. **778,500** (six digits against five).
2. **5,000 km** (the hundreds digit is 8).

1. Earth is wider than Venus (same ten-thousands, then 2 thousands vs 1). Mars is wider than Mercury (6 thousands vs 4). Narrowest to widest: **Mercury, Mars, Venus, Earth**.
2. **13,000 km**. 12,756 is 256 km from 13,000 and 744 km from 12,000, so it is nearer 13,000 (or: the hundreds digit is 7, which is 5 or more).
3. **400,000 km**. The model only has room for that much detail; 384,400 is nearer 400,000 than 300,000. The real distance has not changed.
4. Neptune: 50,000 − 49,528 = 472 km away. Uranus: 51,118 − 50,000 = 1,118 km away. **Neptune** is closer.
5. Three closer than 200 million (Mercury, Venus, Earth); three farther than 1,000 million (Saturn, Uranus, Neptune).

1. A secret number rounds to 380,000 (to the nearest ten thousand) and to 400,000 (to the nearest hundred thousand). What could it be? Is the Moon's distance a possibility?
2. What is the smallest whole number that rounds to 13,000 (nearest thousand)? The largest?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. **384,999** (385,000 would already go to 390,000).

## Math circle: the planet puzzle {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · logic: use every clue, and prove your answer is the only one</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

These are **logic-grid** puzzles. Write the places (or days) in a row. Use each clue once. Cross out what cannot be true. A good heuristic is: start with the clue that pins a thing to one exact place.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Start with the firm placement. Then apply 'next to', 'not at the end', and 'to the left of' one at a time. Check that every clue is still true at the end.

</div>

### Practice

**Puzzle 1.** Four model planets (Mars, Venus, Earth, Jupiter) sit on a shelf in a row of four places. Jupiter is at the right end. Earth is not at either end. Venus is next to Jupiter. Mars is somewhere to the left of Venus. Where is each?

**Puzzle 2.** Five space probes, A, B, C, D and E, were launched on five different weekdays, Monday to Friday. C was launched on Wednesday. B was launched the day after A. E was launched before A. D was launched before E. On which day was each launched?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. The two middle seats (2 and 3). Venus already claims seat 3, so Earth is in seat 2. (You will finish the row in the puzzle.)

**Puzzle 1.** Jupiter is in place 4, so Venus is in place 3. Earth is not at an end, so Earth is in place 2, and Mars takes place 1 (to the left of Venus, as required). **Mars, Earth, Venus, Jupiter.**

**Puzzle 2.** C is Wednesday. A and B are on two days in a row that avoid Wednesday: Monday–Tuesday or Thursday–Friday. If A were Monday, nothing could be before it, but E must be. So A is **Thursday** and B **Friday**. D and E take Monday and Tuesday, and D is before E: **D Monday, E Tuesday**.

### Practice

<p class="note"><strong>How the guide helps today:</strong> help with the long paper strip and the divisions if needed. For writing, the student speaks each stem before writing. Keep the word bank in view. One careful noticing sentence is enough; do not push for a paragraph.</p>

Make a scale strip of planet distances from the Sun using **1 cm for every 20 million km**. To find each length: divide the distance in millions by 10, then halve it. (Example: Earth, 150 → 15 → **7.5 cm**.)

Use a long strip of paper, or tape sheets together. You need well over 2 meters. Mark the Sun at one end, then each planet. Label each mark with the planet's name and real distance.

**1. Draw.** In the notebook, sketch the strip in miniature (it will be cramped; that is part of what you will notice).

**2. Label** the Sun and each planet from the word bank.

**Word bank:** Sun · Mercury · Venus · Earth · Mars · Jupiter · Saturn · Uranus · Neptune · million · kilometer · inner planets · gap · scale

**3. Write.** Say each sentence aloud, then write it.

- The four inner planets are ________.
- The biggest gaps are ________.

Then finish: *I notice that ________.*

**4. Numbers.** Write the eight model lengths you marked on the strip.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. 108 ÷ 20 = **5.4 cm**.

Positions from the Sun: Mercury 2.9 cm · Venus 5.4 cm · Earth 7.5 cm · Mars 11.4 cm · Jupiter 38.9 cm · Saturn 71.6 cm · Uranus 143.4 cm · Neptune 225.8 cm. (Using the unrounded NASA distances: 57.9, 108.2, 149.6, 228.0, 778.5, 1,432.0, 2,867.0 and 4,515.0 million km.) The four inner planets crowd into the first 12 cm, then the gaps grow enormously. Stems should say that (in the student's words).
