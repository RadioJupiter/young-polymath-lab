# Unit 8 · Day 38 · A pattern in a table, and planetary years

**Today's big question:** How do we describe a pattern, and what pattern do the planets’ years show?

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> A classroom pattern has a rule you can say and check. The NASA Planetary Fact Sheet (page last updated 13 November 2026) lists distance from the Sun and orbital period. The honest pattern is: larger distance, longer year. Do not invent a formula or an orbital speed. The Moon’s 27.3-day period and 0.384 million km distance are asterisked on the sheet: they are about the Moon and Earth, not a year around the Sun. Current sheet figures used: Mercury 57.9 and 88.0; Venus 108.2 and 224.7; Earth 149.6 and 365.2; Mars 228.0 and 687.0; Jupiter 778.5 and 4331 (distances in 10^6 km; periods in days).</p>

## Core lesson: a pattern in a table, and planetary years {#core}

<p class="meta">40 minutes · Method: a rule in a table, then a sourced NASA pattern, without inventing a formula</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>


A **pattern** is a rule you can say and use. One way to show it is a table: a position, or an input, and the number that belongs with it.

**A classroom pattern first.** The rule “start at 4, add 5 each time” gives:

1 → 4,  2 → 9,  3 → 14,  4 → 19.

The 5th number is 24. We can write the 1st number as 4, and the nth as 4 + 5(n − 1), if we want a short rule, and we can check it on a number we already have: when n = 3, 4 + 5 × 2 = 14.

**A pattern in planetary data.** The NASA Planetary Fact Sheet lists each planet’s mean distance from the Sun and its orbital period (how many days that planet takes to go once around the Sun).

The Moon also appears on that sheet, with a 27.3-day period and a 0.384 million km distance, both marked with an asterisk: those figures are about the Moon and Earth, not a year around the Sun, so they are not mixed into this pattern.</p>
</div>

**The pattern we can honestly say.** As the distance from the Sun gets larger, the orbital period gets longer. Mercury, closest in this list, has the shortest year here (88.0 days). Jupiter, farthest in this list, has the longest (4331 days). The numbers in between follow that same order.

**What we will not do.** We will not invent a formula that claims to give every period from the distance, and we will not invent an orbital speed. The table is already the sourced record. A homemade rule that “fits” two planets would not automatically be a NASA law.
