# Unit 2 · Day 9 · Messages at the speed of light

**Today's big question:** When you look at the Moon, you see it as it was more than a second ago. When you look at the Sun's light, it left more than eight minutes ago. How do we know, and what does a swinging weight have to do with time?

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> today joins division with physics. The pendulum experiment needs string, a small weight (a washer, nut or bag of coins), tape and a stopwatch. ## Core lesson: light-time, and the pendulum {#core}

<p class="meta">45 minutes</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**Light-time.** Light travels about **300,000 km every second** (the exact value is 299,792,458 metres per second). To find how long light takes to cross a distance, divide the distance by 300,000. A shortcut: divide by 3, then by 100,000.

- Moon: 384,400 ÷ 300,000 ≈ **1.3 seconds**.
- Sun: about 150,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = **500 seconds = 8 minutes 20 seconds**.

So a telescope is a time machine: the farther away something is, the older the light you see from it.

**The pendulum (experiment).** Galileo noticed that a pendulum's swing takes the same time whether the swing is wide or narrow (as long as it is not too wide). Test two questions: does the **weight** change the time of a swing? Does the **length**?

1. Tie a weight to a string. Measure the length from the pivot to the middle of the weight: start at 25 cm. Tape the top to a table edge.
2. Pull the weight a little to one side (a small angle) and let go. Time **10 complete swings** (away and back is one swing). Divide by 10.
3. Repeat with lengths 50 cm and 100 cm. Then repeat one length with twice the weight.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Time = distance ÷ speed. Light travels at about 300,000 km per second. Moon light-time ≈ 384,400 ÷ 300,000 ≈ 1.3 s. Sun light-time ≈ 150,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = 500 s = 8 min 20 s.

## Math circle: the two sand timers {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Two sand timers measure intervals you can add and subtract. The method is to start both together and use the moment one finishes as a new starting mark. The **difference** of 7 and 4 is 3 minutes of leftover sand in the 7-timer; that leftover is a new interval you can use.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Start together. When the shorter timer ends, the longer still holds the difference. Turning a timer over reuses a known interval.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>How to measure 3 minutes with a 7-minute and a 4-minute timer.</p><ol class='steps'><li>Start both at once.</li><li>When the 4-minute timer ends, 3 minutes of sand remain in the 7-minute timer.</li><li>Flip the 7-minute timer (or just watch those 3 minutes run out) to mark 3 minutes from that moment, or use the leftover 3 directly.</li></ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>Start both timers at 0. When the 4-minute timer ends, how much sand is left in the 7-minute timer? How could that leftover help you make 9?</p>

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Leftover</summary>

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Use</summary>

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

### Practice

You have a 7-minute sand timer and a 4-minute sand timer. How can you measure exactly 9 minutes? You may turn the timers over whenever you like, and you start them whenever you choose.

Write your method as a timeline (0 min, 4 min, …), and check it step by step. Then: can you measure exactly 1 minute? 2 minutes?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. **3 minutes** of sand left.
2. **4 minutes**, and you have also isolated a **3-minute** interval.

**9 minutes.** Start both timers at 0.
- At 4 min the 4-timer empties: turn it over.
- At 7 min the 7-timer empties: turn it over straight away.
- At 8 min the 4-timer empties again. The 7-timer has now run for 1 minute since it was turned, so 1 minute of sand sits in its bottom bulb: turn it over. That 1 minute runs out at **9 min**.

**1 minute:** in the plan above, the time from 7 min (7-timer empties) to 8 min (4-timer empties) is exactly 1 minute.

**2 minutes:** start both at 0 and keep turning each timer over the moment it empties. The 4-timer empties at 4, 8 and 12; the 7-timer at 7 and 14. From 12 to 14 is exactly 2 minutes. In the same way, because 4 and 7 have no common factor, combinations of their runs can measure any whole number of minutes.
