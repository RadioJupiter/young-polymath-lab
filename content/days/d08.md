# Unit 2 · Day 8 · Years on other worlds

**Today's big question:** A year on Mars lasts 687 Earth days. How many weeks is that? How many full Moons fit in an Earth year? Division answers "how many groups?" and "how many in each group?"

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> division has two meanings, and both should be acted out with counters: **sharing** (12 counters shared among 3 people: how many each?) and **grouping** (12 counters in groups of 3: how many groups?). Remainders must be interpreted in context: sometimes you round up, sometimes you drop them, sometimes they are the answer.</p>

## Core lesson: division and remainders {#core}

<p class="meta">45 minutes</p>

**The facts.** Mars takes about **687 Earth days** to go once around the Sun. The Moon goes through its full cycle of phases (new moon to new moon) in about **29.5 days**.

**1. Concrete.** Take 23 counters. Put them in groups of 7. How many full groups? How many left over? Write it: 23 ÷ 7 = 3 remainder 2. Check: 3 × 7 + 2 = 23.

**2. Pictorial.** For 687 ÷ 7, think in chunks: 7 × 90 = 630 (take away 630, leaving 57); 7 × 8 = 56 (leaving 1). So 90 + 8 = 98 groups with 1 left over.

**3. Abstract: short division.** Divide place by place, from the left, carrying remainders to the right:

```
   0 9 8  r 1
7 ) 6 8 7
```

7 into 6 does not go (0), carry 6 to make 68. 7 into 68 is 9 (63), remainder 5, carry to make 57. 7 into 57 is 8 (56), remainder 1. **687 days = 98 weeks and 1 day.**

**Check every division by multiplying back:** 98 × 7 + 1 = 687. ✓

### Practice

1. 365 ÷ 7: how many weeks and days are in a 365-day year?
2. Mercury's year is 88 Earth days. How many Mercury years pass in 352 Earth days?
3. 1,000 ÷ 8
4. A mission packs 240 meals for 8 days. How many meals per day?
5. 50 people are going to a rocket launch in vans that seat 8. How many vans are needed? (Careful with the remainder.)
6. Roughly how many full Moons happen in a 365-day year? Use 30 days for the cycle, then think about whether the true 29.5 changes the answer.

### Answer key

1. **52 weeks and 1 day**
2. **4**
3. **125**
4. **30**
5. 50 ÷ 8 = 6 remainder 2. Six vans leave 2 people behind, so **7 vans**.
6. 365 ÷ 30 is about 12, with 5 days left over. With 29.5 days the cycle fits about 12.4 times, so usually **12**, and in some years **13** (when the first full Moon falls in roughly the first ten days of January).

## Challenge problems {#challenge}

<p class="meta">25 minutes</p>

1. This year, a date falls on a Tuesday. Next year is not a leap year. On what day will the same date fall next year? Explain using division.
2. Find the smallest whole number greater than 1 that leaves a remainder of 1 when divided by 2, by 3, by 4, by 5 and by 6.
3. A year on Saturn lasts about 10,747 Earth days. About how many Earth years is that? (Use 365 days in a year; first estimate, then calculate to one decimal place or as "a bit more than ___".)

### Answer key

1. 365 = 52 × 7 + 1: exactly 52 weeks plus 1 day, so the weekday moves one day on: **Wednesday**.
2. The number minus 1 must divide by 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6. The smallest such number (apart from 0) is 60. So the answer is **61**.
3. Estimate: 365 × 30 = 10,950, a bit more than 10,747, so a bit under 30. Calculation: 365 × 29 = 10,585, leaving 162 days, which is under half a year. **About 29 and a half Earth years** (29.4).

## Math circle: secret tests for dividing {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes</p>

Mathematicians have quick tests to see whether a number divides exactly, without dividing:
- by **2**: the last digit is even;
- by **5**: the last digit is 0 or 5;
- by **4**: the number formed by the last two digits divides by 4;
- by **3**: the sum of the digits divides by 3;
- by **9**: the sum of the digits divides by 9.

1. Test 384,400 for division by 2, 3, 4, 5 and 9.
2. Test 1,391,400 (the Sun's diameter in km) for 3 and 9.
3. Investigate: pick any three-digit number, and subtract the sum of its digits from it. Try five numbers. What do you notice about the answers? Can you explain why?

### Answer key

1. Digit sum 3 + 8 + 4 + 4 = 19. By 2: **yes**. By 3: **no** (19). By 4: **yes** (00). By 5: **yes**. By 9: **no**.
2. Digit sum 1 + 3 + 9 + 1 + 4 = 18. By 3: **yes**. By 9: **yes**.
3. The answer always divides by 9. For a number with digits a, b, c, its value is 100a + 10b + c; subtracting a + b + c leaves 99a + 9b, and both 99 and 9 are multiples of 9. (This is *why* the digit-sum test works.)

## Notebook: predicting the full Moon {#notebook}

<p class="meta">60 minutes · draw, label, *because / but / so* · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html) · [Printable sheets](../printables/d08.html)</p>

<p class="note"><strong>How the guide helps today:</strong> the Sun–Earth–Moon drawing is the hard thinking. Help the student shade the half of the Moon that faces *away from the Sun* in each position, then ask "from Earth, which of those looks full?" Do not answer first. Use *because / but / so* on "A full Moon happens".</p>

Look back at your Moon journal. Mark any day the Moon looked full, or nearly full. If you have one, add 29 or 30 days to predict the next full Moon, and write your prediction with a date. If you have not seen a full Moon yet, find the date of the last one from an almanac or NASA's Moon pages and do the same.

Then **explain with a drawing**: the Moon makes no light of its own; it reflects sunlight. Draw the Sun on one side of the page, Earth in the middle, and the Moon in four positions around Earth. Shade the half of the Moon that faces away from the Sun.

**1. Draw** that four-position diagram, large.

**2. Label** Sun, Earth, Moon, full, new, and the two quarter positions if you know them.

**Word bank:** Sun · Earth · Moon · full · new · quarter · reflects · sunlight · shade · prediction · opposite

**3. Write.**

- A full Moon happens when ________.
- My predicted date for the next full Moon is ________.

Then:

- A full Moon happens *because* ________.
- A full Moon happens *but* ________.
- A full Moon happens *so* ________.

**4.** Which position gives a full Moon seen from Earth? Mark it on the drawing.

### Answer key

Full Moon happens when the Moon is on the opposite side of Earth from the Sun, so the whole lit half faces us. New Moon is when the Moon is between Earth and the Sun, with its lit half facing away. The half-lit "quarter" Moons are in between. A prediction 29–30 days after the last full Moon should be correct to within a day. Sample *but*: *but the Moon is not itself a light*. Sample *so*: *so I can predict the next one about 29 or 30 days later*.
