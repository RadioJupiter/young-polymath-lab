# Unit 5 · Day 21 · Angles, and a turn

**Today's big question:** How do we name a turn in degrees, and what facts hold on a straight line and around a point?

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> Two strips or a door help. An angle is a turn, not a length. Do not invent a real telescope pointing or a sky coordinate. Vertically opposite angles are equal; the math circle gives a short reason.</p>

## Core lesson: angles, and a turn {#core}

<p class="meta">35–40 minutes · Method: concrete (two strips or a door), pictorial (a drawing with a marked turn), then the degree facts · no protractor required for today’s numbers</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**An angle is a turn** between two rays (or two line segments) that share a point, the vertex. We measure the turn in **degrees**. A full turn is **360°**. A **straight angle** is 180°. A **right angle** is 90°.

**Names by size.** An **acute** angle is greater than 0° and less than 90°. An **obtuse** angle is greater than 90° and less than 180°. The picture shows a 35° angle (acute) next to a 145° angle (obtuse). They sit on the straight line ABC, and 35° + 145° = 180°.

**Three facts you will use.**
1. Angles on a straight line add to 180°.
2. Angles around a point add to 360°.
3. When two lines cross, the **vertically opposite** angles are equal (the ones that face each other, not the ones that sit side by side).

A telescope, or a door, is pointed in a direction. When you turn it, the change is an angle. Today we do not invent a real sky coordinate or a mission pointing; we learn the turn itself.


</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Angle = a turn, in degrees. Full turn 360°, straight 180°, right 90°. Acute: between 0° and 90°. Obtuse: between 90° and 180°. Straight line: the two angles add to 180°. Around a point: they add to 360°. Vertically opposite angles are equal.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>In the picture, one angle on the straight line is 35°. What is the other, and why?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>The two angles on the straight line add to 180°.</li>
<li>180° − 35° = 145°.</li>
<li>35° is acute; 145° is obtuse. They belong together on one straight line.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>Two lines cross. One of the angles is 70°. What is the angle opposite it, and what is an angle next to it?</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Opposite</summary>

Vertically opposite angles are equal, so the opposite angle is 70°.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: Adjacent</summary>

A 70° angle and its neighbor sit on a straight line, so the neighbor is 180° − 70° = 110°.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These use only the turn facts taught today.</p>

1. An angle of 50° sits on a straight line with another angle. Find the other angle, and name both (acute or obtuse).
2. Three equal angles meet around a point, with no leftover space. How large is each?

</div>

### Practice

1. Two lines cross. One angle is 125°. Find the other three angles.
2. Can a right angle sit on a straight line with an acute angle? Say why, with degrees.
3. A door opens 90°, then 30° more in the same direction. What is the total turn, and is the total acute, right, or obtuse?
4. Draw two lines that cross, mark one angle 40°, and fill in the other three.
5. Why is “a full turn is 360°” a definition we are using, not a measurement of Mars or of a crater?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. 130°, obtuse; 50° is acute.
2. 360° ÷ 3 = 120° each.

1. The opposite angle is 125°. The two adjacent angles are 55° each (180° − 125°). Check: 125 + 55 + 125 + 55 = 360.
2. No. A right angle is 90°, so its partner on a straight line would be 90°, which is right, not acute.
3. 120°, obtuse (greater than 90° and less than 180°).
4. A crossing with 40° opposite 40°, and the other pair 140° and 140°.
5. 360° is the agreed size of a full turn in this degree system. It is not a NASA figure and not a length.

</details>
<details>

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These stay with today’s turn facts.</p>

1. Two lines cross. The adjacent (next-door) angles are in the ratio 1 to 3. Find all four angles.
2. A telescope is turned 40°, then turned back 15° the other way. What is the net turn from the start, and why is that not a distance on the Moon?

</div>

### Practice

1. Can three acute angles add to a straight angle? Give an example, or say why not.
2. If someone writes “the angle is 90 km,” what has gone wrong?
3. Draw a straight line and a ray that cuts it, with one angle 2/5 of 180°. Find both angles.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. The adjacent pair add to 180° and are 1:3, so 45° and 135°. The four angles are 45°, 135°, 45°, 135°.
2. A net turn of 25° from the start. A turn is not a length, so it is not a distance across a crater or across the Moon.

1. Yes: for example 50° + 60° + 70° = 180°, and each is acute.
2. 90° is a turn, not a length. Kilometers measure distance. The unit does not match the job.
3. 2/5 of 180° is 72°. The other angle is 108°.

## Math circle: why vertically opposite angles are equal {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · a short argument, not a new unit</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

We used “vertically opposite angles are equal” as a fact. Here is a short reason, from the straight-line fact you already have. It is an argument, not a new measuring skill.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Each adjacent pair on a straight line adds to 180°. Two angles that are each 180° minus the same neighbor must be equal.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Two lines cross at O. Call one angle a, and the angle next to it (on the straight line) b. What is the angle opposite a, and why?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>a + b = 180°, because they sit on a straight line.</li>
<li>The angle opposite a also sits on a straight line with that same b, so it plus b is 180°.</li>
<li>Two angles that both make 180° with the same b must be equal. The opposite angle is also a.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>If a is 65°, use the argument (not “just because they are opposite”) to find the opposite angle.</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: The neighbor</summary>

The neighbor b is 180° − 65° = 115°.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: The opposite</summary>

The opposite also makes 180° with 115°, so it is 65°.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>The point is the reason, not a new formula.</p>

1. Write the argument in two sentences, using a and b, without a diagram of a particular size.
2. Why does this argument need the straight-line fact first?

</div>

### Practice

1. Give one sentence you want to keep: an angle is a turn, not a length.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. a + b = 180° on a straight line; the angle opposite a also + b = 180°, so it equals a.
2. Without “adjacent angles on a straight line add to 180°,” we would not have a reason that both angles equal 180° − b.

1. Any clear sentence that keeps degrees as a turn, not as centimeters or kilometers.
