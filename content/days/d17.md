## Mental math warm-up: distance around a rectangle {#mental}

<p class="meta">10 minutes · say the answer, then explain how you got it</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

The perimeter of a rectangle is the distance around it: two lengths and two widths, or 2 × (length + width). A 5 cm by 3 cm rectangle has perimeter 2 × (5 + 3) = 16 cm. You can also add 5 + 3 + 5 + 3.

**Perimeter is the distance around.** For a rectangle, P = 2 × (L + W). For a rectilinear L-shape, walk every outer edge; do not add the inner edges that were cut away, and do not skip a step.

**The L-shape in the picture.** The base is 8 cm, the left height is 5 cm, the right height is 3 cm, and the step is 4 cm across. Walking around: 8 + 3 + 4 + 2 + 4 + 5 = 26 cm. (The 2 cm is the little vertical step from height 3 cm up to height 5 cm.) A useful check: the L-shape can be thought of as a large 8 cm by 5 cm rectangle with a 4 cm by 2 cm rectangle cut from a corner — but for *perimeter*, cutting a corner out *adds* the two new cut edges and removes the two old outer edges in a way you must redraw; it is safer today to walk the outline than to invent a shortcut.

**A course design problem (invented, labeled).** A habitat module’s floor is a 6 m by 3 m rectangle. The distance around that floor (a tape along the wall line) is 2 × (6 + 3) = 18 m. Those 6 m and 3 m are classroom numbers, not a NASA specification.

## Math circle: does a bite change the perimeter? {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · a short argument from a sketch</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

When you cut a rectangle from the *corner* of a larger rectangle, the two outer edges you remove have the same lengths as the two new edges you create, so the perimeter stays the same. When you cut a rectangle from the *middle* of a side (a bay, not a corner), you typically add more edge than you remove, and the perimeter grows. The argument is the sketch, not a memorized slogan for every cut.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

A rectangular bite at a corner: perimeter unchanged. A bay cut in the middle of a side: usually a longer walk. Sketch first, then add.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Show, with a 10 by 6 rectangle and a 3 by 3 corner bite, why the perimeter stays 32 cm.</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>The original rectangle has P = 2 × (10+6) = 32 cm.</li>
<li>You remove 3 cm of the top and 3 cm of the right side, and you add two new 3 cm edges along the cut.</li>
<li>3 + 3 removed, 3 + 3 added, so P is still 32 cm.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>If instead you cut a 3 cm by 3 cm square *into* the middle of the 10 cm top side (a bay), does the perimeter stay 32 cm? Sketch and explain.</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: What you remove</summary>

You remove 3 cm of the top side.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: What you add</summary>

You add two 3 cm sides and a 3 cm back of the bay: 9 cm added, 3 cm removed, so P grows by 6 cm to 38 cm.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These need a sketch and a remove-and-add argument.</p>

1. State the corner-bite rule in one sentence, and one case it does *not* cover.
2. A 8 by 5 rectangle, 2 by 2 corner bite: perimeter, with a reason.

</div>

### Practice

1. Why is “cutting always makes the perimeter smaller” false here?
2. Invent a bay cut (labeled invented) on a 6 m by 3 m module and find the new walk.
3. Write a two-sentence argument a classmate could check without you there.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. A rectangular bite from a corner leaves the perimeter unchanged; a bay in the middle of a side is a different cut and usually lengthens the walk.
2. 2 × (8+5) = 26 cm, and the corner bite does not change it, so 26 cm.

1. Because a corner bite can leave the walk the same length, and a bay can make it longer — cutting is not “using up” perimeter.
2. For example, a 2 m wide and 1 m deep bay in a long side: remove 2 m, add 1+2+1=4 m, so P = 18 + 2 = 20 m, invented module. Other labeled bays are fine if the arithmetic matches.
3. A short, standalone remove-and-add argument.
