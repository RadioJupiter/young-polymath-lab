# Unit 1 · Day 1 · How far is the Moon?

**Today's big question:** The Moon looks close enough to touch. How far away is it really, and how do we even write a number that big?

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> work through the blocks in order. In the core lesson, do not skip the concrete step even if the student "already knows" it; seeing why each place is worth ten times the next is the foundation for everything later. Answers go in the math notebook; check them with the key only after the work is done.</p>

## Core lesson: place value to a million {#core}

<p class="meta">45 minutes · concrete → pictorial → abstract</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**The fact.** The Moon is, on average, about **384,400 kilometres** from Earth. Earth itself is about **12,756 km** across. These are real measurements, rounded.

**1. Concrete — place-value discs.** Use discs (or draw them) labelled 1, 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000, 100,000. Ten discs of one kind are traded for one disc of the next kind. This is a **number bond** of ten:

<div class="nbond" aria-hidden="true"><div class="whole">10</div><div class="arms"></div><div class="parts"><span>10 ones</span><span>1 ten</span></div></div>

Build **1,384**: one 1,000-disc, three 100-discs, eight 10-discs, four 1-discs.

**2. Pictorial — the place-value chart.** Put the digits of the Moon's distance in a chart:

| 100,000 | 10,000 | 1,000 | 100 | 10 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 8 | 4 | 4 | 0 | 0 |

Each column is worth **ten times** the column to its right. A number bond can split a number into two parts, for example 384,400 = 300,000 + 84,400.

<div class="nbond" aria-hidden="true"><div class="whole">384,400</div><div class="arms"></div><div class="parts"><span>300,000</span><span>84,400</span></div></div>

**3. Abstract.** 384,400 is 3 hundred-thousands, 8 ten-thousands, 4 thousands and 4 hundreds. Moving every digit one column left multiplies by 10. Ten hundred-thousands make **one million**: 1,000,000. The Sun is about **1,391,400 km** across.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Ten of one disc make one of the next disc. Each place is worth ten times the place to its right. ×10 moves every digit one place left. A number bond splits a number into two parts that add to the whole.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. Chart: 3 thousands, 4 hundreds, 7 tens, 5 ones. 10 × 3,475 = **34,750 km**.
2. 384,400 = 80,000 + **304,400**.

1. Chart: 1 ten-thousand, 2 thousands, 7 hundreds, 5 tens, 6 ones. After ×10: 1 hundred-thousand, 2 ten-thousands, 7 thousands, 5 hundreds, 6 tens = **127,560**. Each digit moved one place left.
2. 384,400 × 10 = **3,844,000 km**.
3. Bond: 1,391,400 = 1,000,000 + **391,400**. 10 × 1,391,400 = **13,914,000 km**.
4. ×10 → **384,400** (digits one place left). ×100 → **3,844,000** (two places left).
5. **484,400** (this is 100,000 more than the Moon's distance).

## Challenge problems {#challenge}

<p class="meta">25 minutes · try every one; being stuck for a while is part of the work</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

A useful heuristic: **try a smaller case**, then use the pattern. To count how many whole numbers have exactly *n* digits, look at the first of them and the last of them, and subtract, remembering to add 1 (the fence-post count).

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

How many whole numbers from *A* to *B* inclusive? *B* − *A* + 1. Two-digit numbers run from 10 to 99, so there are 99 − 10 + 1 = 90.

</div>

### Practice

1. How many whole numbers have exactly six digits?
2. I am a six-digit number whose digits add up to 2. List every number I could be.
3. A number line runs from 0 to 400,000 and is 40 cm long. Where is 384,400 on it?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. 1,000 to 9,999: 9,999 − 1,000 + 1 = **9,000**.

2. **Six numbers:** 200,000; 110,000; 101,000; 100,100; 100,010; 100,001. (The first digit is 2, or the first digit is 1 and exactly one other digit is 1.)
3. Each centimetre stands for 10,000, so 384,400 is at **38.44 cm**: a little less than 38 and a half centimetres from 0.

## Math circle: counting without missing any {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · the math circle tradition: one rich problem, solved and then explained</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

The heuristic today is **make a systematic list** so you cannot miss a case or write a repeat. Fix the first choice, then list every way to finish; then change the first choice and do it again.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

If you use each of *n* different digits once, the number of arrangements is *n* × (*n*−1) × … × 1. A careful list is how you see why.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Using the digits 1 and 2 once each, how many two-digit numbers can you make?</p><ol class='steps'><li>First digit 1: the remaining digit is 2, so 12.</li><li>First digit 2: the remaining digit is 1, so 21.</li><li>Two numbers. That is 2 × 1 = 2.</li></ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>Start the list for digits 1, 2 and 3 with first digit 1. How many numbers begin with 1?</p>

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Remaining digits</summary>

2 and 3 can be arranged as 23 and 32.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: So</summary>

Two numbers begin with 1: 123 and 132. The same will happen when 2 or 3 is first.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.</p>

1. For 1, 2 and 3, how many three-digit numbers are there in all, if you use each digit once?

</div>

### Practice

Using each of the digits 1, 2 and 3 exactly once, how many different three-digit numbers can you make?

Don't guess: make a list *in order*, so that you can be sure you have every one and no repeats. Then predict: with the four digits 1, 2, 3, 4, how many four-digit numbers are there? Check your prediction by a smart method, not by listing them all.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. 3 × 2 × 1 = **6** (123, 132, 213, 231, 312, 321).

Six: 123, 132, 213, 231, 312, 321. Listing in increasing order (all starting with 1, then with 2, then with 3) guarantees none are missed. With four digits: there are 4 choices for the first digit, and for each of them the remaining three digits can be arranged in 6 ways (just found), so 4 × 6 = **24**. The pattern 1, 2, 6, 24 continues by multiplying by the next whole number.

## Notebook: the Earth–Moon model {#notebook}

<p class="meta">60 minutes · draw first, then label, then write · [How the notebook works](../notebook.html) · [Review sheet](../printables/d01-notebook.html)</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Today's scale is **1 cm stands for 1,000 km**. To change a real length in kilometres into a model length in centimetres, divide by 1,000. Earth is 12,756 km across, so the model is 12,756 ÷ 1,000 = 12.756 cm, which we take as about **12.8 cm**. The same rule gives the Moon and the distance between them.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>The Moon is 384,400 km from Earth. What is that in centimetres, and in metres, at today's scale?</p>

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: Divide</summary>

384,400 ÷ 1,000 = 384.4 cm, about 384 cm.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: In metres</summary>

100 cm = 1 m, so 384 cm is almost **4 m**.

</details>

### Practice

<p class="note"><strong>How the guide helps today:</strong> stay for the writing steps. The student draws first, with no writing. Then labels from the word bank. Then says each stem sentence aloud; you confirm it is a complete sentence before it is written. If handwriting is a struggle, write the student's own spoken sentence in light pencil for tracing or copying. Do not invent sentences for the student.</p>

**Build it.** Use the scale **1 cm stands for 1,000 km**. Then:

- Earth (12,756 km across) is a ball about **12.8 cm** across: about the size of a large grapefruit.
- The Moon (3,475 km across) is about **3.5 cm** across: close to a ping-pong ball (4 cm).
- The distance between them (384,400 km) is about **384 cm**: almost **4 metres**.

Find two objects close to these sizes. Hold the "Earth" and ask someone to walk the "Moon" away until it is 3.84 m away. Most people guess the Moon is much closer.

**1. Draw.** Sketch the two objects with the gap between them (you can show a broken or compressed gap, but write the real model distance).

**2. Label**, from the word bank. Point and say each word, then write it on the sketch.

**Word bank:** Earth · Moon · kilometre (km) · centimetre (cm) · metre (m) · model · scale · distance · diameter

**3. Write.** Say each sentence aloud, then write it.

- The Earth in the model is about ________.
- The Moon in the model is about ________.
- The gap between them is about ________.

Then finish this stem in your own words: *What surprised me was ________.*

**4. Numbers.** Write the calculation for each model size (Earth, Moon, distance). Write one question about the Moon that you would like to be able to answer.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. **5 cm**.
2. **10,000 km**.

A good page has a labelled sketch, the three calculations (12,756 km → 12.8 cm; 3,475 km → 3.5 cm; 384,400 km → 384 cm), completed stems, and an honest surprise sentence. The model is accurate to the stated scale; nothing in it is pretend. Keep the question: several will be answered later in the unit.
