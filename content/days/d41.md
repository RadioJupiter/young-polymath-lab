A **tally** is a mark for each thing you count. The first four marks in a group are upright lines. The fifth mark crosses the group, so you can read the count in fives and leftovers.

A **table** is a way to write the same information in rows and columns, with a heading for each column. The table does not add new numbers; it organizes the counts you already have.

The marks and the table above are an **invented classroom meteor watch**: five one-hour watches, not a named meteor shower and not an official rate. The counts are 4, 8, 2, 10, and 6.

Watch 4 has two full groups of five, which is 10. Watch 1 has four marks and no complete group, which is 4. The total of the five invented watches is 4 + 8 + 2 + 10 + 6 = 30.

## Math circle: why tallies group in fives {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · a reason, not a new counting trick</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Grouping tallies in fives is a choice that makes a count easier to read. It does not change the total.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Five-groups let you skip-count. The leftover marks are the ones that do not make a full five. The total stays the same.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Why is a tally of 12 easier to read as two groups of five and two leftovers than as twelve separate marks in a line?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>Two groups of five can be skip-counted: 5, 10.</li>
<li>The two leftover marks make 12.</li>
<li>Twelve separate marks have to be counted one by one, and it is easier to lose your place.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>Does grouping in fives change a count of 12 into a different number? Why or why not?</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: The marks</summary>

Every mark still stands for one thing counted.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: The total</summary>

2 × 5 + 2 = 12, which is the same total.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These check the reason.</p>

1. A tally could, in theory, group in tens instead. Give one reason fives are a handy group for a quick count.
2. Why is a messy scatter of marks a weaker record than grouped tallies?

</div>

### Practice

1. If two people tally the same invented watch and write different totals, what should they do before trusting either total?

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. Ten is larger, so a half-finished group can be a lot of marks to count. Five is small enough to see at a glance, and two fives make ten.
2. A scatter is easy to miscount, and a later reader cannot see the groups.

1. Count the marks again, together if needed, and agree on the record. The total has to match the marks.
