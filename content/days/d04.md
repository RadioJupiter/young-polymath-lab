# Unit 1 · Day 4 · How much farther?

**Today's big question:** Mars is farther from the Sun than Earth, and Jupiter is bigger than Saturn. By how much? Subtraction answers "how much more" questions.

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> the comparison bar model is the key picture today: two bars, one longer, and the question is the difference. Also teach "counting up" (adding on from the smaller number), which is often faster than written subtraction across zeros.</p>

## Core lesson: subtraction and the comparison model {#core}

<p class="meta">45 minutes</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

**1. Concrete.** Build 1,000 with base-ten pieces (one thousand-stack). Take away 375. You must first trade the thousand for ten hundreds, then one hundred for ten tens, then one ten for ten ones. Trading down is the same idea as carrying, run backwards.

**2. Pictorial: the comparison model.** To compare two quantities, draw two bars lined up at the left. The extra piece on the longer bar is the difference.

<div class="barmodel">
<div class="row"><span class="lbl">Earth</span><span class="seg" style="width:38%">same as Mars</span><span class="seg u" style="width:34%">? (the difference)</span></div>
<div class="brace">Earth's whole bar = 12,756 km</div>
<div class="row"><span class="lbl">Mars</span><span class="seg" style="width:38%">6,792 km</span></div>
</div>

**3. Abstract.** 12,756 − 6,792. Ones: 6 − 2 = 4. Tens: 5 − 9 does not work, so trade one hundred for ten tens: 15 − 9 = 6. Hundreds: now 6 − 7 does not work, so trade one thousand: 16 − 7 = 9. Thousands: now 1 − 6 does not work, so trade the ten-thousand: 11 − 6 = 5. Answer: **5,964 km**.

**Check by adding back:** 6,792 + 5,964 = 12,756. ✓ Every subtraction can be checked with an addition.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Trading down is carrying run backwards: one disc is ten of the next smaller disc. Comparison bars: when two amounts are compared, the difference is the extra part of the longer bar. Take-away bars: the whole minus a part leaves the other part.

</div>

## Math circle: odd and even {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · parity, one of the most powerful ideas in the math circle tradition</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Odd + odd = even, odd + even = odd, even + even = even. A sum of an even count of odd numbers is even; a sum of an odd count of odd numbers is odd. For handshakes: each handshake uses **two** hands, so the total number of 'hands used' must be even.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

An odd number of odd addends cannot make an even total. The number of people with an odd number of handshakes must itself be even.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Can three odd numbers add to 10?</p><ol class='steps'><li>Odd + odd = even; even + odd = odd.</li><li>Three odds make an odd total. 10 is even. So no.</li></ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>Can you find four odd numbers that add to 25? Try, then use the rule.</p>

<details>
<summary>Hint 1: How many odds?</summary>

Four is even, so four odds add to an even number.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2: 25 is odd</summary>

An even total cannot equal 25. It is impossible.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These questions use the same skill as the example. Write the answers, then look at the key.</p>

1. What is odd + odd + odd: odd or even?
2. Why must a count of 'hands used' in a room of handshakes be even?

</div>

### Practice

1. Can you find four odd numbers that add up to 25? Try, and then explain why you can or cannot.
2. Five astronauts meet. Can each one shake hands with exactly three of the others? (Hint: count how many "hands used" there would be in total, and remember that every handshake uses exactly two hands.)

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. **Odd**.
2. Each handshake uses exactly two hands.

1. **No.** Two odd numbers always add to an even number (each odd number is an even number plus one, and the two "ones" make two). So four odd numbers make two even sums, which add to an even number. 25 is odd.
2. **No.** Five astronauts each using one hand three times gives 5 × 3 = 15 hand-uses. Every handshake uses exactly two, so the total must be even. 15 is odd, so it is impossible.

## Notebook: the Moon journal begins {#notebook}

<h3>Learn</h3>

The Moon's shape, as we see it, changes through the month because sunlight lights different parts of the Moon as the Moon goes around Earth. ### Practice

<p class="note"><strong>How the guide helps today:</strong> help set up a week's boxes. For the prediction sentence, use *because*: the student says why the Moon might look bigger, smaller or the same in a week. If tonight is cloudy, draw the last Moon that was actually seen and write "cloudy tonight".</p>

Start a **Moon journal**: for the next 30 days, whenever the Moon is visible, draw its shape, note the date and time, and sketch where it is in the sky (above which tree, roof or landmark). If clouds hide it, write "cloudy". The Moon is often visible in the daytime too; look for it.

Today, prepare the journal: a page for each week, with a box for each day. Then draw a careful picture of the Moon as it looks tonight (or the last time you saw it).

*Never look at the Sun, even when looking for the daytime Moon.*

**1. Draw** today's (or the last seen) Moon, large, with any markings you can really see. Add the week of empty boxes for the journal.

**2. Label:** date, time, and the shape. Use the word bank.

**Word bank:** Moon · date · time · sky · cloudy · shape · crescent · half · full · prediction · week

**3. Write.**

- Tonight the Moon looks ________.
- I saw it ________ (where, or "I did not see it").

Then: *In one week the Moon will look ________ because ________.*

**4.** You will check the prediction in a week (Day 8 is on that path if you work each school day, but the journal runs every evening, not only on school days).

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. Date, time, shape, and where it is in the sky.

There is no fixed drawing: this is observation. A good journal has dates and times on every entry and drawings of the shape, not just the words "half" or "full", plus a prediction that uses *because*. By Day 8 the record will show the Moon's shape changing in a regular cycle.
