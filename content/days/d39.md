# Unit 8 · Day 39 · A rule as an input and an output, and the lever

**Today's big question:** What is a rule that takes an input and gives an output, and how can a lever be written as a balance of two products?

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> A rule (a first look at a function) takes an input and gives one output; undo in reverse to find an input. For this course’s first lever model, mass × distance on the left equals mass × distance on the right when it balances. Invented masses and distances stay labeled; do not invent a NASA crane figure. A smaller mass farther from the fulcrum can match a larger mass nearer in, because the products match.</p>

## Core lesson: a rule as an input and an output, and the lever {#core}

<p class="meta">40 minutes · Method: a number machine, then a labeled invented lever whose two sides balance as products</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>


A **rule**, sometimes called a function in later work, takes an **input** and gives one **output**. You can think of it as a machine: a number goes in, the rule is applied, a number comes out.

**A classroom rule.** The rule “double, then add 3,” or n → 2n + 3:

- input 4 → 2 × 4 + 3 = 11
- input 6 → 2 × 6 + 3 = 15

Each input has one output under this rule. To find the input from an output, undo the rule, in reverse order: subtract 3, then halve.

**A lever, as a first model.** A **lever** is a bar that turns on a point, the **fulcrum**. In this course’s first model, a smaller mass farther from the fulcrum can balance a larger mass nearer to it.

All masses and distances in this sitting are **labeled invented** classroom numbers, not a measured machine and not a NASA crane figure.

**The first equation we will use.** For this model, the two sides balance when:

**mass × distance** on the left equals **mass × distance** on the right.

In the drawing, 2 × 6 = 12 and 3 × 4 = 12, so the invented lever is balanced. If one product is smaller, that side would tip up, in this model.

We will not invent a historical measurement, and we will not invent a NASA figure, to “confirm” the products.

## Math circle: why a longer arm can balance a heavier mass {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · a short argument, then a check of the reason</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

Why, in this course’s first lever model, can a smaller mass farther from the fulcrum balance a larger mass nearer to it?

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Each side contributes a product, mass × distance. A smaller mass can make the same product if its distance is larger.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>In the lesson’s invented drawing, 2 at 6 units balances 3 at 4 units. Why is that not a surprise, in this model?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>2 × 6 = 12 and 3 × 4 = 12.</li>
<li>The smaller mass has the longer arm, so the products match.</li>
<li>The numbers are invented and labeled; the reason is the matching products, not a NASA figure.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>What would go wrong if we only compared the two masses, and ignored the distances?</p>
<details>
<summary>Hint 1: The masses alone</summary>

3 is larger than 2, so we might think the 3 must tip that side down.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Hint 2: The model</summary>

The model uses both the mass and how far it sits. Distance is part of the balance, not an extra decoration.

</details>

</div>

<div class="check">

<h3>Check</h3>

<p>These check the reason.</p>

1. A labeled invented lever has 1 at 12 units on the left and 6 on the right. What right distance would balance, and why can 1 balance 6 at all in this model?
2. How is a lever like yesterday’s pan balance, and how is it not the same picture?

</div>

### Practice

1. Why must the units of distance on both sides be the same kind (both in cm, or both in units of the same size)?
2. This argument is not a score or a badge.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. 1 × 12 = 12, so 6 × d = 12 and d = 2 units. The mass 1 has a much longer arm, so its product can still match 6’s product.
2. Both are pictures of two sides that match. On a pan balance, we compared the masses themselves. On the lever, we compare mass × distance, because the arms can be different lengths.

1. The products are like-with-like only when the distances are in the same unit. Mixing cm with a made-up “step” that has not been defined would not be the same job.
2. A note that the circle is a reason to talk through, not a reward.
