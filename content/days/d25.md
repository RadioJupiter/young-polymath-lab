### Unit check

Work alone. Use a sketch when it helps. For Mars, Earth, and Tycho, use the figures taught this unit (NASA, with π and rounding stated). Invented classroom shapes stay labeled invented. Do not use a calculator.

**Part A: angles and polygons**

1. An angle of 47° sits on a straight line with another angle. Find the other angle, and name both (acute or obtuse).
2. Two lines cross. One angle is 110°. Find the other three.
3. A triangle has angles 25° and 90°. Find the third.
4. A quadrilateral has angles 70°, 80°, 95°, and one missing. Find the missing angle.
5. A regular hexagon: what is each interior angle? (Show the sum first.)

**Part B: circles and circumference**

1. A circle has radius 6 cm. Find the diameter.
2. A circle has diameter 10 cm. Using π ≈ 3.14, find the circumference, and label 3.14.
3. A circle has diameter 14 cm. Using π ≈ 22/7, find the circumference, and label 22/7.
4. Mars’s equatorial diameter, as this course used the NASA fact sheet, is about 6,792 km. Using π ≈ 3.14, what circumference does this course write, and what must be labeled?
5. Why is 3.14 not the same thing as π?

**Part C: symmetry, a construction, and a model**

1. How many lines of symmetry does a non-square rectangle have, and where do they run?
2. How many lines of symmetry does a scalene triangle have, and why?
3. List the four steps of the perpendicular-bisector construction, including “same opening.”
4. Tycho is about 85 km across (NASA). If we model the rim as a circle and use π ≈ 3.14, what circumference do we get, and what must the sentence still say?
5. Name one sourced fact from this unit (a NASA figure) and one invented classroom number, and say why the invented number must stay labeled.

Four out of five or better on each part means this level is mastered. Any part below that is reviewed before Level 9. This is a mastery check, not a score, a badge, or a reward.

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. Three headings, ready for the sitting.
2. A note that extra items are not requested; four out of five is the mastery rule, not a score.

**Unit check.**

**Part A.**
1. 133°, obtuse; 47° is acute.
2. The opposite angle is 110°. The two adjacent angles are 70° each. Check: 110 + 70 + 110 + 70 = 360.
3. 65° (180 − 115).
4. 115°, because 70 + 80 + 95 = 245, and 360 − 245 = 115.
5. Sum (6 − 2) × 180° = 720°. Each interior angle is 720° ÷ 6 = 120°.

**Part B.**
1. 12 cm.
2. About 31.4 cm; 3.14 labeled as an approximation of π.
3. 44 cm; 22/7 labeled as an approximation of π.
4. About 21,300 km (3.14 × 6,792 = 21,326.88, rounded to the nearest hundred). Both 3.14 and the rounding must be labeled.
5. 3.14 is a labeled approximation (a rounded stand-in). π is the exact C/d of a circle, and it is not a terminating decimal.

**Part C.**
1. Two: the line halfway between the long sides, and the line halfway between the short sides (not the diagonals).
2. Zero. No two sides are equal, so no fold matches the triangle onto itself.
3. Open the compass to more than half of the segment; draw an arc from one end; with the same opening, draw an arc from the other end so the arcs cross twice; the line through the two crossings is the perpendicular bisector.
4. About 267 km (3.14 × 85 = 266.9), and the sentence must say this is a circle model, not a surveyed walk around a perfect rim.
5. Sourced: for example, Mars about 6,792 km (NASA); Earth about 12,756 km (NASA); Tycho about 85 km (NASA LRO pages); or 2π times 6,378.137 km, about 40,075 km, as a closer equatorial circumference. Invented: for example, a 8 m dish, a 4 cm tube, or a hexagonal pad. Invented numbers must be labeled so they are not mistaken for NASA measurements.

## Math circle: a last short argument {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · after the check, or later if the check took the full sitting</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

A last argument for the level: *why we label π*. A circumference computed with 3.14 is a number we may use, but the number is only as honest as the label. The same is true of a rounding (about 21,300 km) and of a crater model (about 267 km). The argument is not that approximations are bad — they are how we compute at this level — but that they must be named as what they are.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Label the approximation of π. Label a rounding. Label a crater model. That is part of the mathematics, not an extra flourish.

</div>
