# Unit 6 · Day 26 · Tenths, hundredths, and place value

**Today's big question:** What is a tenth and a hundredth, and how do we read each digit in a decimal?

<p class="note"><strong>For the guide:</strong> A strip cut into ten equal parts, and a hundred-square, come first. Each place is ten times smaller than the place on its left. 0.4 is 4 tenths; 0.04 is 4 hundredths. 3.14 is still a labeled approximation of π. Invented classroom lengths stay labeled. Do not invent a decimal NASA size.</p>

**A tenth** is one of ten equal parts of a whole. We write one tenth as **0.1**, or as the fraction **1/10**. The pictured bar is one whole, split into ten equal parts; three parts are shaded, so the shaded length is **0.3**, or 3/10.

**A hundredth** is one of a hundred equal parts of a whole. We write one hundredth as **0.01**, or as **1/100**. The pictured grid is one whole, split into a ten-by-ten square. Twenty-five small squares are shaded, so the shaded part is **0.25**, which is 25/100, and that is the same amount as **1/4**.

**Place value.** Each place to the right of the ones is ten times smaller than the place before it. After the ones comes a **decimal point**, then the **tenths**, then the **hundredths**. In **2.45**, the 2 is 2 ones, the 4 is 4 tenths, and the 5 is 5 hundredths: 2 + 4/10 + 5/100. We can also say 2.45 is 245 hundredths, or 2 ones and 45 hundredths.

**Reading the digits by their places.** 0.4 is 4 tenths (not 4 hundredths). 0.04 is 4 hundredths. 0.40 is 4 tenths and 0 hundredths, which is the same amount as 0.4 — Day 27 will say more about that. For now, put each digit in its column; do not count “how many numbers after the point” as a substitute for place value.

**A number you already use.** The approximation **π ≈ 3.14** is 3 ones, 1 tenth, and 4 hundredths. That is why 3.14 × 10 was 31.4 in Level 8: each digit moves one place to the left. 3.14 is still a labeled approximation of π, not π itself.

**The next column.** The place after hundredths is **thousandths** (0.001, or 1/1,000). We name it so the pattern is clear. Today’s checks stay with tenths and hundredths.

**Classroom lengths stay labeled.** A rod “1.25 m long” in a practice item is an invented classroom length, not a NASA figure.

## Math circle: why the next place is ten times smaller {#circle}

<p class="meta">20 minutes · a short reason, then a check</p>

<div class="learn" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Learn</h3>

We write decimals the way we write whole numbers: each step to the right is a group ten times smaller. That is why ten tenths make a one, and ten hundredths make a tenth, just as ten ones make a ten. The argument is about grouping, not about a new kind of number.

</div>

<div class="remember" data-listen="en-US">

<p class="kicker">Remember</p>

Each place is ten times the place on its right. The decimal point marks the ones place; it is not a mark that “makes the number small” by itself.

</div>

<div class="watch" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Watch me</h3>

<p>Why is 0.1 ten times 0.01?</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li>0.01 is one hundredth.</li>
<li>Ten hundredths make one tenth.</li>
<li>So 10 × 0.01 = 0.1, the same grouping that makes 10 ones into 1 ten.</li>
</ol>

</div>

<div class="together" data-listen="en-US">

<h3>Together</h3>

<p>If we named the next place after hundredths, why would ten of those make one hundredth?</p>

### Answer key

**Check.**
1. One whole; one tenth.
2. The point only marks where the ones place ends. The size of each digit comes from its place (tenths, hundredths), not from the point “halving” anything.

1. 0.3 is 3 tenths; 0.03 is 3 hundredths; ten hundredths make one tenth, so ten times 3 hundredths is 3 tenths.
2. 0.25 is 25/100, and 25/100 = 1/4. The two digits are 2 tenths and 5 hundredths, not a rule that “two digits means a quarter.”
