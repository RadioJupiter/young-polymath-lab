# Method

This page explains how the course teaches a strong young mathematician, and why. The short version: **solid foundations, taught with understanding, at the pace of mastery, with hard problems every day, all inside a world of real science.**

## 1. Mastery and acceleration, with depth

The student moves on when a level is mastered, not when a calendar says so. The research supports this: a large review of a century of studies found that academic acceleration has clear positive effects on the achievement of high-ability students (Steenbergen-Hu, Makel & Olszewski-Kubilius, 2016), and the Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth, which has followed mathematically talented young people for decades, argues that appropriately challenging opportunities, acceleration among them, help such talent develop (Lubinski & Benbow, 2006). Acceleration alone can turn into racing through procedures, so every lesson pairs pace with depth: challenge problems and the math circle are never skipped.

## 2. Concrete → pictorial → abstract (CPA)

Every new idea is first built with objects (base-ten pieces, counters), then drawn (place value charts, arrays, area models, bar models), then written in symbols. This sequence follows Jerome Bruner's account of three modes of representation (enactive, iconic, symbolic) and is the backbone of Singapore's primary mathematics curriculum, which adopted the CPA approach in its 1981 curriculum (Kaur, 2019). Strong students may move through the concrete step quickly, but it is never skipped: the picture is what makes a method *understood*, and it is what lets you rebuild a method you have forgotten.

## 3. Bar models for problem solving

The Singapore **model method**, introduced in 1983 by the team led by Dr Kho Tek Hong, draws the quantities in a word problem as bars: part-whole, comparison and change models (Kaur, 2019). The bar model turns a story into a structure, and later becomes algebra: a bar labelled "?" is an unknown x.

## 4. Problem solving as the main event

Following the approach of Beast Academy, the elementary curriculum from Art of Problem Solving, problems that cannot be solved by applying a just-taught procedure are a daily part of the work, not an extra. Being stuck, trying another way, and finally seeing it is the experience that builds a mathematician. Hints are given in stages and only after real effort.

## 5. The Russian math circle

Each day ends its mathematics with one rich problem from the math circle tradition, as collected in *Mathematical Circles (Russian Experience)* by Fomin, Genkin and Itenberg (AMS, 1996): logic puzzles, systematic counting, parity, invariants, the pigeonhole principle, weighings, divisibility. These topics are rarely in school curricula, but they are the heart of mathematical reasoning: every circle problem ends with *explaining why*, which is the beginning of proof.

## 6. Mental math and retrieval

Ten minutes of mental calculation opens each day. Fluent facts and strategies (making tens, doubling and halving, breaking apart) free attention for harder thinking, and recalling earlier material from memory strengthens it (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Strategies are always explained aloud, never just raced.

## 7. Space and physics, with real numbers

Every unit is set in astronomy or physics: distances in the Solar System, the speed of light, orbits, pendulums, the Moon's phases. All numbers come from authoritative sources (NASA's planetary fact sheets, the international definitions of the metre and the astronomical unit) and are rounded honestly, with the rounding stated. Experiments use household materials.

## 8. The notebook

The hour of notebook and project time, modelled on Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks, is where mathematics meets the world: observing, measuring, drawing from life and inventing. It also builds the habit of recording work carefully enough that someone else could check it.

## 9. What is deliberately left out

- **No points, badges, streaks or leaderboards.** Motivation comes from interesting problems and from visibly being able to do more than before.
- **No babyish framing.** Lessons speak directly and respectfully, and the guide notes speak to the adult.
- **No age or grade labels.** The level map is the only measure of where the student is.

## The guide's role

The guide (a parent or tutor) reads the guide notes, runs the concrete step, gives hints in stages, checks answers with the keys, and decides when a unit check shows mastery. The guide does not need to be a mathematician; the keys explain every solution.
